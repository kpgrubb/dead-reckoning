<Briefing title="Bias is a direction; variability is a spread" topics={["3.4"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Imagine repeating the whole survey a thousand times under the same method. You get a thousand estimates. That cloud of estimates has a **centre** and a **width**, and the two are damaged by completely different things.

**Sampling variability** is the width. It comes from the luck of the draw and nothing else, it is what a standard error measures, and it shrinks as $1/\sqrt{n}$. It is the quantifiable price of not asking everybody, and a sound method produces it in full measure.

**Bias** is the distance from the centre of the cloud to the true value. It comes from the method, it is the same for every repetition, and **it does not shrink with $n$**. A biased design run at $n = 1000$ is the same wrong answer, held more firmly.

The named mechanisms, each with a direction you can usually predict before you collect anything:

- **Undercoverage.** The frame misses part of the population. Direction: toward whatever the reachable part believes. A dock-office frame over-weights whoever docks.
- **Convenience sampling.** Units chosen because they were easy. A special case of undercoverage with no frame at all and no computable error.
- **Voluntary response.** The units decide whether to be in the sample. Direction: toward strong opinion, and usually toward grievance, because a person with a complaint will spend fifteen minutes on a form and a person without one will not.
- **Nonresponse.** You selected them properly and could not get an answer. Direction: toward whatever the *responders* believe. That is a real quantity, and an estimable one, and the rest of this briefing is about how far it can sit from the quantity you were sent to measure.
- **Response bias.** The answer differs from the truth because of how it was asked: **wording** that names the conclusion before the question ("*Do you believe pirates … are responsible?*"), an **interviewer** the respondent wants to please or fears, a question about something the respondent is unwilling to admit. Direction: toward the socially safe answer, or toward whatever the question suggested.

Random selection is a cure for exactly one disease. It makes the selection unbiased *within the frame*. It does nothing about a bad frame, nothing about who refuses to answer, and nothing whatever about a leading question, because it does not change a word of it.

**Two parameters.** Nonresponse is the mechanism worth slowing down over, because it changes which question a survey is *capable* of answering, and that is a harder thing than getting an answer slightly wrong.

Take a frame of $N$ units. Some of them would answer the survey if the design reached them and some would not, and that split is fixed before anybody draws anything. So the frame carries two proportions, not one. Write $p$ for the proportion of all $N$ units with the characteristic, and $p_R$ for the proportion among the $N_R$ units that would answer. A probability design selects at random from the frame, but only the responders come back, so what returns is a random sample **of the responders**. It estimates $p_R$ without bias, at a precision you can compute and report. It estimates $p$ with a bias you can neither compute nor report.

Two things set the size of that bias, and $n$ is not one of them: how large the silent part of the frame is, and how far it differs. If the units that do not answer hold the same view as the ones that do, the two parameters coincide and the distinction costs nothing. If the reason for the silence is related to the answer, the gap is real, it is the same size at every sample size, and the returned data cannot reveal it, because the quantity it depends on is the one nobody collected. A high response rate caps the damage. A low one leaves the estimate free to sit almost anywhere.

A survey in that position is still worth running. It reports both halves: the estimate, named as an estimate of $p_R$ with the responding units identified, and the nonresponse rate printed beside it. Both numbers are findings. The claim about $p$ is what the report declines to make.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\text{bias} = E[\hat{p}] - p, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}" terms={{ 'E[\\hat{p}]': 'the centre of the estimates over repeated sampling', 'p': 'the parameter the claim is about', 'n': 'the sample size' }} label="Centre and width are different quantities" />

<Formula tex="\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\text{bias}^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat{p})} \;\xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{}\; |\text{bias}|" terms={{ '\\text{RMSE}': 'typical total distance from the truth', '\\operatorname{Var}': 'the squared spread, which goes to zero' }} label="Why a bigger biased sample does not help" />

Now the two parameters. Split the frame by who would answer, and the proportion across the whole frame is a weighted average of the two halves:

<Formula tex="p = \frac{N_R}{N}\,p_R + \frac{N - N_R}{N}\,p_M" terms={{ 'N': 'units in the frame', 'N_R': 'units in the frame that would give a usable answer', 'p': 'the proportion across the whole frame: the parameter most reports claim', 'p_R': 'the proportion among the responders: what a probability design estimates without bias', 'p_M': 'the proportion among those who would not answer: never observed' }} label="One frame, two parameters" />

Subtract. A probability sample of the frame returns only responders, so its estimates centre on $p_R$, and its bias as an estimate of $p$ is a rate times a difference:

<Formula tex="E[\hat{p}] - p = \left(1 - \frac{N_R}{N}\right)\left(p_R - p_M\right)" terms={{ 'E[\\hat{p}]': 'the centre of the estimates over repeated probability samples: it sits on the responders’ proportion', 'p_R - p_M': 'how far the silent part of the frame differs, which the returns never contain' }} label="Nonresponse bias: the rate times the difference" />

Each factor offers a way out, and a survey controls only the first of them. Drive the nonresponse rate down and the bias comes down with it whatever the second factor is, which is why response rates are chased so hard. The other way out is to show that the two groups agree, and that requires $p_M$, the one quantity the nonresponders declined to supply. So when the silence has a reason connected to the answer, treat the second factor as large, say why you think so, and report the rate.

<Formula tex="\text{nonresponse rate} = \frac{\text{contacted} - \text{answered}}{\text{contacted}}" terms={{ '\\text{contacted}': 'units the design selected and reached for', '\\text{answered}': 'units that gave a usable response' }} label="The number that must be printed beside the estimate" />
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
**Case one: a bad design run larger.** Your own ship, 2176, and a case where the truth came out afterwards. *Asgard* carried {fmtInt(asgardCrew.ratings)} ratings. The question was whether a rating had been through a sink alarm in the commission, and because the maintenance log recorded every alarm by compartment, the answer was knowable later: **{fmtInt(asgardCrew.sinkAlarms)} of {fmtInt(asgardCrew.ratings)}**, a true share of {fmt(asgardTruth, 3)}.

The first attempt was a notice in the mess. {fmtInt(asgardCrew.volunteers)} ratings filled it in; {fmtInt(asgardCrew.volunteerYes)} said yes, a share of {fmt(asgardVolunteerP, 3)}. Bias: {fmt(asgardVolunteerP, 3)} − {fmt(asgardTruth, 3)} = **{fmt(asgardBias, 3)}**, and the direction was predictable before a single form came back, because a rating who had been sealed in a hot compartment wanted to say so and a rating who had not had nothing to write.

The second attempt was an SRS of {fmtInt(asgardCrew.srsN)} off the watch bill, twice, a week apart: {fmtInt(asgardCrew.srsYesA)} yes and then {fmtInt(asgardCrew.srsYesB)} yes, or {fmt(asgardSrsA, 3)} and {fmt(asgardSrsB, 3)}, on either side of {fmt(asgardTruth, 3)}. The two estimates differ by {fmt(Math.abs(asgardSrsA - asgardSrsB), 3)} and neither is wrong: the standard deviation of an estimate at this size is {fmt(asgardSe, 3)}, so that is exactly the wobble the design promises.

Now the arithmetic that decides everything. Quadruple the mess notice to {fmtInt(4 * asgardCrew.volunteers)} volunteers and its spread halves, to about {fmt(asgardSeBig, 3)}. Its bias sits where it was, at {fmt(asgardBias, 3)}. Typical total distance from the truth: {fmt(asgardRmse, 3)}, which is {fmtPct(asgardSeBig / asgardRmse, 0)} spread and the rest pure direction. Against an SRS of {fmtInt(asgardCrew.srsN)} whose typical distance is the {fmt(asgardSe, 3)} above and whose centre is the truth. **The small honest sample beats the large dishonest one by a factor of {fmt(asgardRmse / asgardSe, 1)}, and the gap widens every time you spend more money on the wrong design.**

**Case two: a sound design, and a part of the frame that will not answer.** Later in the same commission, the ship's office ran a survey on a question it could check afterwards against the yard's defect register: had a rating ever filed a defect report and then been asked to withdraw it? The frame was the same {fmtInt(asgardCrew.ratings)} ratings. {fmtInt(asgardDefect.silent)} of them were in the engineering division, whose chief had told his people to put nothing in writing about the cellar while the refit was under review, and none of them did.

The design itself was sound: names drawn at random off the ship's book, every name chased twice, neutral wording. Every engineering name drawn returned a blank. So what the office actually held was a probability sample of the {fmtInt(asgardAnswerable)} ratings outside engineering, and among those the register later showed {fmtInt(asgardDefect.answeredYes)} with a withdrawn report, giving $p_R$ = {fmt(asgardDefectResponder, 3)}. The register also gave the side the survey could not reach: {fmtInt(asgardDefect.silentYes)} of the {fmtInt(asgardDefect.silent)} engineering ratings, $p_M$ = {fmt(asgardDefectSilent, 3)}. Over the whole company, $p$ = {fmt(asgardDefectFrame, 3)}.

Put those into the identity. The silent share of the frame is {fmt(asgardSilentShare, 3)}, the two groups differ by {fmt(asgardDefectResponder - asgardDefectSilent, 3)}, and the product is {fmt(asgardSilentShare, 3)} × ({fmt(asgardDefectResponder, 3)} − {fmt(asgardDefectSilent, 3)}) = **{fmt(asgardNrBias, 3)}**, which is exactly {fmt(asgardDefectResponder, 3)} − {fmt(asgardDefectFrame, 3)}.

That number is the whole lesson, so read it twice. The estimate {fmt(asgardDefectResponder, 3)} is unbiased for one parameter and {fmt(Math.abs(asgardNrBias), 3)} away from the other. Chasing every answerable name on the ship's book instead of {fmtInt(asgardCrew.srsN)} would have driven the standard error to zero and moved {fmt(asgardNrBias, 3)} not at all, because the second factor in the product is a property of the engineering division and has nothing to do with how many forms came back. Nor could the office have found the gap in its own returns: every quantity in the product's second factor was a quantity engineering declined to supply. What the office could have written, and did not, was which {fmtInt(asgardAnswerable)} ratings the figure was about, with {fmtPct(asgardSilentShare, 0)} of the book recorded as not answering.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Bias fixed by $n$.* The commonest error in the subject. More data narrows the cloud; it does not move it. If you cannot say which direction a mechanism pushes, you cannot correct for it either, and reporting it is all that is left.

*Variability called bias.* Two honest samples of the same population give different numbers. That is the design working, and a reviewer who calls a difference of one standard error "a discrepancy" is reading noise as signal. Bias is a statement about the **centre of the repeated-sampling distribution**, which no single sample displays.

*Quoting the responders' figure as the frame's.* An estimate from a survey with material nonresponse is an estimate of $p_R$. Attaching it to $p$ is the one error in this topic that the data can never catch, and also the cheapest to avoid: name the units the figure is about in the same sentence as the figure, and print the nonresponse rate next to it.

*Random selection as a cure for response bias.* Choosing respondents by digits does not unask a leading question. The Authority's Q4, "{CERES_Q4}", names the conclusion and the culprits before it asks anything, and it would do so just as efficiently to a perfect simple random sample of the corridor.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

  concept="Over repeated sampling an estimate has a centre and a width: bias is the distance from that centre to the parameter and comes from the method (undercoverage, convenience, voluntary response, nonresponse, or response bias from wording or interviewer), while sampling variability is the width and comes only from the draw, shrinking as one over the square root of n. Random selection removes selection bias inside the frame and nothing else: it does not repair a frame, it does not make a refusal answer, and it does not unask a leading question. So a frame whose silent part is silent for a reason connected to the answer carries two parameters, and a probability design estimates the responders' proportion without bias while leaving the frame's own proportion out of reach; the gap between them is the nonresponse rate times the difference between the two groups, and no sample size touches it."
