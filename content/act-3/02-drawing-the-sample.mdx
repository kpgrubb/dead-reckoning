<Briefing title="Four ways to draw a list, and what each one buys" topics={["3.3"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Every design here is a **probability sample**: each unit's chance of selection is known and nobody's chance is zero. That is what makes an estimate's error calculable. Everything else is a trade between *link time* and *what the estimate is protected against*.

**Simple random sample (SRS).** Every set of $n$ units from the frame is equally likely. Not merely "everyone equally likely": that is a weaker property, and stratified and systematic samples have it too. The SRS is the reference design: no structure, no assumptions, and no guarantees about who turns up.

**Stratified.** Split the frame into **strata**, groups you expect to be *internally alike and different from each other*, then draw an independent random sample inside every stratum. You are guaranteed every stratum's share. Because the between-stratum differences are removed from the sampling error, the estimate is **tighter** than an SRS of the same size, and the more the strata differ, the more you gain.

**Cluster.** Split the frame into **clusters**, groups each of which should be a *small copy of the whole*, internally varied, then draw whole clusters at random and take every unit in them. It is cheap when units cluster physically. It is **wider** than an SRS of the same size whenever members of a cluster resemble each other, because then each cluster is closer to one observation than to seven.

The two look identical on a diagram and are opposites in intent:

| | groups differ | inside a group | you sample |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stratified** | a lot (that is why you split) | alike | some of *every* group |
| **Cluster** | ideally not at all | varied (a miniature of the frame) | *all* of some groups |

**Systematic.** Order the frame by something unrelated to the response, pick a random start in $1..k$, then take every $k$th unit. On this schedule, every {fmtInt(SYSTEMATIC_STEP)}th registry hull number is about {fmtInt(systematicN)} masters, and there are only {fmtInt(SYSTEMATIC_STEP)} possible samples in the whole design, one per start. Cheap and tidy, and safe exactly as long as the ordering carries no periodicity that lines up with $k$.

The one rule under all four: **random means a device, not a judgement.** Digits, a generator, a logged seed. A person choosing "at random" from a list chooses hulls whose names they like, and the selection is then unrepeatable and unanalysable.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\text{SRS of size } n \text{ from } N: \quad \Pr(\text{any particular set}) = \binom{N}{n}^{-1}" terms={{ 'N': 'units in the frame', 'n': 'units drawn' }} label="What makes a sample simple random" />

<Formula tex="\text{stratify when } \sigma^2_{\text{between}} \text{ is large}; \qquad \text{cluster when } \sigma^2_{\text{within}} \text{ is large}" terms={{ '\\sigma^2_{\\text{between}}': 'variation between the groups', '\\sigma^2_{\\text{within}}': 'variation inside each group' }} label="Which grouping you want" />

<Formula tex="\text{expected count from a stratum in an SRS} = n\cdot\frac{N_h}{N}" terms={{ 'N_h': 'size of stratum h in the frame', 'n': 'sample size' }} label="What an SRS gives you on average, and only on average" />
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Take an acceptance sample at Adlinda, the kind you signed off for years. A lot of {fmtInt(yard.lot)} coil fittings arrives from three production lines: line A {fmtInt(yard.lineA)}, line B {fmtInt(yard.lineB)}, line C {fmtInt(yard.lineC)}. Line C is the new line, three months old, and the one the yard is actually worried about.

Draw an **SRS of {fmtInt(yard.sample)}**. Every fitting has the same chance, {fmtPct(yard.sample / yard.lot, 0)}, and the expected number from line C is {fmtInt(yard.sample)} × {fmtInt(yard.lineC)} ÷ {fmtInt(yard.lot)} = **{fmt(yardExpectedC, 1)}**. Expected is not guaranteed. The chance that an SRS of {fmtInt(yard.sample)} contains *no* line-C fitting at all is {fmtPct(yardMissC, 1)}, or about one acceptance sample in {fmt(yardOddsC, 0)}. An inspection regime that answers the yard's actual question in {fmtPct(1 - yardMissC, 0)} of samples and is blind to the new line in the rest has a hole in it that opens about once a month.

**Stratify** instead: {fmtInt(yard.perStratum)} drawn at random from each line, {fmtInt(3 * yard.perStratum)} in all. Line C's share of the sample is now {fmtPct(yard.perStratum / (3 * yard.perStratum), 0)} instead of an expected {fmtPct(yard.lineC / yard.lot, 0)}, it is certain rather than expected, and you can report a defect rate *for line C* with {fmtInt(yard.perStratum)} fittings behind it. The price is that the three lines are three separate draws and three separate tallies, and the estimate for the lot as a whole must be reweighted back to {fmtInt(yard.lineA)} : {fmtInt(yard.lineB)} : {fmtInt(yard.lineC)} or it is not a lot estimate at all.

**Cluster** instead: the lot arrives in {fmtInt(yard.crates)} crates of {fmtInt(yard.crateSize)}. Open {fmtInt(yard.cratesDrawn)} crates chosen by digits and inspect every fitting in them: {fmtInt(yardCrateN)} fittings, for three crate-openings instead of {fmtInt(yard.sample)} rummages through the pallet. Cheapest by a distance. And each crate was packed off one line in one shift, so {fmtInt(yardCrateN)} fittings from {fmtInt(yard.cratesDrawn)} crates carry nearer {fmtInt(yard.cratesDrawn)} crates' worth of information about the lot. That is the trade, in a warehouse and on a shipping lane: **clusters are cheap because their members are alike, and their members being alike is exactly the cost.**
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"Stratified means I split them into groups."* Splitting is not the design. A stratified sample draws **at random inside every stratum** and includes all of them; taking whichever groups are convenient, or leaving one out because it is hard to reach, is not stratification and has no error you can compute. Sampling only some of the strata is a cluster sample with the clusters chosen badly.

*"Random means haphazard."* Pointing at a list, taking every hull whose master you have spoken to before, or "just grabbing a spread" is not randomization: the selection probabilities are unknown, so no estimate from it has a standard error. Randomization is a physical procedure: digits read off a table two at a time, out-of-range and repeats discarded, the seed written into the log. Its whole value is that somebody else can repeat it and audit you.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
