<Briefing title="Variation is data" topics={["1.1"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Measure the same quantity six times with the same procedure and you will get six different values. That is not a fault in the procedure. Variation is what measurement does, and a set of measurements therefore raises two separate questions.

**Centre: where do the values sit?** One number that stands in for all of them. The usual choice is the **mean**: add the values, divide by how many there are. It is the balance point of the set.

**Spread: how far apart are they?** The simplest measure is the **range**: largest value minus smallest value, in the same units as the data. It is a real summary of spread and a bad answer to a question about centre. The two questions are not interchangeable, and neither are their answers.

One further distinction, because the exam turns on it. Behind any set of measurements sits a **population**: every value the procedure could produce if you ran it forever. The values you actually hold are a **sample** from it. A number that describes the population is a **parameter**. A number computed from the sample is a **statistic**. You compute statistics. You almost never observe a parameter; you estimate it.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i" terms={{ '\\bar{x}': 'sample mean — the summary of centre', 'n': 'number of observations in the sample', 'x_i': 'the i-th observation (a fix error, km)' }} label="Sample mean" />

**Range** $= \max(x_i) - \min(x_i)$. A summary of spread, in the same units as the data.

A **parameter** describes a population; a **statistic** describes a sample. Notation keeps them apart: $\mu$ is a population mean, $\bar{x}$ a sample mean.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Six along-track fix errors from the yard's acceptance trial, in km, positive meaning ahead of the beacon: {yardAcceptanceFixes.map((v) => fmt(v, 0)).join(', ')}.

*Centre.* The sum is {fmt(sum(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)} and $n = 6$, so $\bar{x}$ = **{fmt(mean(yardAcceptanceFixes), 1)} km**. On average these six fixes fell {Math.abs(mean(yardAcceptanceFixes)) < 1 ? 'on the beacon' : mean(yardAcceptanceFixes) > 0 ? 'ahead of the beacon' : 'behind the beacon'} by that much.

*Spread.* The largest is {fmt(max(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)} and the smallest {fmt(min(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)}, so the range is {fmt(max(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)} − ({fmt(min(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)}) = **{fmt(range(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)} km**.

Report both, and label both. The mean answers *where*; the range answers *how far apart*.

*Parameter or statistic?* {fmt(mean(yardAcceptanceFixes), 1)} km is a statistic: it came from six observations. The parameter it estimates is the long-run mean error of the dead-reckoning procedure on this geometry. That number exists and will never be logged.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**Disagreement is not malfunction.** Six measurements from a sound procedure always disagree. How much they disagree is information about the procedure; it is not evidence against it.

**A range is not a position.** "The error is between −120 and +180" describes spread. It says nothing about where the values sit, so it cannot answer a question about centre. If both are wanted, give two numbers and say which is which.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
