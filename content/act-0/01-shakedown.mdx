On the engineering display the cellar reads four percent and warming. Sixty tonnes of lithium, cold and solid, waiting to be asked to hold the ship's heat.

You walk her. It is the first time you have done it as her captain rather than the Service's build liaison, and you notice that you still put your hand on the same frames. The sink vault, where the Chief has taped a card over the yard's brass plate with her own numbers on it. The radiator wings, folded into the hull like something asleep. The wardroom, where sixteen people who have known each other for nine days are not yet talking the way a crew talks. Ferrier has been aboard nine of those days. She has read every page of the design book and she has not yet said what she thinks of it.

The blister at the mast head is a two-person pressurised station with a manual lidar head and a view. Oyelaran is in it with the hatch open, calibrating the Eyes against Jupiter's limb, which is the only thing out here bright enough and known enough to calibrate against. She does not look up when you put your head through. "Four telescopes, four offsets, all inside spec, sir. I'll have them inside half of spec by the first run." You believe her.

The orders are one page. Three cold runs in Jovian space against Callisto's own telescopes, which know exactly where to look and will therefore find her sooner than anyone else could. The yard wants numbers. The Board wants a ship it can announce. Nobody has yet run the sink with sixteen people breathing in her.

At MET 2/00:00 the torch goes out and the reactor scrams and the wings fold, and *Nightjar* is a cold object on a ballistic path, navigating by dead reckoning. Ferrier runs the first fix against Callisto's beacon at 01:00 and five more at ten-minute intervals: where the ship thinks it is, against where the beacon says it is. Six numbers. They do not agree with each other. Some are ahead of truth, some behind, and the difference between the best and the worst is the width of a small moon.

The yard tug *Adlinda Nine*, standing off at two thousand kilometres as range safety, wants the ship's position error for its log. One number.

<Briefing title="Variation is data" topics={["1.1"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Run the same procedure twice and you get two numbers. That is not a fault in the procedure; it is what measurement is. The question a commander asks of six fixes is not *which one is right* but *what do the six say together* — where they sit, and how far they scatter. Those are two different questions, and they get two different numbers.

The first is a **summary of centre**: one value that stands in for all six. The mean is the balance point. The second is a **summary of spread**. The range — largest minus smallest — is the crudest one, and it is honest as far as it goes; but it answers *how far apart*, never *where*.

Behind the six fixes is the **population**: every fix the dead-reckoning procedure could produce on this geometry, if it were run forever. The six you hold are a **sample** of it. A number that describes the population (the procedure's long-run mean error) is a **parameter**; a number computed from the six (their mean) is a **statistic**. The tug master gets a statistic. Nobody will ever hold the parameter.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i" terms={{ '\\bar{x}': 'sample mean — the summary of centre', 'n': 'number of observations in the sample', 'x_i': 'the i-th observation (a fix error, km)' }} label="Sample mean" />

**Range** $= \max(x_i) - \min(x_i)$ — a summary of spread, in the same units. A **parameter** describes a population; a **statistic** describes a sample.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Adlinda's own acceptance trial, two weeks before the crew came aboard, logged six along-track fixes against the same beacon (km, positive = ahead of truth): {yardAcceptanceFixes.map((v) => fmt(v, 0)).join(', ')}.

Their sum is {fmt(sum(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)}; divided by six, the mean is **{fmt(mean(yardAcceptanceFixes), 1)} km** — the yard's dead reckoning ran, on average, {Math.abs(mean(yardAcceptanceFixes)) < 1 ? 'on the beacon' : mean(yardAcceptanceFixes) > 0 ? 'ahead of the beacon' : 'behind the beacon'} by that much. The largest fix was {fmt(max(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)} and the smallest {fmt(min(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)}, so the range is **{fmt(range(yardAcceptanceFixes), 0)} km**. The yard's report gave both numbers, in that order, and labelled them.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
Two errors, both common on tug channels. *"The fixes disagree, so the navigation is faulty."* No: six fixes from a sound procedure always disagree; the size of the disagreement is information about the procedure, not evidence against it. *"The error is between minus a hundred and plus two hundred."* That is a range dressed as a position. It tells the tug master how far the fixes scatter and nothing about where the ship thinks it is.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

Ferrier notes the class of error: a cold ship on dead reckoning knows its own position to a few hundred kilometres — and so does anyone who has its published profile.">

Solberg sends it as two lines, because you told him to: the mean, {fmt(mean(loggedFixRun.fixes), 1)} km along track, and the spread, {fmt(range(loggedFixRun.fixes), 0)} km across the six. The tug master acknowledges in four words. He was not interested in the second line. He will be, one day, on some other ship.

Ferrier writes the two numbers on the plot's margin in her own hand, the way navigators still do, and adds a third thing that is not a number. *Class of error: plus or minus three hundred.* Not this run's — the procedure's. Six fixes from a cold ship, with the beacon known, scatter across roughly three hundred kilometres; call it the {FIX_ENVELOPE_KM}-kilometre class, she says, and stop pretending the third decimal exists.

"That is what a ship knows about itself when it is cold, Captain," she says. "A ship on a published profile, with the Authority plotting it, is known to about the same. Anyone with the profile could put a rock through it."

The lithium ticks as it warms. Oyelaran reports the Eyes calibrated to half of spec, as promised. You have one number for where the ship is and one for how sure, and you have Ferrier's third thing, which is not about this ship at all.
