<Briefing title="Carrying the test out, and the two tables that must not be tested" topics={["8.6", "8.7"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
**The statistic.** One term per cell, summed over the whole table:

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$$

The numerator is the miss. The denominator is the yardstick, because a miss of four matters far more in a cell expecting six than in one expecting sixty. Each term is called that cell's **contribution**, and the contributions are worth printing: they say *where* the departure is, which is the half of the finding a reader can act on.

**The P-value is always the upper tail.** Every departure from the expected counts, in either direction, makes $(O-E)^2$ bigger and pushes the statistic to the right. A two-sided question about a table is answered by a one-tailed area, and there is nothing to double.

**A χ² statistic has no size of its own.** It answers to its degrees of freedom. The same value is decisive on 1 df and unremarkable on 20, so the df comes before the tail, every time.

**The conclusion, in four parts.**

1. The decision, with $P$ and $\alpha$ written next to each other.
2. Evidence, not fact: *convincing evidence of an association*, never *the variables are associated* flat, and never *the variables are independent* when the test fails to reject.
3. In context: both variables named, and the population named.
4. Where it came from: the largest contribution, and whether that cell came in above or below what the margins predicted. Contributions are squared and all positive, so the direction is read off $O - E$ and nowhere else.

**Two things a small P-value does not say.** It does not say the departure is *large*: on a big table a trivial departure will clear any $\alpha$, and the size of the effect is in the counts and the percentages, not in the statistic. And it does not say one variable *caused* the other. A two-way table from an observational file establishes association; a cause needs assignment, which no file of records has.

**Choosing the procedure.** Categorical questions have seven endings, and the first five are decided by how the data were collected, before a single count is read.

| how the data were collected | what is on each unit | procedure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| one random sample | one two-category outcome, against a published proportion | one-proportion z |
| two independent random samples | one two-category outcome in each | two-proportion z |
| one random sample | one categorical variable, against a claimed distribution | χ² goodness of fit |
| two or more separately drawn samples | one categorical variable in each | χ² homogeneity |
| one random sample | two categorical variables on every unit | χ² independence |

Then the two a calculator will not protect you from.

**The expected counts fail.** Below 5, the χ² distribution is the wrong reference curve for the statistic, and the area under it is not a P-value. The statistic still computes and the screen still prints a number. What replaces it is a **simulation of the null**: shuffle the outcome labels at random among all the units, thousands of times, recompute the quantity of interest each time, and count how often the shuffled data reach what was actually observed. That count over the number of repetitions is the P-value, and it rests on no curve at all.

$$P_{\text{sim}} = \frac{\text{repetitions at least as extreme as the observed}}{\text{number of repetitions}}$$

A simulated P-value of zero is reported as a **bound**, not as a zero. Zero out of ten thousand means *less than one in ten thousand*, because ten thousand repetitions cannot resolve anything finer; run a hundred thousand and the bound moves, not the conclusion.

**The file is a census.** When every unit the question is about is already in the file, there is no sampling variability for a P-value to describe and no wider population to generalise to. The difference between the groups is a fact about that population, reported as counts and row percentages, with the difference named in words and no test attached.
</Briefing.Intuition>

Each time, record the gap between crew D's broken-seal rate and everyone else's. The observed gap is {fmt(dockTable[0][0] / (dockTable[0][0] + dockTable[0][1]), 3)} − {fmt(dockTable[1][0] / (dockTable[1][0] + dockTable[1][1]), 3)} = {fmt(dockObserved, 3)}.

<Briefing.Caution>
*Running χ² on a table whose expected counts fail.* The statistic computes, the calculator prints a P-value, and the number means nothing, because the curve it was read off is not the null distribution of that statistic. On the exam the correct response is to **say so**: name the smallest expected count, state that the condition is not met, and describe the simulation or the collapse of categories that would answer the question instead.

*Reading significance as size.* A P-value answers one question: could ordinary variation have produced a departure this large. On a table of ten thousand rows a departure of no practical interest will return a P-value in the fourth decimal. The size of the effect lives in the counts, the row percentages and the ratio of observed to expected. Report both.

*Association read as cause.* A significant χ² on records says the two columns move together in that population. The three explanations it cannot separate are that one drives the other, that something else drives both, and that the file was assembled in a way that put them together. Say *association*, and put the limit in the same paragraph as the finding.

*Concluding independence from a large P-value.* Failing to reject leaves the data consistent with independence. It is not evidence for it, and on a small table it may say nothing except that the file is small.

*A simulated P-value of zero written as zero.* Zero of ten thousand is a statement about the resolution of the simulation. Write it as *less than one in ten thousand*, and say how many repetitions produced the bound.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
