<Briefing title="Goodness of fit: testing a whole distribution at once" topics={["8.1", "8.2"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Every procedure so far has tested one number: a proportion, a mean, a difference of two. A **goodness-of-fit test** tests a whole *distribution*. One categorical variable, $k$ classes, and a claim from outside the data about what share of the population belongs in each.

**The hypotheses.** $H_0$ names the entire claimed distribution at once:

$$H_0:\ p_1 = \pi_1,\quad p_2 = \pi_2,\ \dots,\ p_k = \pi_k$$

The $\pi_i$ come from somewhere other than the sample: a published mix, a physical model, a manufacturer's specification, an equal-shares assumption. $H_a$ is the logical negation, **at least one** $p_i$ differs from its claimed value. One class off its share already falsifies the claim, and that phrasing has a consequence at the far end of the procedure: a rejection says the distribution is wrong somewhere without saying where.

Both hypotheses are about population proportions $p_i$. The sample's own shares are known exactly and there is nothing left to hypothesise about them.

**Expected counts.** If the claim holds, the class sizes a sample of $n$ would produce on average are

$$E_i = n \pi_i$$

Those are means over repeated samples rather than predictions about one sample, and they keep their decimals. An expected count of 6.51 makes no claim that two thirds of an individual exists; it says that class averages 6.51 members in samples this size. Round it to 7 and you have altered both the numerator and the denominator of a term you are about to add up.

**Conditions.** Three, and each is a sentence with a number in it.

- **Random.** The data are a random sample from the population of interest, or the population entire. Say where they came from.
- **10%.** Sampling without replacement needs $n \le 0.10N$. Write $10n$ against $N$.
- **Large Counts.** Every **expected** count is at least 5. The check is on the $E$ column, computed from the claim. It is never on the observed counts, which may be anything at all including zero: a class the claim expected plenty of and the file barely contains is the departure the test exists to find, and discarding the table on that basis throws away the finding to protect the procedure. Where an expected count genuinely falls below 5, the repair is to merge classes that belong together, decided before anyone looks at the data.

**The statistic.** One term per class:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

The numerator is the gap, squared, so a class that came in low pushes the statistic the same way as one that came in high. The denominator puts each gap on the scale of what that class expected: a miss of 8 against an expectation of 6 is a different event from a miss of 8 against an expectation of 600.

Both $O$ and $E$ are **counts**. Multiply the pair by a constant $c$ and each term becomes $c(O-E)^2/E$, so the whole statistic scales by $c$. Percentages inflate it by $100/n$; proportions shrink it by $n$; and the arithmetic gives no sign that anything has gone wrong.

**Degrees of freedom.** $df = k - 1$. Fix the total and $k-1$ of the cells are free to move, while the last is whatever is left over. The sample size appears nowhere in it. A statistic quoted without its df cannot be read, because $\chi^2$ has no absolute size: under $H_0$ its mean *is* its df, so a value of 12 is decisive on 3 df and ordinary on 12.

**Where the statistic came from.** Each term is that class's **contribution**, and the contributions sum to the statistic, so they divide it between the classes. Reading down that column says which class the departure lives in. It is a follow-up rather than part of the test, it carries no P-value, and it rarely points at the tallest bar: contribution measures distance from an expectation, and the biggest class is usually the one the claim expected to be biggest.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Caution>
**Percentages in the statistic.** The commonest fatal error here, and it leaves no trace. $\chi^2$ runs on counts. Feed it the two percentage columns and every term picks up a factor of $100/n$, so the audit above returns {fmt(auditFromPercent.statistic, 2)} in place of {fmt(auditGof.statistic, 2)}. Feed it proportions and the statistic comes out $n$ times too small. Both screens print a P-value and neither complains.

**Rounding the expected counts.** $E_i = n\pi_i$ is a mean over repeated samples and is entitled to its decimals. On a table of thirty-odd observations the rounding is not cosmetic.

**Checking ≥ 5 on the observed column.** Large Counts is a statement about the expected counts. The observed counts are the evidence, and a class that came in far below what the claim expected is the signal.

**"Reject means every class is off."** $H_a$ says at least one. Which one is a separate reading, taken off the contributions.

**A statistic without its df.** $\chi^2$ has no absolute scale. Its mean under $H_0$ equals its degrees of freedom, so the same number is decisive on a short table and unremarkable on a long one. Quote the two together or quote neither.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
