<Briefing title="Reading a chi-square, and what a rejection is entitled to say" topics={["8.3"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
**The P-value is an upper-tail area, and there is only one tail.**

$$P = P\left(\chi^2_{k-1} \ge \chi^2_{\text{observed}}\right)$$

Every term of the statistic is a squared gap over an expectation, so a class that came in low and a class that came in high push it the same way. A perfect fit gives 0; any departure at all, in any class, in either direction, moves the statistic up. "A worse fit than this one" therefore means "a larger $\chi^2$", with no exceptions, and both directions of departure already sit in the same tail. Doubling that area is wrong, and the goodness-of-fit screen offers no tail selector because there is no choice to make.

**The statistic has no absolute size.** Under $H_0$ the mean of a $\chi^2$ is its degrees of freedom, because each of the $k$ terms contributes about 1 on average when the claim is true. So a table of twelve classes is *expected* to produce a statistic near 11 with nothing whatever wrong with it, while the same 11 on a four-class table is well outside what chance supplies. A statistic reported without its df is unreadable rather than incomplete.

**The conclusion.** The same five parts as every significance test, and the exam marks all five.

> Because $p$ = [value] [is / is not] less than $\alpha$ = [level], we [reject / fail to reject] $H_0$. There [is / is not] convincing evidence that the distribution of [variable] among [population] differs from [the claimed distribution].

*Reject* and *fail to reject*, never *accept*. The alternative in a goodness-of-fit test is always "differs from the claim", so the direction clause names the claim rather than a direction of travel.

**What a rejection does not say.** $H_a$ is *at least one* proportion differs. A small P-value therefore condemns the claimed distribution as a whole and says nothing about any individual class. A conclusion asserting that every class is off its share claims far more than the test established, and it is usually false: most tables reject on the strength of one or two classes while the rest sit close to what the claim expected.

**Finding where the departure is.** The statistic is a sum of $k$ non-negative terms, so it divides between the classes by construction, and the largest **contribution** locates the departure:

$$\text{contribution}_i = \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}, \qquad \sum_i \text{contribution}_i = \chi^2$$

Three things to keep straight about that reading:

- It is a **follow-up**, reported after the test and labelled as such. It has no P-value of its own. The $\alpha$ was spent on the whole table at $k-1$ degrees of freedom, and picking the largest of $k$ terms after seeing them and testing it again is a different procedure with a different error rate.
- The largest contribution is **rarely the tallest bar**. Contribution measures distance from an expectation, scaled by that expectation, and the biggest class is usually the one the claim expected to be biggest.
- Say the **direction**: whether that class came in above or below what the claim expected. The squaring destroyed the sign, so it has to be read back off the $O - E$ column.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex={`P = P\\left(\\chi^2_{df} \\ge \\chi^2_{\\text{obs}}\\right), \\qquad df = k-1, \\qquad \\text{reject } H_0 \\text{ when } P < \\alpha`} terms={{ '\\chi^2_{\\text{obs}}': 'the statistic computed from the table', df: 'degrees of freedom, k − 1 for goodness of fit', '\\alpha': 'the significance level, fixed before the data are seen' }} label="P-value for a chi-square test: the upper tail, and only the upper tail" />

<Formula tex={`\\text{contribution}_i = \\frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i}, \\qquad \\sum_{i=1}^{k}\\text{contribution}_i = \\chi^2`} terms={{ 'O_i - E_i': 'the gap, whose sign the squaring destroys and the O − E column preserves' }} label="Where the statistic came from" />
</Briefing.Definition>

*The statistic and its df.*

<Formula tex={`\\chi^2 = ${asgardGof.contributions.map((c) => fmt(c, 3)).join(' + ')} = ${fmt(asgardGof.statistic, 3)}, \\qquad df = ${fmtInt(asgardRegistries.length)} - 1 = ${fmtInt(asgardGof.df)}`} label="Asgard, 2176" />

*The P-value.* The area above {fmt(asgardGof.statistic, 3)} under the chi-square curve on {fmtInt(asgardGof.df)} degrees of freedom is **{fmtP(asgardGof.pValue)}**. On a TI-84 that is `χ²cdf(` with lower {fmt(asgardGof.statistic, 3)}, upper `1E99` and df {fmtInt(asgardGof.df)}. The 5% critical value on {fmtInt(asgardGof.df)} df is {fmt(chi2.isf(0.05, asgardGof.df), 3)}, and the statistic is above it, which agrees.

*The conclusion.* {asgardConclusion.exemplar}

*The level matters and was chosen first.* At $\alpha$ = 0.01 the same table gives {fmtP(asgardGof.pValue)} against 0.01, and the report fails to reject. The level belongs at the top of the page, fixed before any of this arithmetic was run.

*Where the departure is.* {asgardRegistries[asgardDriver]} contributes {fmt(asgardGof.contributions[asgardDriver], 3)} of the {fmt(asgardGof.statistic, 3)}, or {fmtPct(asgardGof.contributions[asgardDriver] / asgardGof.statistic, 0)} of the statistic, with {fmtInt(asgardObserved[asgardDriver])} boarded against {fmt(asgardGof.expected[asgardDriver], 2)} expected: **below** what the schedule predicts. The tallest bar in the file belongs to {asgardRegistries[asgardTallest]} at {fmtInt(asgardObserved[asgardTallest])} boardings, and it contributes {fmt(asgardGof.contributions[asgardTallest], 3)}, because the schedule expected {fmt(asgardGof.expected[asgardTallest], 2)} of them and very nearly got it.

*What that follow-up is worth.* It locates the departure and it is not a test. The report that says "{asgardRegistries[asgardDriver]} registries were significantly under-represented, p = {fmtP(asgardGof.pValue)}" has attached the whole table's P-value to one cell, which is the commonest way a correct analysis becomes a false claim.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**Doubling the tail for a two-sided alternative.** A $z$ or $t$ statistic keeps the direction of the departure in its sign, so a two-sided alternative needs both ends of the curve. Squaring the gaps throws that sign away before the sum, which is exactly what puts every kind of departure into the upper tail. Chi-square is one-tailed whatever the alternative is called.

**Reading the lower tail as a P-value.** The area *below* the statistic grows as the fit gets worse, so using it would make the worst tables look the most convincing. It has a legitimate use of its own: a $\chi^2$ far below its df says the counts are suspiciously close to the claim, which is how audit offices find files that were written rather than collected.

**"We reject, so every class differs."** $H_a$ says at least one. In the *Asgard* table, {asgardRegistries.map((r, i) => (i === asgardDriver ? null : `${r} contributes ${fmt(asgardGof.contributions[i], 2)}`)).filter(Boolean).join(', ')}, and the test establishes nothing whatever against any of them individually.

**A large statistic without its df.** $\chi^2$ = 20 has a P-value of {fmtP(chi2.sf(20, 1))} on 1 degree of freedom and {fmtP(chi2.sf(20, 20))} on 20. Neither the size of the number nor the word *large* carries any information on its own.

**Giving the biggest contributor a P-value.** The level was spent on the whole table. Naming the class that carries the statistic is a description of where the statistic came from, reported as such, and a formal test of that one class needs its own data.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
