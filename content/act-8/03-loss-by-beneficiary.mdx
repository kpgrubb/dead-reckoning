<Briefing title="Two-way tables: expected counts, two designs, and the degrees of freedom" topics={["8.4", "8.5"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A **two-way table** cross-classifies a set of units by two categorical variables. The counts inside it are the data; the **margins**, the row totals and column totals, are what the null hypothesis is built out of.

**Expected counts.** Under the null hypothesis, the column variable is distributed the same way in every row. So take each column's overall share of the grand total and apply it to each row total:

$$E_{ij} = \frac{(\text{row } i \text{ total}) \times (\text{column } j \text{ total})}{n}$$

Expected counts are averages, not counts of anything, and they are not rounded to whole units. Their only job is to be the denominator and the yardstick in $(O - E)^2/E$.

Two properties follow immediately, and both are worth using as checks. Every row of the expected table totals the same as the observed row, and every column totals the same as the observed column. An expected table whose margins do not reproduce the observed margins has an arithmetic error in it.

**Two tests, one piece of arithmetic.** The statistic, the degrees of freedom and the P-value are computed identically. What separates them is **how the data were collected**, and that decides the hypotheses and the population the conclusion is about.

- **Test of independence.** *One* sample is drawn, and each unit in it carries *two* categorical variables that were recorded on it. Only the grand total was fixed in advance; both margins are results. H₀: the two variables are independent in the population the sample came from. Hₐ: they are associated.
- **Test of homogeneity.** *Several* samples are drawn separately, one from each population or treatment group, and *one* categorical variable is recorded in each. The row totals were fixed by whoever drew the samples, before any outcome existed. H₀: the distribution of that variable is the same in every population. Hₐ: the distributions are not all the same.

The question that settles it is always the same: *which totals were decided by a person before any outcome was recorded?* A single file sorted afterwards by one of its own columns is one sample, however many rows the finished table has.

**Conditions**, in the order they are checked:

1. **Random.** The data come from a random sample, or from a randomized experiment. Settled by the sentence describing how the units were obtained, and by nothing downstream.
2. **Independent.** Sampling without replacement needs $10n \leq N$, group by group for homogeneity.
3. **Large counts.** Every **expected** count is at least 5. Observed counts may be anything, including zero. This is the condition that decides whether the χ² curve is the right reference distribution at all, and it can only be checked after the expected counts are computed.

**Degrees of freedom** count how many cells are free once both margins are fixed:

$$df = (r - 1)(c - 1)$$

Fill an $r \times c$ table in from the top left. Every cell in the first $r-1$ rows and first $c-1$ columns is yours to choose. The last cell of each row is then forced by that row's total, and the whole last row is forced by the column totals. The count does not depend on $n$: a $2 \times 3$ table carries 2 degrees of freedom with forty observations in it or forty thousand.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex={`E_{ij} = \\frac{R_i C_j}{n}, \\qquad \\sum_j E_{ij} = R_i, \\qquad \\sum_i E_{ij} = C_j`} terms={{ 'E_{ij}': 'expected count in row i, column j, under H₀', 'R_i': 'row i total', 'C_j': 'column j total', n: 'grand total' }} label="Expected counts, and the margins they must reproduce" />

<Formula tex={`df = (r - 1)(c - 1)`} terms={{ r: 'rows in the table', c: 'columns in the table' }} label="Degrees of freedom of a two-way table" />

<Formula tex={`\\chi^2 = \\sum_{\\text{all cells}} \\frac{(O - E)^2}{E}`} terms={{ O: 'the count recorded in a cell', E: 'the count the margins predict for it' }} label="The statistic both tests compute" />
</Briefing.Definition>

<Formula tex={`df = (${fmtInt(patrolWatches.length)} - 1)(${fmtInt(patrolOutcomes.length)} - 1) = ${fmtInt(patrolTest.df)}`} label="Free cells once both margins are fixed" />

Not {fmtInt(patrolWatches.length * patrolOutcomes.length - 1)}, which is the goodness-of-fit rule for a single row of categories, and not {fmtInt(patrolWatches.length * patrolOutcomes.length)}, which is the number of cells.

Everything above happens before a statistic exists. The setup is where a two-way analysis is won or lost, because the arithmetic afterwards will run happily on a table that was framed wrongly and will print a number that answers nobody's question.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*The two tests swapped.* A single file sorted into two rows by one of its own columns is **one** sample, and its test is independence. Fixed row totals, set before any outcome was recorded, mean **several** samples and a test of homogeneity. The statistic and the P-value come out identical either way, so nothing in the output will ever catch the error. What goes wrong is the sentence at the top and the sentence at the bottom: hypotheses about the wrong thing, and a conclusion generalised to a population nobody sampled.

*$df = rc - 1$.* That rule belongs to goodness of fit, where the categories sit in a single row and only one margin is estimated. A two-way table pays for both margins, and the free cells are the product $(r-1)(c-1)$. A $3 \times 4$ table has 6 degrees of freedom and not 11. If a calculator prints a df that does not match your table's shape, the matrix was typed in the wrong shape.

*Expected counts that break the margins.* Each expected count is the product of **that cell's own** row total and column total, divided by $n$. Dividing a row total evenly among the columns, or rounding every expected count to the nearest whole unit, gives a table whose margins no longer match the observed ones. Add the expected rows up before going any further.

*Checking the observed counts against five.* The large-counts condition is about the **expected** counts. An observed zero breaks nothing; it is often the most informative cell in the table.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
