<Briefing title="Paired data: one sample of differences" topics={["7.4", "7.5"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Two columns of numbers are sometimes two samples and sometimes one. The design decides, and it decides before any data exists.

A design is **paired** when each observation in the first column is matched to exactly one observation in the second by something real: the same unit measured twice, the same subject before and after a treatment, the left eye and the right eye, two hulls deliberately matched on age and route. The matching rule is set in advance. If you can point to a row and say *this is one thing, measured twice*, the rows are the units.

When the rows are the units, there is only one sample in the file: the **differences**. Subtract within each row and what remains is $n$ numbers, one per pair, and every procedure from a one-sample $t$ applies to them unchanged. The parameter is $\mu_d$, the mean difference in the population of pairs. The degrees of freedom are $n - 1$, where $n$ counts **pairs**, not measurements.

Why this is worth doing rather than merely correct: each unit carries a level of its own that appears in both of its measurements. A navigation marker with a fast clock is fast in both columns. A hull that files a hair slow against the nominal plot is slow in both columns. That per-unit level inflates the spread of each column, and a two-sample standard error is built out of exactly those spreads. Subtracting cancels it. The standard error of $\bar{d}$ is built from the spread of the differences, which no longer contains it, and it can be a small fraction of the two-sample one on the same numbers.

The hypotheses are about $\mu_d$ and they are written that way: $H_0: \mu_d = 0$ against $H_a: \mu_d > 0$, $< 0$ or $\neq 0$. Decide the order of subtraction first, write it into the hypotheses, and keep it there.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="d_i = (\text{second measurement})_i - (\text{first measurement})_i \qquad \bar{d} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i" terms={{ 'd_i': 'the difference for pair i — one number per unit', 'n': 'the number of PAIRS, not the number of measurements' }} label="Collapse the file to one column" />

<Formula tex="SE(\bar{d}) = \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad t = \frac{\bar{d} - \mu_0}{s_d/\sqrt{n}} \qquad df = n - 1" terms={{ 's_d': 'the standard deviation of the differences', '\\mu_0': 'the hypothesised mean difference, almost always 0' }} label="Paired t-test: the one-sample t procedure, applied to the differences" />

<Formula tex="\bar{d} \pm t^\star \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}}" terms={{ 't^\\star': 'the critical value at the chosen level on n − 1 degrees of freedom' }} label="Paired t-interval for the mean difference" />

**Conditions.** Random: the pairs were randomly sampled, or the treatment was randomly assigned within pairs. Independent: the *pairs* are independent of one another, and $10n$ is no more than the population of pairs. Normal: the sampling distribution of $\bar{d}$ is approximately Normal, justified by $n \geq 30$ or by a graph of **the differences** showing no strong skewness and no outliers.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The Ceres Authority audited the clock drift of {fmtInt(beaconN)} Lane navigation markers before and after the 2183 timing load. Drift in milliseconds per day, marker by marker, positive meaning the marker's clock runs fast.

Before: {beaconBefore.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

After: {beaconAfter.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

Those are the same {fmtInt(beaconN)} markers in both lines, in the same order, so the file has {fmtInt(beaconN)} rows and not {fmtInt(2 * beaconN)}.

*The differences* (after minus before), in ms/day: {beaconDiff.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

$\bar{d}$ = **{fmt(beaconPaired.estimate, 3)} ms/day**, $s_d$ = {fmt(sd(beaconDiff), 3)}, and with $n$ = {fmtInt(beaconN)} the standard error is {fmt(sd(beaconDiff), 3)} ÷ √{fmtInt(beaconN)} = **{fmt(beaconPaired.se, 4)}**. Against $\mu_0 = 0$ that is $t$ = **{fmt(beaconPaired.statistic, 2)}** on $df$ = {fmtInt(beaconPaired.df)}, one-sided $P$ = **{fmtP(beaconPaired.pValue)}**. The {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} interval for $\mu_d$ runs from {fmt(beaconCi.ci[0], 3)} to {fmt(beaconCi.ci[1], 3)} ms/day, and every value in it is negative.

Now the same fourteen numbers thrown into two buckets. The before column has mean {fmt(mean(beaconBefore), 2)} and standard deviation {fmt(sd(beaconBefore), 2)}; the after column has mean {fmt(mean(beaconAfter), 2)} and standard deviation {fmt(sd(beaconAfter), 2)}. The estimate is unchanged at {fmt(beaconTwo.estimate, 3)}, because a mean of differences and a difference of means are the same arithmetic. The standard error is not: $\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}$ = **{fmt(beaconTwo.se, 4)}**, nearly {fmt(beaconTwo.se / beaconPaired.se, 0)} times the paired one. That gives $t$ = {fmt(beaconTwo.statistic, 2)} on {fmt(beaconTwo.df, 1)} degrees of freedom and $P$ = {fmtP(beaconTwo.pValue)}.

One reading finds a shift of {fmt(Math.abs(beaconPaired.estimate), 2)} ms/day that no timing office would dispute. The other finds nothing at all. The markers differ enormously from one another, from {fmt(Math.min(...beaconBefore), 1)} to {fmt(Math.max(...beaconBefore), 1)} ms/day before the load, and the two-sample reading spends all of its precision on that variation. The paired reading never sees it.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**A two-sample procedure on paired data is the wrong procedure, and no software will warn you.** It runs, it prints, and it answers a different question: whether two independent populations have the same mean, when the file contains one population measured twice. It is almost always less sensitive, sometimes dramatically so, and on a file like the markers above it reports nothing where there is something.

**Normality is checked on the differences.** Each raw column carries every unit's own level, so either one may look skewed or strung out and neither has any bearing on the procedure. Draw the differences. That is the sample.

**Degrees of freedom count pairs.** With {fmtInt(beaconN)} markers measured twice, $df$ = {fmtInt(beaconN - 1)}, never {fmtInt(2 * beaconN - 2)}.

**Pairing has to come from the design.** Sorting two independent samples and lining them up by rank produces a column of differences and a completely invented standard error. When there is no rule matching one observation to one other, the samples are independent and the two-sample procedure is the right one.

**The order of subtraction is a decision.** After minus before, or before minus after: pick one, state it, and make the hypotheses and the conclusion agree with it. The interval and the sign of $t$ both flip if you change your mind halfway.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
