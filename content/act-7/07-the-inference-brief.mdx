<Briefing title="Choosing the procedure, and writing it up" topics={["7.10"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
By the end of Unit 7 there are five inference procedures in play, each with an interval form and a test form. Nothing about choosing between them depends on the numbers. The choice is settled by three facts about how the data were collected and one fact about what is being asked, in that order.

**1. What was recorded on each unit?** A category, or a number. A yes/no outcome gives a proportion and the large-counts conditions; a measurement on a scale gives a mean, an $s$ in the denominator, and a $t$ distribution.

**2. How many groups of units?** One group, read against a fixed published figure, or two groups compared with each other.

**3. If two columns: are they the same units?** This is the question that decides paired against independent, and it is answered from the collection rule rather than from the spreadsheet. If a row is one unit measured twice, the file holds one sample of differences and $df = n - 1$ pairs. If the two columns hold different units matched by nothing, the samples are independent, the standard error adds two variance contributions, and the degrees of freedom are Welch's.

**4. Magnitude or decision?** An interval answers *how large, and how precisely*. A test answers *is there evidence, at a level fixed in advance*. They run on the same estimate and the same standard error.

| recorded on each unit | groups | procedure | interval | test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a category | one | one-proportion z | margin on p̂ | p̂ against p₀ |
| a category | two independent | two-proportion z | margin on p̂₁ − p̂₂ | p̂₁ − p̂₂ against 0 |
| a number | one | one-sample t | x̄ ± t*·s/√n | x̄ against μ₀ |
| a number, twice on each unit | the pairs are the units | paired t | d̄ ± t*·s_d/√n | d̄ against 0 |
| a number | two independent | two-sample t | (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± t*·SE | x̄₁ − x̄₂ against 0 |

**The four-step write-up.** Once the procedure is chosen, the answer has a fixed shape, and a reader checking the work reads it in order.

- **State.** The parameter in words and in symbols, and the hypotheses about it, or the quantity the interval estimates. Written before any statistic exists.
- **Plan.** The procedure by name, and each condition with the evidence that carries it: how the units were obtained, the $10n$ arithmetic against the population, and what the graph shows.
- **Do.** The standard error, the statistic, the degrees of freedom, and the p-value or the endpoints. Full precision carried to here, rounded once.
- **Conclude.** The decision with $p$ and $\alpha$ beside each other, or the interval read in context, and then the limit: what the design does not let the finding claim.

**When the Normal condition is thin.** Below thirty observations the $t$ procedure needs the sample to look plausibly Normal, and a graph of fifteen or twenty points settles that question weakly at best. A **bootstrap** offers a second reading. Treat the sample as a stand-in for the population, draw $n$ values back out of it with replacement, compute the mean, and repeat a few thousand times. The middle $C$ of those resampled means, read off directly, is the **percentile interval**.

$$\left(q_{(1-C)/2},\ q_{(1+C)/2}\right) \text{ of the resampled } \bar{x}^{*}$$

The bootstrap drops the Normal assumption and inherits every other one. It still needs the observations to be independent, and it still needs the sample to resemble the population, because every resample is drawn from the sample and from nowhere else. Where the two intervals agree, the honest sentence is that the conclusion does not depend on the assumption they differ in. Where a sample is biased, both are precisely centred on the wrong number.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="SE_{\text{one}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad SE_{\text{paired}} = \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n_{\text{pairs}}}} \qquad SE_{\text{two}} = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}" terms={{ 'SE_{\\text{one}}': 'one group of measurements; df = n − 1', 'SE_{\\text{paired}}': 'one column of differences; df = pairs − 1', 'SE_{\\text{two}}': "two independent groups; df from Welch's rule or min(n₁ − 1, n₂ − 1)" }} label="The three standard errors of Unit 7, and where each one comes from" />

<Formula tex="\hat{p} \pm z^\star \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \qquad (\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z^\star \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}" terms={{ 'z^\\star': 'the proportion procedures use z, because the standard error is built from p̂ rather than from a separate s', '\\hat{p}': 'the sample proportion, with the Large Counts condition in place of Normality' }} label="The two proportion procedures, for completeness" />

**The percentile bootstrap.** Resample $n$ values from the sample with replacement, $B$ times; compute the statistic on each resample; take the $(1-C)/2$ and $(1+C)/2$ quantiles of those $B$ values. Raising $B$ shrinks the simulation noise in the endpoints and buys no information about the population. The width is set by the $n$ observations that were collected.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The Adrastea tender's quarterly return, which asks four questions and answers them with four different screens.

| the question | what was recorded, and on what | procedure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How common are amended departure plots at this berth? | for each of a random sample of departures: amended, or not | one-proportion z-interval |
| Do the day and night watches differ in amendment rate? | the same yes/no outcome, in two independent samples of departures | two-proportion z-test |
| Does the reclaimer bay's mean run-up time exceed the shop standard? | run-up seconds on each of a random sample of pumps, against a published figure | one-sample t-test |
| Did the bearing change alter run-up time? | run-up seconds on each pump before the change and again after it | paired t-test on the differences |

Nothing in that column came from a number. Each row was settled by reading the middle column and asking what a row of the file is.

**The bootstrap on the third question.** The bay logged run-up time, in seconds, for **{fmtInt(bayRunUp.length)}** pumps drawn at random from the quarter's rebuilds: {bayRunUp.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

$\bar{x}$ = {fmt(mean(bayRunUp), 3)} s, $s$ = {fmt(sd(bayRunUp), 3)} s, $n$ = {fmtInt(bayRunUp.length)}. The Normal condition is the awkward one: skewness is {fmt(skewness(bayRunUp), 2)}, the {fmtInt(bayRunUp.length)} values run from {fmt(Math.min(...bayRunUp), 1)} to {fmt(Math.max(...bayRunUp), 1)}, and the $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ rule flags {fmtInt(bayStrays.length)} of them. On {fmtInt(bayRunUp.length)} observations a graph like that is a weak argument in either direction.

*The t-interval.* At {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} on {fmtInt(bayT.df)} degrees of freedom, $t^\star$ = {fmt(bayT.criticalValue, 3)} and the margin is {fmt(bayT.marginOfError, 3)} s:

<Formula tex={`(${fmt(bayT.ci[0], 3)},\\ ${fmt(bayT.ci[1], 3)})\\ \\text{s}`} label="One-sample t-interval for the mean run-up time" />

*The bootstrap interval.* {fmtInt(bayBoot.reps)} resamples of {fmtInt(bayRunUp.length)} values drawn with replacement, the mean of each, and the middle {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} read off:

<Formula tex={`(${fmt(bayBoot.ci[0], 3)},\\ ${fmt(bayBoot.ci[1], 3)})\\ \\text{s}`} label="Percentile bootstrap interval for the same mean" />

Widths {fmt(bayT.ci[1] - bayT.ci[0], 3)} and {fmt(bayBoot.ci[1] - bayBoot.ci[0], 3)} seconds. The bootstrap standard error, {fmt(bayBoot.se, 4)}, sits beside the $s/\sqrt{n}$ of {fmt(sd(bayRunUp) / Math.sqrt(bayRunUp.length), 4)}.

*What that buys.* The two intervals were built on different assumptions about shape and they land in the same place, so the report quotes the $t$-interval, adds one line saying a percentile bootstrap on {fmtInt(bayBoot.reps)} resamples gave {fmt(bayBoot.ci[0], 2)} to {fmt(bayBoot.ci[1], 2)}, and moves on. Had they disagreed, the report would owe the reader an explanation rather than a choice.

*What it does not buy.* If the quarter's rebuilds were logged only when a fitter remembered to, the sample is unlike the population and both intervals are wrong by the same amount. Resampling redistributes the {fmtInt(bayRunUp.length)} values in hand. It collects nothing.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**Choosing by the number of columns.** Two columns on a screen are two columns. They are two independent samples when a unit in the first has no partner in the second, and one sample of differences when each row is one unit measured twice. The spreadsheet looks identical either way, and the two procedures can return opposite answers on the same twenty-four numbers.

**Skipping the conditions because the software ran.** No package and no calculator screen checks a condition. Every screen in the TESTS menu accepts whatever is in the lists and prints a p-value, including for procedures the data cannot support. Random, the 10% arithmetic and the graph are the operator's, every time, and the conditions block is where an examiner and a hearing both start.

**Naming a procedure without naming the parameter.** "A two-sample t-test" is half a plan. The other half is $\mu_1 - \mu_2$, named in words, with the order of subtraction fixed and the population attached.

**Treating a bootstrap as extra data.** Four thousand resamples of eleven observations contain eleven observations. What the replications buy is stability in the percentile endpoints; the width comes from $n$, and a biased sample resampled four thousand times is a biased sample four thousand times.

**Picking the form after seeing the result.** An interval that happens to exclude zero is not a test at a level nobody stated, and a p-value that happens to be small is not a magnitude. Write down which one the question asked for before running either.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

  concept="The procedure follows from three facts about collection and one about the question, and never from how many columns are on the screen: what was recorded on each unit (a category gives a proportion procedure, a measurement gives a t procedure), how many groups of units, and — where there are two columns of measurements — whether the two hold the same units, which decides paired against independent. Only the last choice, interval or test, comes from what is being asked: an interval returns a magnitude with its precision, a test a decision at a level fixed in advance.
