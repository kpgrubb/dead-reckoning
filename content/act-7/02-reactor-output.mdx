<Briefing title="The one-sample t-interval, and what it is about" topics={["7.2", "7.3"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
An interval estimates a parameter. Here the parameter is $\mu$, the mean of the measured quantity across the whole population the sample came from, and the interval is

$$\bar{x} \pm t^\star \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Three quantities decide its width and exactly three.

- **$n$**, through $\sqrt{n}$ in the standard error and through $t^\star$ on $n-1$ df. Both push the same way. Four times the sample gives a little better than half the margin.
- **$C$**, the confidence level, through $t^\star$. A higher level buys a higher long-run capture rate by covering more ground, so it widens the interval. The level is chosen before the data exist and is never something the data earn.
- **$s$**, the spread of the individuals. It is a property of the population, not a choice.

The **population size is not on that list.** It appears once, in the 10% condition, as a yes-or-no gate on whether the procedure applies. Past that gate, twelve observations out of a register of four hundred buy exactly the precision twelve observations out of four thousand buy.

**Sample size for a target margin.** Set the margin you want and solve:

$$n = \left(\frac{z^\star \sigma}{m}\right)^2$$

rounded **up**, because a fraction of an observation buys nothing. Planning uses $z^\star$ and a $\sigma$ from history for a circular reason worth saying out loud: $t^\star$ needs the df, the df needs $n$, and $n$ is what you are solving for. The interval you eventually build will use $t^\star$ and come out a little wider, so a careful planner adds a few.

**Interpretation.** One sentence, four parts, and the exam marks all four.

> We are **C% confident** that the **true mean** [quantity] of **[the population]** is between **[lower]** and **[upper]** [units].

*Confident*, not *probable*: the parameter is a fixed number and this interval either contains it or does not. The level describes the long-run capture rate of the **procedure** that built it. Run the procedure on sample after sample and C% of the intervals it produces will contain $\mu$; that is the whole content of the claim.

**Judging a claim.** An interval supports "the mean exceeds $k$" only when the *entire* interval lies above $k$, which is a question about the **lower endpoint** and not about $\bar{x}$. A value inside the interval is a value the data cannot rule out, and the honest sentence about it says so.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex={`\\bar{x} \\pm t^\\star \\frac{s}{\\sqrt{n}}, \\qquad df = n - 1`} terms={{ '\\bar{x}': 'sample mean — the centre of the interval', 't^\\star': 'critical value of t on n − 1 df at level C', '\\frac{s}{\\sqrt{n}}': 'standard error of x̄: how far a sample mean typically falls from μ' }} label="One-sample t-interval for a population mean" />

<Formula tex={`n = \\left(\\frac{z^\\star \\sigma}{m}\\right)^2 \\ \\text{(round up)}`} terms={{ 'm': 'the largest margin of error you are willing to report', '\\sigma': 'a planning value for the spread, from history or a pilot run' }} label="Sample size for a target margin on a mean" />
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
*Halden Reach*'s boat bay tested **{fmtInt(clampN)}** rebuilt mooring clamps to first slip, in kilonewtons, against a rated {fmtInt(clampRated)} kN: {clampLoads.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

*Summaries.* $\bar{x}$ = {fmt(mean(clampLoads), 4)} kN, $s$ = {fmt(sd(clampLoads), 4)} kN, $n$ = {fmtInt(clampN)}, $df$ = {fmtInt(clampN - 1)}.

*Standard error.* $s/\sqrt{n}$ = {fmt(sd(clampLoads), 4)} ÷ √{fmtInt(clampN)} = {fmt(clampSe, 4)} kN.

*Critical value.* At 95% on {fmtInt(clampN - 1)} df, $t^\star$ = {fmt(tStar(0.95, clampN - 1), 4)}.

*Margin.* {fmt(tStar(0.95, clampN - 1), 4)} × {fmt(clampSe, 4)} = {fmt(clampInterval.marginOfError ?? 0, 4)} kN.

*Interval.* {fmt(mean(clampLoads), 4)} ± {fmt(clampInterval.marginOfError ?? 0, 4)} = **({fmt(clampInterval.ci[0], 2)}, {fmt(clampInterval.ci[1], 2)}) kN**.

*In a sentence.* We are 95% confident that the true mean first-slip load of all clamps rebuilt to this standard is between {fmt(clampInterval.ci[0], 2)} and {fmt(clampInterval.ci[1], 2)} kilonewtons.

*Does it carry the claim?* The bay wants to say the rebuild holds above the {fmtInt(clampRated)} kN rating. The lower endpoint is {fmt(clampInterval.ci[0], 2)}, so every value the interval contains is above {fmtInt(clampRated)}, and the claim is carried. Had the lower endpoint come out at {fmtInt(clampRated - 1)}, the honest report would say the data are consistent with the rating and cannot improve on it, whatever $\bar{x}$ happened to be.

*What would a tighter margin cost?* The bay would like ±{fmt(clampTarget, 1)} kN. Planning on $\sigma$ = {fmt(sd(clampLoads), 4)} from this batch, $n = (z^\star \sigma / m)^2$ = ({fmt(zStar(0.95), 3)} × {fmt(sd(clampLoads), 4)} ÷ {fmt(clampTarget, 1)})² = {fmt(((zStar(0.95) * sd(clampLoads)) / clampTarget) ** 2, 2)}, rounded up to **{fmtInt(clampSize)} clamps**. Roughly {fmt(clampSize / clampN, 1)} times the batch for a margin {fmt((clampInterval.marginOfError ?? 0) / clampTarget, 1)} times narrower. Precision costs the square.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**"95% of the clamps hold between {fmt(clampInterval.ci[0], 2)} and {fmt(clampInterval.ci[1], 2)} kN."** The interval estimates one number, the population mean. The individual clamps scatter around that mean by roughly $s$ = {fmt(sd(clampLoads), 2)} kN, which is about {fmt(sd(clampLoads) / (clampInterval.marginOfError ?? 1), 1)} times the margin, so the range the individuals occupy is far wider than the interval and the two are not the same statement. Say **true mean**, every time, and the error becomes impossible to write.

**The interval is not about $\bar{x}$.** {fmt(mean(clampLoads), 2)} kN is known exactly and needs no interval. The unknown is $\mu$.

**"95% probability that $\mu$ is in this interval."** $\mu$ is a fixed number and this interval either contains it or does not; there is nothing left to be probabilistic about once the data are in. The 95% belongs to the procedure. It is the rate at which intervals built this way capture the parameter over many samples.

**Reading a claim off the point estimate.** $\bar{x}$ clears a published figure in every sample where the true mean is anywhere near it. The lower endpoint is what says the claim survives the sample having come out differently by an ordinary amount.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
