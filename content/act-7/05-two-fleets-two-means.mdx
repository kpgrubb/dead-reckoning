<Briefing title="Two independent samples, one difference" topics={["7.6", "7.7"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Two groups are **independent** when knowing any value in one tells you nothing about any value in the other: different units, no matching rule, and usually different group sizes. There is no row to subtract along, so the file keeps both columns and each group keeps its own $n$, its own $\bar{x}$ and its own $s$.

The parameter is $\mu_1 - \mu_2$, a difference between two population means, and the statistic that estimates it is $\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2$. Its standard error comes from the rule for the variance of a difference of independent quantities: variances add. Each group contributes $s^2/n$, the two are summed, and the square root is taken at the end.

$$SE = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}$$

Every symbol in that expression belongs to one group only. Nothing is averaged across the two, and in particular the two standard deviations are never combined into a single figure. Combining them is called **pooling**, and it is legitimate only when the two populations are known to have the same standard deviation, which observational data essentially never establishes. The AP two-sample procedure is the unpooled one.

The price of not pooling is that $(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)/SE$ does not follow a $t$ distribution exactly, only approximately, and the approximation needs a number of degrees of freedom. Two rules are accepted.

**Welch's.** A formula that returns a real number, usually not an integer, chosen so the approximation is as close as it can be. Every calculator and every package uses it, and a printed $df$ of {fmt(escortWelchDf, 2)} is the formula working rather than a rounding fault.

**The conservative rule.** $df = \min(n_1 - 1,\ n_2 - 1)$, which is always smaller than Welch's. A smaller $df$ gives a larger $t^\star$ and a wider interval, so an interval built this way captures the parameter at least as often as the stated level. It is a hand method for a table, it is safe, and it is not more accurate. Either rule is accepted; the write-up must say which one produced the number.

The interval is the familiar shape, and the sentence under it names both endpoints, the parameter, the population and which group was subtracted from which.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t^\star \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}" terms={{ '\\bar{x}_1 - \\bar{x}_2': 'the point estimate of μ₁ − μ₂, in the order you chose', 's_1, s_2': "each group's own sample standard deviation — never combined", 't^\\star': 'the critical value at the chosen level, on Welch or conservative df' }} label="Two-sample t-interval for a difference of means" />

<Formula tex="df_{\text{cons}} = \min(n_1 - 1,\ n_2 - 1) \qquad\text{or}\qquad df_{\text{Welch}} = \frac{\left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\frac{1}{n_1-1}\left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{n_2-1}\left(\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}" terms={{ 'df_{\\text{cons}}': 'the hand rule: always smaller, always wider, always safe', 'df_{\\text{Welch}}': 'what technology returns; real-valued, and reported as such' }} label="Two rules for the degrees of freedom" />

**Conditions, checked group by group.** Random: each group is a random sample from its own population, or the two arose by random assignment. Independent: the two groups are independent of one another, and each sample is no more than 10% of its own population. Normal: for each group, $n \geq 30$, or a graph of that group showing no strong skewness and no outliers. A failure in either group stops the procedure.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The 2176 escort trial on the Mars–Belt corridor, from the report that has your name on the cover. Delay against the filed plot, in days, for hulls that took an escort and for hulls that routed independently over the same eleven weeks. Different hulls in the two lists, assigned by the Authority's duty roster rather than by anyone's choice, and nothing matching one to another.

Escorted, {fmtInt(escorted.length)} hulls: {escorted.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

Independently routed, {fmtInt(unescorted.length)} hulls: {unescorted.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

Group summaries. Escorted: $\bar{x}_1$ = {fmt(mean(escorted), 3)} d, $s_1$ = {fmt(sd(escorted), 3)}, $n_1$ = {fmtInt(escorted.length)}. Independent: $\bar{x}_2$ = {fmt(mean(unescorted), 3)} d, $s_2$ = {fmt(sd(unescorted), 3)}, $n_2$ = {fmtInt(unescorted.length)}.

*Estimate.* {fmt(mean(escorted), 3)} − {fmt(mean(unescorted), 3)} = **{fmt(escortWelch.estimate, 3)} d**, escorted minus independent.

*Standard error.* {fmt(sd(escorted), 3)}² ÷ {fmtInt(escorted.length)} = {fmt(sd(escorted) ** 2 / escorted.length, 5)} and {fmt(sd(unescorted), 3)}² ÷ {fmtInt(unescorted.length)} = {fmt(sd(unescorted) ** 2 / unescorted.length, 5)}. Add, take the root: **{fmt(escortWelch.se, 4)}**.

<Formula tex={`(${fmt(escortWelch.ci[0], 2)},\\ ${fmt(escortWelch.ci[1], 2)})\\ \\text{days}`} label="The 95% interval for the difference in mean delay, escorted minus independently routed" />

Both endpoints are negative, so every difference the data are consistent with has escorted hulls arriving nearer their filed plot, by somewhere between {fmt(Math.abs(escortWelch.ci[1]), 2)} and {fmt(Math.abs(escortWelch.ci[0]), 2)} days on average. The direction is half the finding. The size is the other half, and it is the half a Board acts on.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**Pooling.** Ticking the box replaces $s_1$ and $s_2$ with one weighted figure and claims $n_1 + n_2 - 2$ degrees of freedom for it. That is a great many more degrees of freedom than the data earned, and it rests on an assumption about two population standard deviations that nothing in an observational file supports. When the groups are also unequal in size the larger one sets the spread for the smaller, and the interval can come out badly wrong in either direction. Leave the box clear.

**"Conservative df is more accurate."** It is smaller on purpose. A smaller $df$ widens the interval, and an interval wider than it needs to be captures more often than the level it advertises, which is caution rather than accuracy. Use it when you are working from a table; use Welch's when a machine is doing the arithmetic; say which one you used either way.

**An interval that contains zero.** Zero inside the interval means the data have not ruled out "no difference". They have not ruled out the endpoints either, and those may be large. Report it as an absence of convincing evidence and give the range, because a reader deciding what to do next needs to know how much room is left. The two means being equal is a claim no interval can make.

**The order of subtraction.** Group 1 minus group 2 reflects through zero if you swap them, and the two intervals mean the same thing. A reader who is not told which way round it goes will take the sign at face value, so the sentence has to name the order.

**Two columns are not automatically two samples.** If the two values in a row came off the same unit, this is the wrong procedure entirely and the paired procedure is the right one. Independence is a fact about the design, checked before the arithmetic starts.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
