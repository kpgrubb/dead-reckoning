<Briefing title="The same two samples, as a decision" topics={["7.8", "7.9"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
An interval and a test for two independent means are built from the same two numbers. The point estimate $\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2$ and the unpooled standard error $\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}$ are computed once. An interval multiplies the standard error by $t^\star$ and lays the product either side of the estimate. A test divides the estimate by the standard error and reads the result against a null model. Same arithmetic, two questions: how large, and whether there is evidence of anything at all.

The **parameter** is $\mu_1 - \mu_2$, and the hypotheses are written about it before the statistic exists.

$$H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0 \qquad H_a: \mu_1 - \mu_2 > 0,\ < 0,\ \text{or} \neq 0$$

The null value is almost always zero, which says the two population means are equal. The alternative comes from the question the office asked, and from nothing that happened in the data.

The **statistic** is the estimate, measured in standard errors, away from the null value.

$$t = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - 0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

The **degrees of freedom** are Welch's, real-valued, or the conservative $\min(n_1 - 1, n_2 - 1)$, which is smaller and gives a larger p-value. Either is accepted; the write-up names which one produced the number.

The **p-value** is the area in the tail the alternative points to, under $t$ on those degrees of freedom. It is the probability, computed in the world where $H_0$ is exactly true, of a difference in sample means at least as extreme as the one observed. A two-sided alternative counts both tails.

The **conclusion** compares $p$ with $\alpha$, states the decision, and then says what the decision is evidence about: two population means, named, in a population, named.

**Conditions, group by group.** Random: each group is a random sample from its own population, or the two arose by random assignment. Independent: the groups are independent of one another, and each sample is no more than 10% of its own population. Normal: for each group, $n \geq 30$ or a graph of that group showing no strong skew and no outliers. A failure in either group stops the procedure.

**Scope, which is a separate question from significance.** A small p-value says the gap would be surprising under $H_0$. It says nothing about who the two groups are or how they came to be different, and two features of the design settle that instead.

- **Random assignment** to the groups is what licenses a causal conclusion, and only about the units studied.
- **Random sampling** from a population is what lets the conclusion travel to that population.

A study can have either, both or neither, and a comparison that has neither still produces a legitimate finding: a direction, a size, and whatever the timestamps on the measurements establish about the order events happened in. What it cannot produce is a cause or a population, and the write-up says so in its own words rather than waiting to be told.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="t = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - \Delta_0}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}, \qquad \Delta_0 = 0" terms={{ '\\bar{x}_1 - \\bar{x}_2': 'the point estimate of μ₁ − μ₂, in the order you chose', '\\Delta_0': 'the hypothesised difference, almost always 0', 's_1, s_2': "each group's own standard deviation — never combined" }} label="The two-sample t statistic" />

<Formula tex="P = P(T_{df} \geq t) \quad\text{or}\quad P(T_{df} \leq t) \quad\text{or}\quad 2P(T_{df} \geq |t|)" terms={{ 'df': "Welch's, real-valued, or the conservative min(n₁ − 1, n₂ − 1)", 'P': 'the area in the tail the alternative points to, under the null model' }} label="The p-value, one tail or two" />

The interval and the test are the same procedure asked two ways. A two-sided test at $\alpha$ rejects exactly when the $100(1 - \alpha)\%$ interval excludes the null value; a one-sided test at $\alpha$ lines up with the $100(1 - 2\alpha)\%$ interval instead. Where a report quotes both, it quotes the level of each.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The 2176 escort trial on the Mars–Belt corridor, the same file the previous module put an interval around. Delay against the filed plot, in days, for hulls that took an escort and for hulls that routed independently over the same eleven weeks. Different hulls in the two lists, assigned by the Authority's duty roster.

Escorted, {fmtInt(escorted.length)} hulls: {escorted.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

Independently routed, {fmtInt(unescorted.length)} hulls: {unescorted.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

*State.* Let $\mu_1$ be the true mean delay of escorted hulls and $\mu_2$ that of independently routed hulls on the Mars–Belt corridor in 2176. The Authority asked whether escorts get hulls nearer their filed plot, so $H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0$ against $H_a: \mu_1 - \mu_2 < 0$, at $\alpha$ = {fmt(escortAlpha, 2)}.

*Plan.* A two-sample $t$-test, unpooled. Random: the roster assigned the escorts. Independent: no hull is in both lists, and {fmtInt(10 * escorted.length)} and {fmtInt(10 * unescorted.length)} are far below the corridor's traffic. Normal: both groups are under thirty, and both dotplots are roughly symmetric with nothing stranded.

*Do.* $\bar{x}_1$ = {fmt(mean(escorted), 3)}, $s_1$ = {fmt(sd(escorted), 3)}, $n_1$ = {fmtInt(escorted.length)}. $\bar{x}_2$ = {fmt(mean(unescorted), 3)}, $s_2$ = {fmt(sd(unescorted), 3)}, $n_2$ = {fmtInt(unescorted.length)}.

<Formula tex={`\\bar{x}_1 - \\bar{x}_2 = ${fmt(escortTest.estimate, 4)}, \\qquad SE = \\sqrt{\\frac{${fmt(sd(escorted), 3)}^2}{${escorted.length}} + \\frac{${fmt(sd(unescorted), 3)}^2}{${unescorted.length}}} = ${fmt(escortTest.se, 4)}`} label="Estimate and unpooled standard error" />

<Formula tex={`t = \\frac{${fmt(escortTest.estimate, 4)} - 0}{${fmt(escortTest.se, 4)}} = ${fmt(escortTest.statistic, 4)}, \\qquad df_{\\text{Welch}} = ${fmt(escortWelchDf, 2)}, \\qquad P = ${fmtP(escortTest.pValue)}`} label="Statistic, degrees of freedom and one-sided p-value" />

The conservative rule reads the same statistic at $\min$({fmtInt(escorted.length - 1)}, {fmtInt(unescorted.length - 1)}) = {fmtInt(escortConsDf)} degrees of freedom and returns $P$ = {fmtP(escortCons.pValue)}, a little larger and the same decision. The two-sided reading of the same statistic doubles the tail to {fmtP(escortTwoSided.pValue)}.

*Conclude.* Because $P$ = {fmtP(escortTest.pValue)} is less than $\alpha$ = {fmt(escortAlpha, 2)}, reject $H_0$. There is convincing evidence that the true mean delay against the filed plot of escorted hulls is less than that of independently routed hulls, for hulls working the Mars–Belt corridor in 2176.

*Scope.* The duty roster assigned the escorts, and it did so by rota rather than at anyone's request, which is close enough to random assignment that the report argued for a causal reading and said exactly what it was arguing from. The hulls were not a random sample of the corridor's traffic, so the conclusion covers the eleven weeks in the trial and stops there.

*The same file as an interval.* The {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} two-sample $t$-interval for $\mu_1 - \mu_2$ runs {fmt(escortInterval.ci[0], 3)} to {fmt(escortInterval.ci[1], 3)} days, which is the estimate {fmt(escortInterval.estimate, 4)} give or take a margin of {fmt(escortInterval.marginOfError, 4)}. Every number in it has already appeared above. The interval says how much and the test says how surprising, and a brief that has to survive a hearing carries both with their levels named.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**A conclusion about the samples.** "The escorted hulls were faster" describes {fmtInt(escorted.length)} rows that are already known exactly and needed no procedure. The claim the test supports is about $\mu_1 - \mu_2$, a difference between two population means nobody has measured, and the sentence has to name the population it belongs to.

**Significance read as size.** A p-value falls when the gap grows and it also falls when the samples grow. On two groups of several hundred, a difference too small to matter clears any level; on two groups of nine, a difference that matters enormously may not. The magnitude lives in the interval, and a report that quotes only $P$ has thrown it away.

**Proof, and intent.** A significance test returns evidence at a stated level. It cannot return proof, and it cannot return a reason. Where the groups were not assigned at random, every difference that put a unit in one group rather than the other is still in the comparison, and the arithmetic has no way to tell them apart from the one being measured.

**Selecting a group on its outcome.** A group assembled by reading the outcome first is unusual by construction, which costs two things exactly: it is a sample of no population, and it supports no causal claim. It does not cost the description. Direction survives, size survives, and so does anything the timestamps establish about what was recorded before the outcome occurred.

**Choosing the tail after looking.** The alternative is written from the question. Picking the direction the data happened to fall and then reporting one tail is a two-sided procedure quoting a one-sided number, and it halves the p-value on no evidence at all.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
