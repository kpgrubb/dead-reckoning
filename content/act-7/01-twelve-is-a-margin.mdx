<Briefing title="What happens when s stands in for σ" topics={["7.1", "7.2"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Unit 5 standardized a sample mean like this:

$$z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

and every number in the denominator was fixed before the sample was drawn. $\sigma$ was a property of the population, $n$ a property of the design. Only the numerator varied from sample to sample, and the result followed a standard normal distribution.

In practice $\sigma$ is not available. You have the sample, so you estimate the population standard deviation with the sample standard deviation $s$, and the statistic becomes

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{s / \sqrt{n}}$$

Now **both** parts vary. Each new sample produces a new $\bar{x}$ *and* a new $s$, and the two are not in step. A sample that happens to come out tightly bunched gives a small $s$, which shrinks the denominator, which inflates the statistic. A sample that happens to come out spread gives a large $s$ and deflates it. The extra wobble shows up where it matters most: far more of these statistics land out in the tails than a standard normal distribution would put there.

The distribution they do follow is the **t distribution**, and it comes in a family indexed by one number, the **degrees of freedom**. For a one-sample procedure, $df = n - 1$.

Why $n - 1$, and why it matters here: $s$ is built from the deviations $x_i - \bar{x}$, and those deviations always sum to exactly zero, because $\bar{x}$ was computed from the same data. Fix any $n - 1$ of them and the last one is determined. Only $n - 1$ pieces of information about spread are free, which is the quantity the distribution is indexed by.

Every t distribution is symmetric about zero and bell-shaped, like the normal, and shorter in the middle with heavier tails. As $df$ grows, $s$ estimates $\sigma$ more reliably, the extra wobble shrinks, and the t curve closes on the standard normal. It closes fast at first and then very slowly, and it never arrives.

The practical consequence is the **critical value**. A confidence interval for a mean is

$$\bar{x} \pm t^\star \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

and $t^\star$ is always larger than the $z^\star$ at the same level. At 95% and $df = 6$, $t^\star$ is {fmt(tStar(0.95, 6), 3)} against $z^\star$ = {fmt(zStar(0.95), 3)}: a margin {fmtPct(tStar(0.95, 6) / zStar(0.95) - 1, 0)} wider. At $df = 60$ it is {fmt(tStar(0.95, 60), 3)}, and at $df = 500$, {fmt(tStar(0.95, 500), 3)}.

**Conditions.** A t procedure for a mean needs three things, and the third is the one that changes on a small sample.

- **Random.** The data come from a random sample, or from a census of the whole population, in which case say so and say what the population is.
- **10%, or independence.** Sampling without replacement makes observations slightly dependent. If $n$ is at most a tenth of the population, the effect is negligible. State the arithmetic.
- **Normal / Large Sample.** With $n \ge 30$ the central limit theorem carries the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ to approximate Normality whatever the population looks like. Below thirty it does not, and there is no substitute: graph the sample and argue from the graph. The working standard is no outliers by the $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ rule and no strong skew. That is why a dotplot gets drawn before an interval gets quoted.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex={`t = \\frac{\\bar{x} - \\mu}{s / \\sqrt{n}}, \\qquad df = n - 1`} terms={{ 's': 'sample standard deviation — an estimate of σ, computed from the same data as x̄', 'df = n - 1': 'degrees of freedom: one is spent estimating the mean, so n − 1 deviations are free', '\\bar{x}': 'sample mean' }} label="The one-sample t statistic" />

<Formula tex={`\\bar{x} \\pm t^\\star \\frac{s}{\\sqrt{n}}`} terms={{ 't^\\star': 'critical value of t on n − 1 df, with the central C of the distribution between ±t*', '\\frac{s}{\\sqrt{n}}': 'standard error of the sample mean' }} label="The one-sample t-interval for a mean" />

For every $df$ and every level, $t^\star > z^\star$. The gap is the price of not knowing $\sigma$.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The tender's own workshop logged the run-up time of **{fmtInt(pumpN)}** reclaimer pumps after a bearing change, in seconds: {pumpRunUp.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}.

*Summaries.* $\bar{x}$ = {fmt(mean(pumpRunUp), 3)} s, $s$ = {fmt(sd(pumpRunUp), 3)} s, $n$ = {fmtInt(pumpN)}.

*Degrees of freedom.* $n - 1$ = **{fmtInt(pumpDf)}**. Not {fmtInt(pumpN)}.

*Standard error.* $s/\sqrt{n}$ = {fmt(sd(pumpRunUp), 3)} ÷ √{fmtInt(pumpN)} = **{fmt(pumpSe, 4)} s**.

*Critical value.* At 95% the interval leaves {fmt(0.05, 2)} outside it, {fmt(0.025, 3)} in each tail, so $t^\star$ is the {fmt(0.975, 3)} quantile of t on {fmtInt(pumpDf)} df: **{fmt(pumpT, 4)}**. The normal table would have given {fmt(pumpZ, 4)}.

*Margin.* {fmt(pumpT, 4)} × {fmt(pumpSe, 4)} = **{fmt(pumpT * pumpSe, 4)} s**, against {fmt(pumpZ * pumpSe, 4)} s from the normal multiplier. The same seven pumps, the same spread, and {fmt(pumpT * pumpSe - pumpZ * pumpSe, 4)} seconds of difference in what the workshop is entitled to claim.

*Tails.* The cutoff ±{fmt(1.96, 2)} carries {fmt(2 * normal.sf(1.96), 4)} of a standard normal distribution outside it. On t with {fmtInt(pumpDf)} df it carries {fmt(2 * t.sf(1.96, pumpDf), 4)}, more than twice as much. A p-value read off the wrong curve here would be understated by that factor.

*Conditions.* Random: the workshop tested every pump it rebuilt, a census of the batch, and that has to be written down as what it is. 10%: {fmtInt(10 * pumpN)} is far below the number of pumps of this type in service. Normal: $n$ = {fmtInt(pumpN)} is well under thirty, so the central limit theorem has nothing to offer; the seven values have {fmtInt(outliers(pumpRunUp).values.length)} outliers by the $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ rule and a skewness of {fmt(skewness(pumpRunUp), 2)}, which is a graph a t procedure can be run on.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**"t is for small samples, z is for large ones."** The size of the sample decides how close $t^\star$ and $z^\star$ come. It never decides which one is correct. The correct distribution is t whenever $s$ stands in the denominator, at $n$ = 7 and at $n$ = 7,000 alike, and $\sigma$ does not become known by the sample getting bigger. What happens with a large sample is that the error from using the wrong curve falls below the precision anyone is reporting to.

**$df = n$.** Degrees of freedom are $n - 1$ for every one-sample t procedure. Off by one in the df is off in the third decimal of $t^\star$ on a large sample and off in the first on a small one, which is exactly where it does damage.

**The central limit theorem on a sample of twelve.** The theorem describes what happens as $n$ grows without bound. Quoting it for twelve observations is citing a limit that has not been approached. On a small sample the Normal condition is settled by looking at the data and saying what the display shows.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
