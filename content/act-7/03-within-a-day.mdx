<Briefing title="Testing a mean, and what a comparison could have seen" topics={["7.4", "7.5"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
**Hypotheses.** A significance test about a mean starts with two statements about the parameter $\mu$, written before the data are read.

$$H_0: \mu = \mu_0 \qquad H_a: \mu > \mu_0 \ \text{ or } \ \mu < \mu_0 \ \text{ or } \ \mu \neq \mu_0$$

$\mu_0$ is the hypothesised value: a published figure, a filed plot, a specification. Both hypotheses are about the population. Neither mentions $\bar{x}$, which is already known and needs no test.

**The statistic.** Unit 5 standardized a sample mean against a known $\sigma$ and read the result off a normal table. That step is gone. With $\sigma$ estimated by $s$ from the same sample,

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}, \qquad df = n - 1$$

and the p-value is a tail area under $t$ with that many degrees of freedom. The contrast is worth naming because it is the whole difference between Unit 5 and this one: **σ known gives z, s estimated gives t.** Nothing else changed. The hypotheses, the conditions, the conclusion and the interpretation are the same work in the same order.

**Conditions.** Random, 10% or independence, and Normal/Large Sample, with numbers attached to each, exactly as for the interval.

**The p-value.** Assume $H_0$ is exactly true. The p-value is the probability that a sample of this size would produce a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It is conditional on $H_0$ from its first word to its last. It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true, and it is not the probability that the result arose by chance; both of those would require something the procedure never computed.

**The conclusion.** Compare $p$ with $\alpha$. If $p < \alpha$, reject $H_0$ and say there is convincing evidence of the alternative, named in context. If $p \ge \alpha$, fail to reject, and never write *accept*. A test that fails to reject has found the data consistent with $H_0$, which is a far weaker statement than $H_0$ being true.

**Why a subset vanishes into its population.** Suppose $N$ units in a fleet, of which $k$ run late by $d$ on average and the remaining $N - k$ fly their filed plots. The fleet mean is the weighted average, so it sits above the honest mean by

$$\frac{k \, d}{N}$$

and that fraction $k/N$ is the whole story. A large effect on a small subset arrives at the fleet mean already divided by the size of the fleet. The subset did not become less late. The average stopped being able to see it.

**What a comparison could have seen: power.** A test that reports nothing is telling you one of two things, and the arithmetic cannot tell you which: either there is nothing there, or the test could not have found what is there. **Power** settles it. Power is the probability of rejecting $H_0$ when a *specified* alternative is true, and it needs three inputs, none of which is the data you collected:

1. the effect you care about detecting;
2. the standard error the design gives you;
3. $\alpha$.

For a one-sided comparison of two means with groups large enough that $t$ is the normal:

$$\text{reject when the observed difference exceeds } z^\star \cdot SE, \qquad \text{power} = P\!\left(Z > \frac{z^\star SE - \text{effect}}{SE}\right)$$

When the cutoff sits well above the effect, the power is small and a null result carries no information at all. Computing it is the only honest way to read "we found nothing", and it is the same Type I / Type II machinery Unit 6 used on proportions, in the units of a mean.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex={`t = \\frac{\\bar{x} - \\mu_0}{s / \\sqrt{n}}, \\qquad df = n - 1`} terms={{ '\\mu_0': 'the hypothesised mean — a published figure, never the sample mean', 's': 'sample standard deviation: σ is unknown, which is what makes this t and not z' }} label="One-sample t-test statistic" />

<Formula tex={`\\bar{x}_{\\text{all}} = \\bar{x}_{\\text{honest}} + \\frac{k \\, d}{N}`} terms={{ 'k': 'units carrying the excess', 'd': 'the excess each of them carries', 'N': 'units in the whole population the mean is taken over' }} label="A late subset averaged into its population" />

<Formula tex={`\\text{power} = P\\!\\left(Z > \\frac{z^\\star SE - \\delta}{SE}\\right)`} terms={{ '\\delta': 'the specified effect the test is being asked to detect', 'z^\\star SE': 'the observed difference the test needs before it rejects', 'SE': 'standard error of the difference in sample means' }} label="Power of a one-sided comparison against a specified effect" />
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
**A test about a mean.** *Halden Reach*'s navigation office timed **{fmtInt(buoyRuns.length)}** of her own boats from the lock to the outer buoy, in minutes, against a filed transit of {fmtInt(buoyFiled)}: {buoyRuns.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}. The office wants to know whether the boats are running long.

*Hypotheses.* $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ against $H_a: \mu > \mu_0$, with $\mu_0$ = {fmtInt(buoyFiled)} minutes and $\mu$ the true mean lock-to-buoy time for boats on this run.

*Conditions.* The {fmtInt(buoyRuns.length)} runs were the office's whole week of traffic, so it is a census of that week and the scope of the conclusion is that week's conditions. Ten times {fmtInt(buoyRuns.length)} is far below the boats' annual movements. With $n$ = {fmtInt(buoyRuns.length)} under thirty, the dotplot is what carries Normality, and it has nothing stranded and no tail.

*Statistic.* $\bar{x}$ = {fmt(mean(buoyRuns), 4)} min and $s$ = {fmt(sd(buoyRuns), 4)}, so $s/\sqrt{n}$ = {fmt(buoySe, 4)} min.

<Formula tex={`t = \\frac{${fmt(mean(buoyRuns), 4)} - ${buoyFiled}}{${fmt(buoySe, 4)}} = ${fmt(buoyTest.statistic, 4)}`} label={`One-sample t on ${buoyRuns.length - 1} degrees of freedom`} />

*p-value.* The area above that statistic under t on {fmtInt(buoyRuns.length - 1)} degrees of freedom is **{fmtP(buoyTest.pValue)}**. Off a normal table the same statistic would have read {fmtP(pValueZ(buoyTest.statistic, 'greater'))}, smaller, and smaller in the direction that flatters the finding.

*Conclusion at $\alpha$ = {fmt(0.05, 2)}.* {fmtP(buoyTest.pValue)} < {fmt(0.05, 2)}, so reject $H_0$. There is convincing evidence that the true mean lock-to-buoy time for boats on this run is greater than {fmtInt(buoyFiled)} minutes.

*The p-value in words.* Assuming the null hypothesis is true, so that the true mean is exactly {fmtInt(buoyFiled)} minutes, about {fmtP(buoyTest.pValue)} of all samples of {fmtInt(buoyRuns.length)} runs would produce a sample mean at least as far above {fmtInt(buoyFiled)} minutes as the {fmt(mean(buoyRuns), 2)} these eight produced.

**A subset, and the power to see it.** A different office, a different corridor. Of {fmtInt(demoFleet)} transits on a run, suppose {fmtInt(demoLate)} carry an average excess of {fmt(demoExcess, 1)} days and the rest are honest. On the fleet mean that excess arrives as

<Formula tex={`\\frac{${demoLate} \\times ${fmt(demoExcess, 1)}}{${demoFleet}} = ${fmt(demoDilution, 4)}\\ \\text{days}`} label="The excess after the fleet has been averaged over it" />

Compare that run against a second group, and suppose the comparison's standard error is {fmt(demoSe, 2)} days. At $\alpha$ = {fmt(0.05, 2)} one-sided, the test rejects only when the observed gap exceeds {fmt(demoPower.rejectAbove, 4)} days, and the power against a real gap of {fmt(demoDilution, 4)} days is **{fmt(demoPower.power, 3)}**.

Read that as it deserves to be read. With {fmtInt(demoLate)} genuinely late transits sitting in the file the whole time, this comparison reports nothing {fmtPct(demoPower.beta, 0)} of the time. Nothing about its arithmetic is wrong. It was asked a question it had no instrument for.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
**Reading a small-sample p-value off a normal table.** On {buoyRuns.length - 1} degrees of freedom the statistic above gives {fmtP(buoyTest.pValue)}; the normal table gives {fmtP(pValueZ(buoyTest.statistic, 'greater'))}. The t tail is always the heavier one, so the normal reading always understates the p-value, and it understates it most when the sample is smallest.

**$\mu_0$ set to the sample mean.** The hypothesised value comes from outside the data: a filed figure, a plate, a standard. Entering $\bar{x}$ in its place gives $t = 0$ and $p = 1$ every time, which is a reliable sign the wrong number went in the wrong field.

**"Not significant" read as "no difference".** Failing to reject means the data are consistent with $H_0$. It does not mean $H_0$ is true, and a comparison with low power against the effect in question was never able to say anything else. Before reading a null result as an absence, price the miss: what effect were you looking for, what standard error did the design give you, and what was the power against it?

**A pooled mean read as a clean bill.** An average over a mixed population is exactly what it should be when a small subset is badly wrong. The average agreeing with expectation is not evidence that every unit does. The fix is to stop pooling and compare the groups.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
