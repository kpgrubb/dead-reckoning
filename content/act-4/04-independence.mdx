<Briefing title="The given that changes nothing" topics={["4.6"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Conditional probability asked what happens to $P(A)$ when you are told $B$. **Independence** is the case where the answer is *nothing*.

$$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$

That is the whole definition. Knowing $B$ moves the probability of $A$ nowhere; the "given" is information you could have thrown away. Multiply both sides of the definition of a conditional by $P(B)$ and you get the same statement in a form that does not care which event you condition on:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$$

Two checks, one idea. Use whichever the data hands you: a table of conditional rows suits the first, a joint cell against a product of margins suits the second. They agree, always, and they fail together.

**Why the multiplication rule needs it.** The general multiplication rule is always true: $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)$. Probability flows down one branch and then down another, and the second branch's probability is a *conditional* one. Independence is the licence to replace $P(B \mid A)$ with $P(B)$, to forget the branch you came down. Without that licence, multiplying $P(A)$ by $P(B)$ answers a question about some other process. When the two events reinforce each other, as consecutive sweeps do, the product comes out far too small and runs look rare that are not rare. When they oppose each other, the product comes out far too large.

**Mutually exclusive is the opposite of independent, not a synonym.** If $A$ and $B$ cannot both happen then $P(A \cap B) = 0$, so learning that $B$ happened tells you $A$ certainly did not: $P(A \mid B) = 0$, and unless $P(A)$ was zero to begin with, that is the largest change a "given" can make. Disjoint events with positive probability are as dependent as two events get. The words sound alike and mean opposite things.

**The general addition rule is a separate matter.** "Or" needs no independence at all, only care about double counting:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

Add the two, and everything in the overlap has been counted twice, so take it off once. When the events are mutually exclusive the overlap is zero and the rule collapses to $P(A) + P(B)$. That is the only case in which the short form is correct.

**Independence is a property of a model, tested against a file.** Nothing is independent because it feels unrelated. Two scheduled close passes by two hulls filed months apart by two offices are independent because no mechanism joins them. Two consecutive sweep entries are not, because a mechanism does join them and the ship wrote that mechanism into its own doctrine. When a check fails, the useful question is never "how far from independent" but **what connects them**, and the physics, the procedure or the traffic answers that. The table never does.

Last, and this one ends careers: a table can only ever say that two variables *appear* dependent **in that table**. Records are not a random sample of the Lane, nobody assigned a hull its owner, and the sentence that survives a hostile reader says *in this file*. Getting from these records to the Lane is Act VIII's work, and it needs a hypothesis, a null model and a sampling distribution, none of which is in front of you yet.
</Briefing.Intuition>

**And the other table.** The Ledger's loss × owner counts are {fmtInt(LEDGER.perrineLosses)} and {fmtInt(LEDGER.perrineTransits - LEDGER.perrineLosses)} against {fmtInt(LEDGER.otherLosses)} and {fmtInt(LEDGER.otherTransits - LEDGER.otherLosses)}. </Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"Independent" read as "mutually exclusive".* They are opposite properties. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together, so one occurring rules the other out, which is the strongest dependence there is. Independent events with positive probability *must* be able to occur together, and the size of their overlap is fixed at exactly $P(A)\,P(B)$. "The two are independent, so they cannot both happen" contradicts itself.

*Multiplying probabilities of dependent events.* $P(A)\,P(B)$ is a valid answer to "what is the chance of both" only when the second event does not know the first happened. Consecutive sweep entries, a reweigh on the same scale, a second inspection by the same examiner, tomorrow's weather: each of these carries the first event's information into the second. The general multiplication rule, $P(A)\,P(B \mid A)$, is always available and always correct. Independence is a shortcut you must earn.

*Reading a sample's dependence as the population's.* $P(A \mid B) \ne P(A)$ **in this file** is a description of the file. Whether owner class and loss are associated on the Lane is an inference, and it needs a null model, a test statistic and a sampling distribution. That is Act VIII. Write *appear*, write *in this Ledger*, and let the next Act earn the stronger verb.

*Reading dependence as a mechanism.* Even inside the file, a failed independence check says the two variables move together. It does not say that being Perrine-flagged is what loses a hull. Route, cargo, drive model, age and destination all ride along with owner class, and the table cannot separate them.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
