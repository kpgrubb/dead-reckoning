<Briefing title="How long until the first one" topics={["4.12"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A **geometric** setting is the binomial's four conditions with one of them removed and the question turned round.

- **Binary.** Each trial is a success or it is not. A passage diverts or it does not.
- **Independent.** One trial's result changes nothing about the next.
- **Same probability** $p$ on every trial.
- **No fixed number of trials.** The counting runs until the first success and then stops. $n$ is not given; $n$ is the answer.

That last line is the whole distinction. In a binomial the trials are settled in advance and the count of successes is random. In a geometric the successes are settled at one, the first, and the *number of trials* is random. Same process, same $p$, adjacent questions.

**The convention, stated once and used everywhere.** $X$ is the number of trials **up to and including** the first success. The support is 1, 2, 3, … There is no trial 0, and $P(X = 1) = p$ exactly, because a wait of one trial is a success on the first attempt. Some books count the *failures before* the first success and get a support starting at 0 and a mean of $(1-p)/p$. That is a different variable with a different mean, and it is not the one AP or your calculator uses.

To wait exactly $k$ trials is to fail $k-1$ times and then succeed. Independent, so they multiply:

$$P(X = k) = (1-p)^{k-1}\,p$$

There is no binomial coefficient here. "First success on trial $k$" names one specific sequence of outcomes; there is nothing to arrange.

**The tail is where a plan lives.** "Still waiting after $k$ trials" is the single event *every one of those $k$ trials failed*, so it needs no summation at all:

$$P(X > k) = (1-p)^{k}$$

That is the survival form, and it is the number a window length is chosen against. It never needs $1 - P(X \le k)$ assembled from a pile of cells, and it is not $P(X \ge k)$, which keeps the cell at $k$ and answers a different question.

**The mean, and the trap inside it.** The expected wait is

$$\mu = \frac{1}{p} \qquad \sigma = \frac{\sqrt{1-p}}{p}$$

Two things about that pair. First, for small $p$ the standard deviation is very nearly the mean itself: a wait whose spread is the size of its own average is a wait you cannot forecast. Second, and this is the one that gets written into plans, **the mean is not the most likely value.** Every term of the pmf is the one before it times $(1-p)$, so the distribution is *strictly decreasing* for every $p$ there is. The single most likely wait is always **one trial**. The mean $1/p$ sits far out along a long thin tail, and that tail is what drags the average to the right. Most waits are shorter than the mean; for small $p$, the share still running past it settles near $(1-p)^{1/p} \to e^{-1} \approx 0.37$.

**And the mean is a reciprocal, which is why a guess at $p$ is expensive.** Halve $p$ and you double the wait. A rate known only to lie between one in fifty and one in ten does not give a wait known to within a few passages; it gives a wait known to within a factor of five. The remedy is to report that sensitivity in place of a single number, and to choose a window against the tail rather than against the average.
</Briefing.Intuition>

</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"The mean $1/p$ is the most likely wait."* It is not, and it never is. A geometric pmf falls from trial 1 onward, each term being the previous one times $(1-p)$, so the **mode is trial 1 for every $p$**, and the mean sits far to its right, pulled out by a thin tail of very long waits. At the twelve-month rate {fmtPct(lastYearByMean, 0)} of waits are finished before the mean is reached. A plan that budgets for $1/p$ has budgeted for something that happens less than half the time, and has also budgeted for a wait it will usually beat.

*Indexing the pmf from 0.* $X$ counts trials **up to and including** the first success. The support is 1, 2, 3, … and $P(X = 1) = p$. Counting the failures *before* the first success is a legitimate variable, though not this one: its support starts at 0, its mean is $(1-p)/p$, and mixing the two shifts every answer by exactly one trial. Write the exponent as $k-1$ and the convention takes care of itself.

*Answering the binomial question well.* "How many diversions in the next twenty passages" and "how many passages until the first diversion" are different questions about one process. If $n$ is handed to you and the count is unknown, it is binomial; if the count is fixed at one and $n$ is what you are being asked for, it is geometric. They agree on exactly one thing, and nowhere else: at least one success in $n$ trials is the same event as the first success arriving by trial $n$.

*Building a tail out of cells.* $P(X > k)$ is $(1-p)^{k}$ in one line, because it is one event. Summing $k$ pmf terms and subtracting gets the same answer more slowly and with more places to drop a boundary. And $P(X \ge k)$ differs from $P(X > k)$ by the whole cell at $k$.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

  concept="When independent trials share a success probability p and the counting runs until the first success rather than over a fixed number of trials, X, the number of trials up to and including that success, is geometric: P(X = k) = (1 − p)^(k−1)p on the support 1, 2, 3, …, and the probability of still waiting after k trials is the single power (1 − p)^k.
