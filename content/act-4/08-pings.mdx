<Briefing title="A fixed number of independent trials" topics={["4.10"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A **binomial** setting is four conditions, and they are worth learning as four words, because the model dies the moment one of them fails.

- **B**inary. Each trial lands in one of two buckets: seen or not seen, queried or not queried. No third outcome, no "partial contact".
- **I**ndependent. One trial's result does not change the chance on the next. This is earned from the mechanism, never from the feel of the data.
- **N**umber fixed. $n$ is settled *before* the counting starts. If the count of trials is the thing you are recording, as in sweeps *until* a contact, that is a different distribution.
- **S**ame probability. One $p$, holding on every trial. Three loiter points with three per-pass probabilities are three settings, not one.

Then $X$, the number of successes, has

$$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$$

and the shape of that formula is worth reading rather than memorising. $p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of **one particular arrangement**: these $k$ trials succeed and those $n-k$ do not. Because the trials are independent and share a $p$, every arrangement with $k$ successes is worth exactly the same, so the answer is that one value multiplied by $\binom{n}{k}$, the number of arrangements there are. Drop the binomial coefficient and you have answered a question about one specific set of passes rather than about *some* $k$ of them.

**Independence here is a claim about mechanism, and it has to be earned.** Two hulls whose tracks were filed months apart by two offices that have never heard of this ship are independent because no mechanism joins them. Two consecutive entries in *Nightjar*'s own sweep log are not, because the ship's doctrine sends the aperture back to a sector that just produced a contact. The second sweep exists *because* of the first. Same arithmetic, two settings, and only one of them may be multiplied out. The difference is physical, and it is the whole reason the close-pass count has a binomial and the sweep count does not.

**Cumulative probabilities, and the cell on the boundary.** Most real questions are not about one count but about a range, and a range is a sum of cells:

$$P(X \le k) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} P(X = i)$$

On a discrete count, "at most $k$" and "fewer than $k$" differ by the entire cell at $k$: a real quantity of probability, not a rounding error. The same distinction runs up the other end. "At least $k$" keeps the cell at $k$; "more than $k$" throws it away. Write the inequality in symbols before you compute, every time. Upper tails are best taken as $P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k-1)$, the complement of everything strictly below the boundary. That form keeps the boundary cell where it belongs.

**Arrivals as a binomial.** Traffic does not arrive in trials; it arrives in continuous time. Cut the window into hours, though, and each hour is very nearly a trial: either a Perrine hull passes in it or one does not, the rate is the same hour to hour, the number of hours is fixed by the plan, and one hour's traffic does not schedule the next hour's. It is a model laid over a process, and it is a good one while $p$ per hour stays small, which at {fmt(ARRIVALS_PER_DAY.Perrine, 2)} arrivals a day it does.

**The 10% condition.** When the trials are *draws without replacement* from a finite file, say a hundred and forty certifications pulled from an archive, the trials are not strictly independent at all, because every draw changes what is left. The binomial survives it when the sample is no more than a tenth of the population: $n \le 0.10N$. Under that limit the probability drifts too little between draws to matter. Over it, the model misstates how far the count wanders, and the honest answer is that a binomial does not apply. The condition is a ratio; a sample size means nothing until you have named what it is a tenth of.
</Briefing.Intuition>

**None at all**, which is the count the plan is really written against. Zero successes is a single arrangement, so there is no coefficient to apply, only the probability that a pass misses her, raised to the number of passes:

</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Multiplying without checking independence.* The binomial's whole licence is that the trials do not talk to each other. *Nightjar*'s consecutive sweep entries fail that test, because a contact is what sends the aperture back, and a binomial laid over them makes runs look far rarer than they are. Before the formula, name the mechanism that would connect two trials, and then say why it is absent. "They seem unrelated" is not that sentence.

*Sampling without replacement, with no 10% check.* Pulling records from a file is not a sequence of independent trials: each draw changes the file. The binomial is adequate anyway when $n \le 0.10N$, and not otherwise. Quoting a sample size without naming the population it came out of leaves the condition unchecked: 140 draws are fine from an archive of 2,800 and not fine from an archive of 400.

*Reading $P(X \le k)$ for $P(X < k)$.* On a count these differ by the whole cell at $k$. "At most", "no more than" and "up to and including" keep it; "fewer than", "under" and "less than" throw it away. The same trap runs upward: "at least $k$" keeps the cell, "more than $k$" does not, which is why an upper tail is taken as $1 - P(X \le k-1)$ and not $1 - P(X \le k)$.

*Quoting the expected count as a probability.* $np$ is an average over many repetitions of the window. It is not the chance of seeing one. A window with an expected {fmt(dayMoments.mean, 2)} arrivals a day carries a {fmt(dayAtLeastOne, 4)} probability of at least one. Only one of those belongs in a risk column.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
