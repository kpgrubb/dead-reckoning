The wings came in at 105/12:36 and the ship has been quiet for an hour, which is the only kind of quiet anyone aboard trusts.

Twenty-three days. {PURGE_COUNT} purges, {fmt(PURGE_HOURS_TOTAL, 1)} hours of them with the radiators out and the ship a lamp to anything with a naval-grade aperture inside half an astronomical unit. {fmt(windowHours, 0)} hours on Watch across two windows. One hull that came up the Lane on profile, began to fade, and turned eleven degrees while it was still braking.

Sandoval has the heat plan on the wardroom table, face up, which is where she put it and not where you left it. Beside it, in Ebele's hand, the annex: the close-pass count for each window, the wait, the cluster study, the two baselines, the six subsystem loads with their spreads. Every page of it is arithmetic somebody did on this ship in the last three weeks.

The report goes at MET 106. Solberg has the slot.

</Scene>

<Dialogue speaker="engineer" aside="One hand flat on the plan. She does not slide it across.">
Every number in this goes on the record, Skipper, and the record is what the Chief works to. I am not asking you to defend the plan. The plan is done; we flew it. I am asking you to defend the arithmetic, because that is the part they can take apart from a room at Valhalla with no cellar gauge in front of them.
</Dialogue>

<Dialogue speaker="engineer">
Twelve things. How the Ensign's simulation was built and what its number means. The complement, because that is where he put a probability above one. Both directions of a conditional and which one the Board quoted. Whether two events are independent and which check says so. A distribution that sums to one, what it averages, and how far it spreads. Variances. The binomial on the passes and the binomial on the count. And the wait, which is the one I care about, because the wait is what I am buying with lithium.
</Dialogue>

<Dialogue speaker="xo" aside="Standing where she can read it the right way up. She has already read it.">
Assuming the annex is complete — and it is, I checked it against the log — the annex is the part that survives. The finding is one ship. The method is what makes the next nineteen legible. Where you are asked for a sentence, write the one you would sign.
</Dialogue>

*At MET 104/03:50 this ship observed MV* Marius Regio *(Perrine flag, Adlinda Mk 3) at Mark 9.4, on profile, transponder degrading. Four paragraphs. The annex runs to eleven displays and every one of them carries the denominator it was computed against.

*The Board acknowledges the commanding officer's report of MET 106. </Scene>

<Dialogue speaker="analyst" aside="He has the annex open and is turning pages he wrote.">
So — sir. They didn't say the arithmetic is wrong. Not one line of it. They said one is one.
</Dialogue>

<Dialogue speaker="xo">
Because one is one. One ship is an anecdote, Ensign. You witnessed a hull turn eleven degrees and you can put a number on how long you waited for it and how likely it was that nobody saw you doing the waiting. You cannot put a number on whether it was the only one.
</Dialogue>

<Scene stamp="MET 107/09:40" location="Bridge" condensed="The turn: the Board's rebuttal is not about this hull but about nineteen. To answer it you need to know what nineteen honest hulls would look like — the noise, before the signal. Ceres is 0.4 AU away and the Board's own comparison table is aboard.">

She is right, and the thing that makes her right is the thing you have spent three weeks building.

You know what one hull turning costs in lithium. You know the odds a freighter's nav sensors caught you while it happened, pass by pass. You know how long you should have expected to wait and how badly that expectation moves when the rate moves. Every one of those is a probability about *this* ship, in *this* window, and the Board has just declined every one of them by pointing at a denominator of one.

The Register says thirty-one hulls in six years. Nineteen of them are Perrine. All nineteen faded gradually and every one of them was coded *unknown* by the same office. You have the tightest cluster of loss dates in the file and a simulation that says chance produces it about twice in a hundred years of that kind.

What you do not have is the shape of ordinary. If nineteen hulls were late, or lost, or gradual, or Perrine, purely because nineteen hulls have to be *something* — what would that look like? How far from the middle does a batch of nineteen wander when nothing at all is wrong with it?

Ferrier says it the way she says everything, which is once.

</Scene>

<Dialogue speaker="xo">
We have been computing probabilities of events. The Board is going to answer with a statistic — a rate, a mean, a count out of a corridor — and statistics have distributions of their own. Until we know what the noise looks like, we cannot tell them we have heard a signal. Ceres is fifteen days at fifteen milligee and the fuel state is adequate. I would like to be there with that work already done.
</Dialogue>
