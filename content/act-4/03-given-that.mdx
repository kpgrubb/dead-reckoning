<Briefing title="Given that" topics={["4.5"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A conditional probability is not a new kind of number. It is the same counting done inside a smaller room.

$P(A \mid B)$ — "the probability of $A$ **given** $B$" — says: throw away every individual that is not $B$, and *then* ask what share are $A$. The word *given* names the new denominator. That is the whole definition, and almost every error in this topic is an error about the denominator.

Three probabilities live in any two-way table and they are told apart by what they divide by:

- **Joint** $P(A \cap B)$ — one cell over the **grand total**. What share of everybody is both things at once.
- **Marginal** $P(B)$ — one row or column total over the **grand total**. One variable; the other ignored.
- **Conditional** $P(A \mid B)$ — one cell over **that row or column's total**. The only one that changes the denominator.

Rearranging the definition gives the **general multiplication rule**, and it is how a tree is read: a path's probability is the first branch times the conditional on the branch that follows it. Not the first branch times the *overall* second-stage probability — the one sitting on that branch. Multiply along a path; add down a column of paths.

Now the move this module exists for.

**Reversing a conditioning.** You are given $P(A \mid B)$ and you want $P(B \mid A)$. These share a numerator, $P(A \cap B)$, and nothing else, and there is no arithmetic that turns one into the other on its own — you need the base rates. Build the tree forwards, multiply out the joints, add the joints that end in $A$ to get $P(A)$, and divide the one you care about by that new total.

The reason this matters more than it looks is that both numbers are often **true**, and a reader cannot tell from the sentence which one they have been handed. "Of the certifications Voss signed, 19% were queried" and "of the queried certifications, 19% were signed by Voss" are different claims about the same file, and the passive voice makes them look identical. Reported to the wrong question, a true number is worse than a false one, because it survives every check a careful reader can make.

That swap has a name: the **prosecutor's fallacy** — quoting $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{innocent})$ as though it were $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{evidence})$. The gap between the two is the base rate, and when the base rate is small the two numbers are not close, they are not even the same order of magnitude.

So the discipline is small and absolute: **before you quote a conditional, say out loud what you divided by.** If the sentence does not make its denominator visible, rewrite the sentence.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
The tree the ship is built around, and not the one the Board wrote about — that one is yours, below.

**Stage one, us.** Over the whole loiter the wings are out for {fmt(PURGE_HOURS, 1)} hours per purge, which works out at a duty cycle of {fmt(pPurging, 4)}. **Stage two, them.** Given cold, the pass detects her with probability {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_COLD, 2)}. Given purging, {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_PURGING, 2)}. **Multiply along the paths.** The general multiplication rule, four times:

Two numbers, two denominators, one tree.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*P(A | B) read as P(B | A) — the prosecutor's fallacy.* The two share a numerator and nothing else. "Given purging, seen {fmtPct(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_PURGING, 0)} of the time" and "given seen, purging {fmtPct(pPurgingGivenSeen, 0)} of the time" are both true here and they are about different populations of close passes. No amount of care with the arithmetic protects you; the error happens in the sentence, before the arithmetic starts.

*Conditioning on the wrong total.* A cell over the grand total is a **joint** probability; a cell over its row total is a **conditional**. Both are true statements about the table, both are quotable, and only one of them answers a question that began with the word *given*. When a figure looks too small, check whether you have divided by everybody instead of by the group.

*Treating a true number as an honest answer.* A staff paper that reports the conditional which is easy to compute, when the reader's question is the other one, has made no arithmetic error at all — which is precisely why the error survives review. The defence is to compute both directions and print both, every time, with their denominators attached.

*Branches that do not sum to one.* Every stage of a tree is a sample space: its branches must total 1, conditional on where they hang. If they do not, the joints below them are not probabilities of anything, and no amount of correct multiplication downstream will repair it.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
