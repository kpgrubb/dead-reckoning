<Briefing title="Given that" topics={["4.5"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A conditional probability is the same counting done inside a smaller room.

$P(A \mid B)$, read "the probability of $A$ **given** $B$", says: throw away every individual that is not $B$, and *then* ask what share of what is left are $A$. The word *given* names the new denominator. Almost every error in this topic is an error about the denominator.

Three probabilities live in any two-way table, and they are told apart by what they divide by:

- **Joint** $P(A \cap B)$: one cell over the **grand total**. What share of everybody is both things at once.
- **Marginal** $P(B)$: one row or column total over the **grand total**. One variable; the other ignored.
- **Conditional** $P(A \mid B)$: one cell over **that row or column's total**. The only one that changes the denominator.

Rearranging the definition gives the **general multiplication rule**, and that rule is how a tree is read. A path's probability is the first branch times the conditional sitting on the branch that follows it, which is a different figure from the overall second-stage probability. Multiply along a path. Add down a column of paths.

Now the reversal, which is the move this topic exists for. You are given $P(A \mid B)$ and you want $P(B \mid A)$. They share a numerator, $P(A \cap B)$, and nothing else, and no arithmetic turns one into the other on its own; you need the base rates. Build the tree forwards, multiply out the joints, add the joints that end in $A$ to get $P(A)$, then divide the one you care about by that new total.

This matters more than it looks because both numbers are usually **true**, and a reader cannot tell from the sentence which one they have been handed. "Of the certifications Voss signed, 19% were queried" and "of the queried certifications, 19% were signed by Voss" are different claims about the same file, and the passive voice makes them look identical. Reported against the wrong question, a true number survives every check a careful reader can make.

That swap has a name: the **prosecutor's fallacy**, quoting $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{innocent})$ as though it were $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{evidence})$. The gap between the two is the base rate, and when the base rate is small the two numbers are not the same order of magnitude.

So the discipline is small and absolute: **before you quote a conditional, say what you divided by.** If the sentence does not make its denominator visible, rewrite the sentence.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
The being-seen tree, worked end to end. The Register's fade table is below, and that one is yours to build.

**Stage one, us.** Over the whole loiter the wings are out for {fmt(PURGE_HOURS, 1)} hours per purge, a duty cycle of {fmt(pPurging, 4)}. So a close pass drawn at random from the loiter finds *Nightjar* purging with probability {fmt(pPurging, 4)} and cold with probability {fmt(pCold, 4)}.

**Stage two, them.** Given cold, the pass detects her with probability {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_COLD, 2)}. Given purging, {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_PURGING, 2)}. Each of those is a probability inside its own branch, conditional on the stage above it.

**Multiply along the paths.** The general multiplication rule, four times:

**Add down the column** to get the marginal. P(a random close pass detects her) = {fmt(jointColdSeen, 4)} + {fmt(jointPurgeSeen, 4)} = **{fmt(pSeen, 4)}**, about {oneIn(pSeen)}.

**Now reverse it.** A pass *did* return a contact. Given that, was she purging?

<Formula
  tex={`P(\\text{purging} \\mid \\text{seen}) = \\frac{P(\\text{purging} \\cap \\text{seen})}{P(\\text{seen})} = \\frac{${fmt(jointPurgeSeen, 4)}}{${fmt(pSeen, 4)}} = ${fmt(pPurgingGivenSeen, 4)}`}
  label="The numerator is one leaf; the denominator is the column that leaf sits in"
/>

The wings are out **{fmtPct(pPurging, 1)}** of the loiter, and they account for **{fmtPct(pPurgingGivenSeen, 1)}** of every detection.

<ProbabilityBar value={pPurging} label="share of the loiter spent purging" complementLabel="cold" />
<ProbabilityBar value={pPurgingGivenSeen} label="share of detections that happen while purging" complementLabel="detected while cold" />

Note what did *not* happen. {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_PURGING, 2)} did not turn into {fmt(pPurgingGivenSeen, 4)} by any operation on {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_PURGING, 2)} alone. P(seen | purging) is a property of the sensor. P(purging | seen) is a property of the sensor **and the timetable together**, and it moves whenever the purge schedule changes while the sensor stays exactly as it was.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*P(A | B) read as P(B | A): the prosecutor's fallacy.* The two share a numerator and nothing else. "Given purging, seen {fmtPct(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_PURGING, 0)} of the time" and "given seen, purging {fmtPct(pPurgingGivenSeen, 0)} of the time" are both true here, and they are about different populations of close passes. The error happens in the sentence, before the arithmetic starts, so no amount of care with the arithmetic protects you.

*Conditioning on the wrong total.* A cell over the grand total is a **joint** probability. A cell over its row total is a **conditional**. Both are true statements about the table, both are quotable, and only one of them answers a question that began with the word *given*. When a figure looks too small, check whether you have divided by everybody instead of by the group.

*Treating a true number as an honest answer.* A staff paper that reports the conditional which is easy to compute, when the reader's question is the other one, has made no arithmetic error at all, which is precisely why the error survives review. The defence is to compute both directions and print both, every time, with their denominators attached.

*Branches that do not sum to one.* Every stage of a tree is a sample space: its branches must total 1, conditional on where they hang. If they do not, the joints below them are not probabilities of anything, and no correct multiplication downstream will repair it.
</Briefing.Caution>
