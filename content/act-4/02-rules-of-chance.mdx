<Briefing title="The sample space, and the two rules that live inside it" topics={["4.3", "4.4"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Everything in this module is bookkeeping on one picture: a unit bar.

**The sample space** is every result a single repetition of a process can produce, listed so that exactly one of them happens every time. Two properties make it a sample space, and both are needed: the results are **mutually exclusive** (no trial lands in two) and **exhaustive** (no trial lands outside all of them). Then, and only then, the areas of the bar sum to 1 and arithmetic on it is safe. An **event** is any collection of those results: a region of the bar. A probability is the area, and it is a long-run relative frequency, meaning the share of trials landing there as the number of trials grows without bound.

**The complement rule** is the cheapest tool in the subject. The bar is one unit wide, so whatever is not in $A$ is the rest of it. When an event has many pieces and its opposite has one, do the one.

**The addition rule** and the trap under it. If $A$ and $B$ share no part of the bar, the area of "A or B" is the two areas added, full stop. If they overlap, adding them counts the shared region twice, and the general rule subtracts it back out once. You can catch the error without looking at the table: a bare sum that lands above 1 is not a probability, and a sum that lands below 1 can still be wrong by exactly the overlap.

Now the distinction that goes wrong most often.

> **Mutually exclusive and independent are different properties.** *Mutually exclusive* says the two events cannot both happen on one trial: the regions do not touch. *Independent* says that knowing one happened does not change the probability of the other. Two events with positive probability that are mutually exclusive are about as **dependent** as events get, because learning that $A$ happened tells you $B$ definitely did not. Mutually exclusive is not independent.

One last idea, and the rest of the topic rests on it: **events across several trials do not add**. {fmtInt(SWEEP_SECTORS)} sweeps are {fmtInt(SWEEP_SECTORS)} separate bars, one per trial. Probabilities add along a *partition of one trial*. Across trials, the "at least one" question is answered by its complement: the only way for "at least one" to fail is for **every** trial to miss, and that is a single event on a single line.

Watch how the two expressions behave as $k$ grows. $k \cdot p$ climbs past 1 and keeps going, which no probability can do. $1 - (1-p)^k$ rises toward 1 and never reaches it.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
**One trial: the four outcomes of a sweep.** The log's own relative frequencies, over {fmtInt(SWEEP_COUNT)} entries.

<KeyTable
  columns={['outcome of one sweep', 'entries', 'relative frequency']}
  rows={[...SWEEP_OUTCOMES.map((o) => [o, fmtInt(sweepOutcomeCounts[o]), fmt(sweepOutcomeProbs[o], 4)]), ['total', fmtInt(SWEEP_COUNT), fmt(SWEEP_OUTCOMES.reduce((s, o) => s + sweepOutcomeProbs[o], 0), 3)]]}
  emphasisRow={SWEEP_OUTCOMES.length}
  caption="Four mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes. The relative frequencies sum to 1 because every entry closes exactly one way."
  ariaLabel="Sweep outcome sample space with counts and relative frequencies"
/>

Exclusive outcomes, so combinations of them add with no correction. P(the sweep resolved by RF *or* by thermal) = {fmt(sweepOutcomeProbs.RF, 4)} + {fmt(sweepOutcomeProbs.thermal, 4)} = **{fmt(sweepOutcomeProbs.RF + sweepOutcomeProbs.thermal, 4)}**. And the complement of a big event is a small one: P(*not* thermal) = 1 − {fmt(sweepOutcomeProbs.thermal, 4)} = {fmt(1 - sweepOutcomeProbs.thermal, 4)}, which is the other three columns added.

**Two variables on one trial: the general addition rule.** Restrict to the {fmtInt(detections.length)} entries that resolved to something, and cross the range band against the mode that resolved it. Now the two events *can* happen together.

<KeyTable
  columns={['range band', ...MODES, 'total']}
  rows={[...BANDS.map((b, i) => [b, ...bandModeCounts[i].map((c) => fmtInt(c)), fmtInt(bandMode.rowTotals[i])]), ['total', ...bandMode.colTotals.map((c) => fmtInt(c)), fmtInt(bandMode.total)]]}
  emphasisRow={BANDS.length}
  caption={`The ${detections.length} detections by range band and resolving mode.`}
  ariaLabel="Two-way table of range band against resolving mode among the detections"
/>

Draw one detection at random. P(near) = {fmtInt(bandMode.rowTotals[0])}/{fmtInt(bandMode.total)} = {fmt(pNear, 4)}. P(thermal) = {fmtInt(bandMode.colTotals[0])}/{fmtInt(bandMode.total)} = {fmt(pThermal, 4)}. Those are **marginal** probabilities: one variable, the other ignored, denominator the grand total. The cell where they meet, near *and* thermal, is a **joint** probability: {fmtInt(bandModeCounts[0][0])}/{fmtInt(bandMode.total)} = {fmt(pNearAndThermal, 4)}.

Add the two margins and you get {fmt(naiveNearThermal, 4)}, because the {fmtInt(bandModeCounts[0][0])} detections that are both got counted in each. Subtract them once:

<Formula
  tex={`P(\\text{near} \\cup \\text{thermal}) = \\frac{${bandMode.rowTotals[0]}}{${bandMode.total}} + \\frac{${bandMode.colTotals[0]}}{${bandMode.total}} - \\frac{${bandModeCounts[0][0]}}{${bandMode.total}} = \\frac{${bandMode.rowTotals[0] + bandMode.colTotals[0] - bandModeCounts[0][0]}}{${bandMode.total}} = ${fmt(pNearOrThermal, 4)}`}
  label="The general addition rule, with the overlap taken back out"
/>

**Several trials: the complement.** The loiter point has {fmtInt(coldPasses)} scheduled close passes inside {fmt(CLOSE_PASS_KM / 1e6, 1)} million kilometres during a Watch window. Each passing hull's navigation suite picks up a cold *Nightjar* with probability {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_COLD, 2)}, and the passes are different hulls on scheduled tracks.

The only way the window passes clean is for every pass to miss: {fmt(1 - P_SEEN_PER_PASS_COLD, 2)} to the power {fmtInt(coldPasses)} = {fmt(pNoneCold, 4)}. So P(at least one pass sees us) = 1 − {fmt(pNoneCold, 4)} = **{fmt(pSeenCold, 4)}**, or about {oneIn(pSeenCold)}. Adding the passes instead gives {fmtInt(coldPasses)} × {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_COLD, 2)} = {fmt(naiveCold, 4)}: close enough to look right, and wrong in the direction that flatters the plan. That sum leaves the sample space altogether at {fmtInt(coldCrossesAt)} passes.

Raise the per-pass probability and the error stops being subtle. During a purge it is {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_PURGING, 2)}. Over {fmtInt(purgePasses)} passes in {fmt(PURGE_HOURS, 1)} hours, both miss with probability {fmt(pNonePurging, 4)}, so at least one sees us with probability **{fmt(pSeenPurging, 4)}**. Added instead, {fmtInt(purgePasses)} × {fmt(P_SEEN_PER_PASS_PURGING, 2)} = {fmt(naivePurging, 2)}. The sum is already above 1 after {fmtInt(purgePasses)} trials.

<ProbabilityBar value={pSeenCold} label={`at least one of ${fmtInt(coldPasses)} cold passes detects us`} complementLabel="the window passes clean" />
<ProbabilityBar value={pSeenPurging} label={`at least one of ${fmtInt(purgePasses)} passes detects us while purging`} complementLabel="both miss" />
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"Mutually exclusive" and "independent", swapped.* They answer different questions. Exclusive is about whether the two events can occur **together** on one trial; independent is about whether one **informs** the other. Two exclusive events with positive probability are strongly dependent, because learning that one happened settles the other completely. The addition rule cares about exclusivity. The multiplication rule cares about independence. Bring the wrong one and the arithmetic will not complain.

*P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B), always.* It is true only when the two events cannot happen together. On the four outcomes of a single sweep they cannot, so the addition is free, which is why the habit carries so easily into a table where they can. The general rule is the real rule; the special case is the general rule with a zero in it.

*Adding probabilities across trials.* {fmtInt(SWEEP_SECTORS)} sweeps are {fmtInt(SWEEP_SECTORS)} trials, each with its own bar. The bare sum double-counts every window in which two or more of them find something, and past {fmtInt(ringCrossesAt)} sweeps it returns a number above 1. That is the cheapest error check in the subject. Compute "none", then take the complement.

*Subtracting the count instead of the probability.* "1 − {fmtInt(sweepOutcomeCounts.thermal)}" is not a probability and never was. The complement rule subtracts from one whole sample space, and the sample space is 1, not {fmtInt(SWEEP_COUNT)}.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
