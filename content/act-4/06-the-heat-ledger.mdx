<Briefing title="What a window costs when you fly enough of them" topics={["4.8"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A distribution has a centre of mass, and for a random variable it has a name: the **expected value**, $E(X)$, also written $\mu_X$. It is the average of the values *weighted by their probabilities* — which is the only kind of average a distribution can have, because its values are not equally common.

$$\mu_X = E(X) = \sum_x x\cdot p(x)$$

Read it as a bookkeeping instruction. Take each cost the window might run to, multiply it by the share of windows that run to it, and add the column. The weights sum to 1, which is what makes the result an average and not merely a sum. The data mean you computed in Act I is the same operation with every weight equal to $1/n$: in a sample, every observation is one observation, so they all weigh the same. In a distribution they do not.

**What "expected" promises.** Exactly one thing: fly this window many times and the cost per window settles on $\mu_X$. That is the *long-run average*, and it is what a budget is made of — twenty windows at $\mu_X$ each is what the lithium has to cover.

**What it does not promise.** Anything whatever about the next window. Three things follow, and each one has ended a plan:

- $\mu_X$ need not be a value $X$ can take. A table in two-hour cells can have an expected value that falls between two cells, and then *no* window costs the expected amount.
- $\mu_X$ is not the **most likely** outcome. The most likely outcome is the tallest bar — the mode. On a symmetric table they land in the same place and the distinction looks pedantic; on a skewed one they are nowhere near each other, and reading one for the other misprices everything downstream.
- $\mu_X$ says nothing about the *spread*, and spread is what saturates a cellar.

So the second number. The **standard deviation of a random variable** is weighted exactly as the mean is: every squared distance from $\mu$ counts for as much probability as sits on it.

$$\sigma_X^2 = \sum_x (x-\mu_X)^2\,p(x), \qquad \sigma_X = \sqrt{\sigma_X^2}$$

Square, weight, add, and take the root **once, at the end**. The probabilities are inside the sum, not outside it. Take the spread of the numbers in the left-hand column instead — as if every cost on the axis were equally common — and you have computed the spread of the axis, which is a property of how you drew the table and not of anything the ship does.

**And the third number, which is the one a decision actually rides on.** A capacity is a line drawn across the axis, and what matters is how much of the distribution lies past it:

$$P(X > c) = \sum_{x > c} p(x)$$

This is not derivable from $\mu_X$. Two plans can have the same expected cost and tails that differ by a factor of forty, because a tail is made out of width as well as centre. A wide, cheap plan and a tight, dear plan will rank one way on the mean and the other way on the tail — and when they do, the ranking you use is a command decision, not an arithmetic one.

Expected value ranks the cost. The tail ranks the risk. Neither of them ranks the *mission*, which is the third column on the plot and belongs to nobody but the officer who signs for it.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Caution>
*"The expected value is the most likely outcome."* It is not, and on this ship it is frequently not even a possible one. The mode is the tallest bar; the mean is the balance point of the whole table. They coincide only when the distribution is symmetric, which a cost distribution with a hard floor and a long tail never is. An officer who plans to the mode buys the commonest window and is surprised by the bill; an officer who reads the mean as a forecast is surprised by the *next* window, which was never promised to him.

*Computing the SD of a random variable by ignoring the probabilities.* The spread of the values in the left-hand column — {fmt(farFlatSd, 2)} hours on the far point's table — describes the axis you drew, not the windows you will fly. Every squared deviation belongs inside the sum with its own $p(x)$ against it, which is why the weighted answer here is {fmt(farSd, 2)}. The tell is that the unweighted figure does not change when you change the probabilities, and a distribution that does not notice its own probabilities is not a distribution.

*Ranking two plans on the mean alone.* The mean is the right answer to "what does this cost over many windows" and no answer at all to "what are the odds this one saturates". The two rankings can disagree, and when they do the disagreement is the whole decision: cheapest is not safest, and safest is not most useful.

*Reading the standard deviation as a bound.* $\sigma_X$ is a typical distance from the mean, not a fence. Windows land outside it routinely — that is what the tail was for.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
