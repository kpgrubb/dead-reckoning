<Briefing title="Variances add; standard deviations do not" topics={["4.9"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Three rules, one idea underneath them: **spread lives in the variance, and only the variance behaves arithmetically.**

**One variable, rescaled.** Multiply a random variable by a constant and add another — $Y = aX + b$, which is every unit conversion there is:

$$\mu_{aX+b} = a\mu_X + b, \qquad \sigma_{aX+b} = |a|\,\sigma_X, \qquad \sigma^2_{aX+b} = a^2\sigma^2_X$$

The offset $b$ shifts every value by the same amount, so no distance inside the distribution changes and the spread is untouched. The multiplier scales distances, so it lands on the standard deviation **once** — in absolute value, because a spread has no sign — and inside the variance **twice**, because the variance is built out of squares. Kilowatts into percent-of-cellar per hour, hours into days, celsius into kelvin: the mean goes through the whole conversion, the SD goes through only the multiplying part of it.

**Two variables, added.** For **independent** $X$ and $Y$:

$$\mu_{X+Y} = \mu_X + \mu_Y, \qquad \sigma^2_{X+Y} = \sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_Y, \qquad \sigma_{X+Y} = \sqrt{\sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_Y}$$

Means add, always, independent or not. Variances add *only* under independence, and standard deviations do not add at all. Adding the SDs asks for the spread of a total in which both parts run high together every single time; independence is precisely the statement that they do not. Most of the time one is above its mean while the other is below, and the two partly cancel — so the total's spread is wider than either part and narrower than the two widths laid end to end. The square root is taken once, at the end, after the squares have been added.

**Two variables, subtracted.** Here is the one that costs marks:

$$\mu_{X-Y} = \mu_X - \mu_Y, \qquad \sigma^2_{X-Y} = \sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_Y$$

The mean subtracts. The variance **still adds**. A margin — a rating minus a load, a budget minus a bill, a design figure minus a measurement — is *less* certain than either quantity that made it, never more. Uncertainty in either number makes the gap between them harder to pin down, and a minus sign cannot cancel ignorance. Subtracting the variances is not merely wrong; on many tables it asks for the square root of a negative number, which is the arithmetic telling you what it thinks of the idea.

**Why independence, and what fails without it.** The full rule carries a covariance term:

$$\sigma^2_{X+Y} = \sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_Y + 2\,\text{Cov}(X, Y)$$

Independence sets that term to zero, and the AP rule is the special case. So the rule is not a fact about addition; it is a claim about the hardware. Six loads drawn by scrubbers, processors, a receiver, the skin loops, the pumps and sixteen people are independent because nothing joins them: one running high does not make another run high. Put them all on one thermal loop, or make them all respond to the same cabin temperature, and the covariance is positive, the total's spread is larger than $\sqrt{\sum\sigma_i^2}$, and a margin computed under the rule is too small. **State the assumption out loud every time you use the rule**, because when it fails it fails in the direction that flatters the plan.

**And why a total's spread is the whole of an endurance figure.** A cellar of fixed capacity divided by a load is an endurance. The load is a random variable, so the endurance is one too, and a single endurance number without a spread beside it is not a limit — it is an average with its uncertainty filed off. That is the difference between the design curve's figure, the figure an engineer works to, and the figure she will put in writing: all three are the same distribution, read at three distances from its centre.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Caution>
*Adding the standard deviations.* The commonest error in the topic, and it looks like diligence. $\sum\sigma_i$ is the spread of a total in which every part runs to the same side of its mean at the same instant; for independent parts that is a rare watch, not a typical one. Square first, add second, root last — and the answer always comes out smaller than the sum of the SDs and larger than the biggest one of them. If your total's spread is not between those two bounds, the arithmetic is wrong before you check anything else.

*Subtracting variances for a difference.* $\sigma^2_{X-Y} = \sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_Y$, with a plus sign, for independent $X$ and $Y$. A margin inherits both uncertainties; it cannot be more precisely known than either of the quantities that produced it. The tell is a negative number under the root, but the error is just as fatal when both variances happen to be large enough to hide it.

*Forgetting that the multiplier squares.* $\sigma_{aX+b} = |a|\sigma_X$ and $\sigma^2_{aX+b} = a^2\sigma^2_X$. Using $a$ on the variance understates the spread; using $a^2$ on the SD mangles it the other way. And the offset $b$ appears in neither — a shift moves the centre and no distance inside the distribution, so it can never change a spread.

*Using the rule without earning it.* Variances add **for independent random variables**. Nothing in a table tells you whether the parts are independent; only the hardware, the procedure or the traffic does. Six systems on one thermal loop are not six independent loads, and a margin computed as if they were is too small — in the direction that makes the plan look affordable. Say the assumption out loud, in the report, next to the number.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
