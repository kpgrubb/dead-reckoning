<Briefing title="Where a count sits, how far it wanders, and what counts as far" topics={["4.11"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A binomial count is a sum of $n$ independent trials worth 1 or 0. Means add, and for independent trials variances add too, so a single trial's mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$ scale straight up:

$$\mu_X = np \qquad \sigma_X^2 = np(1-p) \qquad \sigma_X = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$$

Two things about those are worth holding. First, **$np$ is where the distribution sits, not how far it moves.** Quoting the mean as the spread is the commonest error in this topic, and it is a large one: it drops the factor $(1-p)$ and the square root, and on a count of a few thousand trials it misses by an order of magnitude. Second, the mean grows with $n$ while the standard deviation grows only with $\sqrt{n}$. That is why a big file has a count that is large and comparatively steady at the same time, and why "more records" tightens a rate.

**Shape.** The distribution is squashed by whichever boundary is close. A count cannot fall below 0 or rise above $n$, so when $np$ is small the mass piles against zero and the distribution is skewed right; when $n(1-p)$ is small it piles against $n$ and is skewed left; and when both $np$ and $n(1-p)$ are at least about ten, neither boundary is in reach and the pmf has gone near-symmetric about its mean. Raising $n$ at a fixed $p$ pushes both products up and drags the shape toward symmetry. It is a boundary effect rather than a rule to memorise.

**The 10% condition, for a count drawn from a finite file.** The Ledger's {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} transits are records drawn, without replacement, from the Lane's whole traffic over those six years, rather than $n$ tosses of a coin. Draws without replacement are never exactly independent, because every record taken out changes the mix left behind. The binomial survives that when the draw is no more than a tenth of the population it came from:

$$n \le 0.10\,N$$

For {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} transits, that asks for at least {fmtInt(tenPercentNeeds)} transits of the Lane behind the file. The Lane's six-year traffic clears it comfortably, so the binomial is adequate here. The condition bounds the *size of the departure* from independence rather than denying that there is one. And it is a ratio: {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} records mean nothing until the population they were drawn from has been named.

**Surprise is a tail.** The probability of *exactly* the count you observed is always small, because the probability has to be shared among every count the distribution can produce. On the Board's baseline even the likeliest single count carries only {fmt(boardModeCell, 4)}. So $P(X = k)$ cannot tell you whether $k$ is surprising; every $k$ would be.

What can tell you is the weight of everything at or beyond the observed count:

$$P(X \ge x) = 1 - P(X \le x - 1)$$

and, as a first orientation, the standardized distance $(x - np)/\sqrt{np(1-p)}$: how many standard deviations out the count has landed. Two standard deviations is worth a look; four or five is a count the model cannot absorb.

**And the whole of it is conditional on $p$.** A tail is the probability of a count this extreme *given a baseline rate*. It belongs jointly to the count and the rate assumed, and it says nothing about whether that rate is the right one. Change nothing but $p$ and the same $n$, the same observed count and the same two formulas return a different mean, a different spread, and a verdict that points the other way. The method is behaving correctly when it does that. The argument was never about arithmetic. It is about which rate a corridor is entitled to be measured against, and whoever chooses that has chosen the answer before the first multiplication.
</Briefing.Intuition>

Less than half a standard deviation. That is the middle of the distribution.

**The tail against the point.**

<Formula tex={boardTailTex} label="Everything at or beyond 31, against the chance of exactly 31" />

Under the Board's baseline, {fmtPct(board.tail, 1)} of six-year files like this one would carry {fmtInt(LEDGER_LOSSES)} losses or more. That is {oneIn(board.tail)}: utterly ordinary. The point probability, {fmt(boardPoint, 4)}, is smaller and says nothing at all, because the *most likely* count under this baseline, {fmtInt(boardModeK)}, carries only {fmt(boardModeCell, 4)}.

**Shape.** $np$ = {fmt(board.mean, 1)} and $n(1-p)$ = {fmt(LEDGER_TRANSITS * (1 - BASELINES.board.p), 0)}, both far above ten, so the distribution is near-symmetric about its mean. The standardized distance is therefore a fair first reading here, and the tail and the two-standard-deviation band tell the same story.

**The 10% check, stated.** These {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} transits are drawn without replacement from the Lane's six-year traffic, so independence is approximate. It needs at least {fmtInt(tenPercentNeeds)} transits behind the file, and the Lane carries several times that in the period, so the binomial stands.

Everything above holds *under the Board's p*. Now do it under the other one, and watch which numbers move.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"np is the standard deviation."* It is the mean. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$: the second factor and the square root are both doing work, and on {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} transits at the Board's rate the two differ by a factor of more than five. A report that quotes {fmt(board.mean, 1)} as the spread has said the file wanders by an amount that is actually where the file is centred.

*Declaring surprise from $P(X = k)$.* Every individual count in a wide distribution is improbable, including the likeliest one. The probability of exactly {fmtInt(LEDGER_LOSSES)} under the Board's baseline is {fmt(boardPoint, 4)}, and the probability of exactly {fmtInt(boardModeK)} (the mode) is {fmt(boardModeCell, 4)}. Neither number is evidence of anything. Shade the tail: everything at or beyond what you saw.

*Using a binomial on draws without replacement with no 10% check.* Records pulled from a file fall short of independent trials. The model is adequate while $n \le 0.10N$, and the condition cannot be checked until the population is named. A sample size on its own never answers it.

*Reading the tail as a probability about the baseline.* $P(X \ge x)$ is the chance of a count this extreme **given** the rate you assumed. It does not say the rate is correct, and it does not say the corridor is dangerous. Those sentences point in the opposite direction and the arithmetic does not support them.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
