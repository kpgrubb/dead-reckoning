<Briefing title="Where a count sits, how far it wanders, and what counts as far" topics={["4.11"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A binomial count is a sum of $n$ independent trials worth 1 or 0. Means add, and for independent trials variances add too, so a single trial's mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$ scale straight up:

$$\mu_X = np \qquad \sigma_X^2 = np(1-p) \qquad \sigma_X = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$$

Two things about those are worth holding. First, **$np$ is where the distribution sits, not how far it moves.** Quoting the mean as the spread is the single commonest error in this topic, and it is not a small one: it leaves out the factor $(1-p)$ and the square root, and on a count of a few thousand trials it is wrong by an order of magnitude. Second, the mean grows with $n$ while the standard deviation grows only with $\sqrt{n}$ — which is why a big file has a count that is both large and comparatively steady, and why "more records" tightens a rate rather than loosening it.

**Shape.** The distribution is squashed by whichever boundary is close. A count cannot fall below 0 or rise above $n$, so when $np$ is small the mass piles against zero and the distribution is skewed right; when $n(1-p)$ is small it piles against $n$ and is skewed left; and when both $np$ and $n(1-p)$ are at least about ten, neither boundary is in reach and the pmf has gone near-symmetric about its mean. Raising $n$ at a fixed $p$ pushes both products up and drags the shape toward symmetry. It is a boundary effect, not a rule to memorise.

**The 10% condition, for a count drawn from a finite file.** The Ledger's {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} transits are not $n$ tosses of a coin. They are records drawn, without replacement, from the Lane's whole traffic over those six years — and draws without replacement are never exactly independent, because every record taken out changes the mix left behind. The binomial survives that when the draw is no more than a tenth of the population it came from:

$$n \le 0.10\,N$$

For {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} transits, that asks for at least {fmtInt(tenPercentNeeds)} transits of the Lane behind the file — which the Lane's six-year traffic clears comfortably, so the binomial is adequate here. Note what the condition is and is not. It is a statement about the *size of the departure* from independence, not a claim that the departure is absent. And it is a ratio: {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} records mean nothing until the population they were drawn from has been named.

**Surprise is a tail.** Here is the part that ends arguments. The probability of *exactly* the count you observed is always small, because the probability has to be shared among every count the distribution can produce. On the Board's baseline even the likeliest single count carries only {fmt(boardModeCell, 4)}. So $P(X = k)$ cannot tell you whether $k$ is surprising; every $k$ would be.

What can tell you is the weight of everything at or beyond the observed count:

$$P(X \ge x) = 1 - P(X \le x - 1)$$

and, as a first orientation, the standardized distance $(x - np)/\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ — how many standard deviations out the count has landed. Two standard deviations is worth a look; four or five is a count the model cannot absorb.

**And the whole of it is conditional on $p$.** A tail is the probability of a count this extreme *given a baseline rate*. It is not the probability that the baseline is right, and it is not a property the count possesses on its own. Change nothing but $p$ and the same $n$, the same observed count and the same two formulas will return a different mean, a different spread and a different verdict. That is not a flaw in the method. It is the method telling you, honestly, that the argument was never about arithmetic — it was about which rate this corridor is entitled to be measured against, and whoever chooses that has chosen the answer before the first multiplication.
</Briefing.Intuition>

**Shape.** $np$ = {fmt(board.mean, 1)} and $n(1-p)$ = {fmt(LEDGER_TRANSITS * (1 - BASELINES.board.p), 0)}, both far above ten, so the distribution is near-symmetric about its mean — which is why the standardized distance is a fair first reading here, and why the tail and the two-standard-deviation band tell the same story.

**The 10% check, stated.** These {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} transits are drawn without replacement from the Lane's six-year traffic, so independence is approximate. It needs at least {fmtInt(tenPercentNeeds)} transits behind the file, and the Lane carries several times that in the period, so the binomial stands.

</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"np is the standard deviation."* It is the mean. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ — the second factor and the square root are both doing work, and on {fmtInt(LEDGER_TRANSITS)} transits at the Board's rate the two differ by a factor of more than five. A report that quotes {fmt(board.mean, 1)} as the spread has said the file wanders by an amount that is actually where the file is centred.

*Declaring surprise from $P(X = k)$.* Every individual count in a wide distribution is improbable, including the likeliest one. The probability of exactly {fmtInt(LEDGER_LOSSES)} under the Board's baseline is {fmt(boardPoint, 4)}, and the probability of exactly {fmtInt(boardModeK)} — the mode — is {fmt(boardModeCell, 4)}. Neither number is evidence of anything. Shade the tail: everything at or beyond what you saw.

*Using a binomial on draws without replacement with no 10% check.* Records pulled from a file are not independent trials. The model is adequate while $n \le 0.10N$, and the condition cannot be checked until the population is named. A sample size on its own never answers it.

*Reading the tail as a probability about the baseline.* $P(X \ge x)$ is the chance of a count this extreme **given** the rate you assumed. It is not the chance that the rate is correct, and it is not the chance the corridor is dangerous. Those sentences point in the opposite direction and the arithmetic does not support them.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
