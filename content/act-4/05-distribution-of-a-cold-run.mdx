<Briefing title="Mass at points, area over intervals" topics={["4.7"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A **random variable** is a number attached to the outcome of a chance process: hours a crossing takes, degrees on a gauge. Write it $X$; write a particular value it can take $x$. The whole subject from here forward is the behaviour of such numbers.

A **discrete** random variable takes values you can list: 0, 1, 2, 3, or 44, 48, 52. Its **probability distribution** is a table, one row per value, and the table has exactly two obligations:

- every probability is between 0 and 1, and
- they sum to exactly **1**.

The second obligation is the statement that the outcome is *somewhere* in the table. A table that sums to 1.04 has stopped being a distribution, and every probability computed off it (every expected cost, every tail) is wrong by the same factor, in the direction that flatters whoever filed it.

With the table in hand, a probability about a range of values is a **sum of cells** and nothing cleverer:

- $P(X = k)$ is one cell.
- $P(X \le k)$, the **cumulative** probability, is every cell from the bottom up to and including $k$.
- $P(X > k)$ is everything above $k$, most easily got as $1 - P(X \le k)$, which is the complement rule doing the work.

On a discrete variable the boundary value carries a **real quantity of probability**:

$$P(X \ge k) = P(X > k) + p(k)$$

"At least $k$" keeps the cell at $k$; "more than $k$" throws it away. If the table puts fifteen hundredths on that value, the two answers differ by fifteen hundredths. Read the wording twice. *At most* includes. *Less than* excludes. *At least* includes. *More than* excludes.

A **continuous** random variable is the other kind: a temperature, a pressure, a duration measured as finely as the gauge allows. Between any two values it can take there is another one, so the values cannot be listed and no table can hold them. Its distribution is a **density curve** $f(x)$, and probability is the **area under it**:

$$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

Three consequences, all of which are the same consequence.

**The height is not a probability.** $f(x)$ is probability *per unit* of the axis: per degree, per second, per tonne. Multiply it by a width and you have a probability. Because it is a rate, it may exceed 1 without anything being wrong: squeeze a distribution into a narrow enough interval and the curve must rise to keep the area at one.

**A single point has probability zero.** An interval of width zero has area zero, whatever the height above it. $P(X = x) = 0$ exactly, for every $x$, and the model assigns nothing to any point. What Sandoval means by "still the most likely number" is a statement about *density*: of any two intervals of the same width, the one under the tallest part of the curve carries the most probability.

**"At least" and "more than" become the same question.** No point carries mass, so $P(X \ge a) = P(X > a)$ for a continuous variable. The off-by-one that matters so much on the hours table does not exist on the temperature curve. Apply either rule to the wrong kind of variable and the report comes out wrong in both directions at once.

Both kinds hand out a total of one. The discrete model puts it in cells you can point at; the continuous model spreads it along an axis so that only intervals carry any.
</Briefing.Intuition>

Every cell is between 0 and 1 and the column sums to 1, so it is a distribution and the questions have answers.

*A cumulative probability.* The chance the crossing is done inside fifty-two hours: add the cells from the bottom.

<Formula tex={cumulativeTex} label="P(X ≤ 52): three cells, added" />

So {fmtPct(pAtMost52, 0)} of crossings are finished by fifty-two hours, and by the complement rule {fmt(1 - pAtMost52, 2)} of them run longer.

*The boundary.* At fifty-six hours the table puts {fmt(massAt56, 2)} on the value itself, so the two questions a reader might think are the same are not:

<Formula tex={boundaryTex} label="At least fifty-six, against more than fifty-six" />

{fmtPct(massAt56, 0)} of the whole distribution sits in that one cell. Four hours of cellar, and which side of the inequality they fall on is a decision rather than a rounding.

**The continuous one.** Sink temperature at hour sixty of a Watch run, $T$, modelled as a normal density with mean {SINK_TEMP.mean} {SINK_TEMP.units} and standard deviation {SINK_TEMP.sd} {SINK_TEMP.units}.

The probability that $T$ comes out at exactly {SINK_TEMP.mean} {SINK_TEMP.units} is **zero**. Not small: zero. The event "exactly {SINK_TEMP.mean} degrees" is an interval of width zero, and an area of width zero is zero however tall the curve is over it.

And yet the curve is tallest there, and that is what "most likely" means for a continuous variable. The density at the mean is {fmt(peakDensity, 5)} per {SINK_TEMP.units}. Multiply that height by a width of one degree and you have very nearly the probability of landing in the degree-wide sliver around the mean:

<Formula tex={sliverTex} label="Density × width ≈ probability" />

The same sliver two standard deviations out, a degree wide and centred on {fmt(SINK_TEMP.mean + 2 * SINK_TEMP.sd, 0)}, carries {fmt(halfDegreeOut, 5)}, about {fmt(densityRatio, 1)} times less. That is the entire content of "most likely" for a continuous variable: neighbourhoods near the peak carry more area than neighbourhoods of the same width anywhere else.

Widen the interval and the probability grows: within one standard deviation of nominal, {fmt(oneSd, 4)}; above {fmt(SINK_TEMP.mean + 2 * SINK_TEMP.sd, 0)} {SINK_TEMP.units}, {fmt(overRated, 4)}. And because no point carries mass, "at or above {fmt(SINK_TEMP.mean + 2 * SINK_TEMP.sd, 0)}" is that same {fmt(overRated, 4)}. On this side of the plan the off-by-one does not exist.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*A table that does not sum to 1.* Ebele's first draft was over by {fmt(badExcess, 2)} and every figure read off it was over by the same share. Check the column before you use it: the two obligations are non-negative cells and a total of exactly one, and a model that fails the second has stopped being a probability model. Repairing it by dividing every cell through is a different operation from correcting the cell that was mistyped, and the first spreads one error across every value of $X$.

*"At least" read as "more than".* On a discrete variable $P(X \ge k)$ and $P(X > k)$ differ by exactly $p(k)$, the cell on the boundary: here {fmt(massAt56, 2)} at fifty-six hours, and more than that at the Chief's limit. It is the single commonest arithmetic error in this topic and it is always worth the whole boundary cell, in whichever direction hurts.

*$P(X = x)$ for a continuous $X$.* Zero, for every $x$, always. A density's height is probability per unit and not probability; asking for the chance of exactly {SINK_TEMP.mean} degrees is asking for the area of a line. Ask for an interval instead, and keep the units of the density in mind, per degree, because that is what turns a height into a probability when you multiply by a width.

*Reading the two rules onto the wrong variable.* Boundaries matter on tables and do not matter on densities. Applying the discrete rule to a curve makes you hunt for a mass that is not there; applying the continuous rule to a table makes you throw away a cell worth {fmtPct(massAt56, 0)} of the distribution and call it a rounding convention.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
