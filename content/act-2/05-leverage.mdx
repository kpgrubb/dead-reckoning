<Briefing title="How much, and how far" topics={["2.8"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A fitted line comes with two summaries, and they answer questions that sound alike and are not.

**r², how much.** The response varies. Some of that variation tracks the explanatory variable, and the line captures it; the rest is left in the residuals. $r^2$ is the first as a share of the whole: the proportion of the variation in $y$ that the linear model accounts for. It is a proportion, so it is unitless, it lies between 0 and 1, and it is the square of the correlation, never the correlation itself. It is also, and this matters here, sensitive to the *range* of $x$ in the file: widen the range and there is more variation for the line to explain, so $r^2$ rises without the fit becoming one tonne more accurate about anybody.

**s, how far.** The standard deviation of the residuals, $s = \sqrt{\sum e_i^2/(n-2)}$, is the typical vertical distance from an individual to its own prediction, **in the units of the response**. It is the number you quote when somebody asks how big a miss is ordinary. Unlike $r^2$ it does not care how wide the file's $x$-range is; it cares only how tightly the individuals hug the line. A report that gives $r^2$ and withholds $s$ has told the reader that the model is good and left them no way to judge any particular departure from it.

**Leverage and influence.** Leverage is a property of $x$ alone: $h_i = 1/n + (x_i - \bar{x})^2/S_{xx}$, large for individuals far from the centre of the explanatory variable. A high-leverage point *can* move the line a long way, because the fit's balance point pivots and a long arm needs little force. Whether it *does* depends on its residual against the line the rest of the file makes. Far out in $x$ and close to that line: high leverage, no influence, and the point stretches the plot, inflates $r^2$ and changes nothing anybody would report. Far out in $x$ and well off the line: **influential**, and the slope you quote is partly a statement about one individual.

The only honest test is the one Ferrier asked for. Fit it with. Fit it without. Report both.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="r^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}" terms={{ '\\text{SSE}': 'variation the line did not account for', '\\text{SST}': 'total variation in the response about its mean', 'r^2': 'the share of the variation in y explained by the linear model' }} label="Coefficient of determination" />

<Formula tex="s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{n-2}} \qquad h_i = \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_i - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}" terms={{ 's': 'standard deviation of the residuals: a typical miss, in the units of y', 'n-2': 'degrees of freedom: two were spent estimating the slope and intercept', 'h_i': 'leverage of the i-th individual: a property of its x value alone' }} label="Residual standard deviation, and leverage" />

A regression **computer-output table** carries both, and the equation. The `Coef` column holds the intercept (the *Constant* row) and the slope (the predictor's row); `SE Coef` says how precisely each is pinned down; `S` is the residual standard deviation and `R-Sq` is $r^2$ as a percentage. The equation is read out of `Coef` and nowhere else.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Adlinda Yards again: the eight Tessera-C acceptance runs, declared mass against fuel loaded on the yard's 2.4-AU proving track, both in kilotonnes. Over those eight hulls the fit is ŷ = {fmt(provingFit.intercept, 4)} + {fmt(provingFit.slope, 4)}x, with $r^2$ = {fmt(provingFit.r2, 4)} and $s$ = {fmt(provingFit.s, 4)} kt.

In words: about {fmt(100 * provingFit.r2, 1)} % of the variation in fuel loaded is explained by the linear relationship with declared mass, and a hull's actual fuel typically lands about {fmt(provingFit.s, 3)} kt from the line's prediction for her.

Now the yard's ninth acceptance. The tug *{tug.hull}* is {fmt(tug.declared, 1)} kt, two thirds again as heavy as anything else on the file, and she loaded {fmt(tug.fuel, 2)} kt, which is within a few kilograms of what the eight-hull line predicts for her. Refit and compare:

<Formula tex={`b: ${fmt(provingFit.slope, 4)} \\rightarrow ${fmt(nineFit.slope, 4)} \\qquad r^2: ${fmt(provingFit.r2, 4)} \\rightarrow ${fmt(nineFit.r2, 4)} \\qquad s: ${fmt(provingFit.s, 4)} \\rightarrow ${fmt(nineFit.s, 4)}`} label="Eight hulls, then nine: what the distant point changed" />

Her leverage is {fmt(nineFit.leverage[8], 3)}, far the largest on the file, and {fmt(nineFit.leverage[8] / (4 / 9), 1)} times the conventional flag. She moved the slope by {fmt(Math.abs(nineFit.slope - provingFit.slope), 4)} kt per kt, which is nothing. Her Cook's distance is {fmt(nineFit.cooks[8], 3)}.

Look at what *did* move. $r^2$ went {nineFit.r2 > provingFit.r2 ? 'up' : 'down'}, from {fmt(provingFit.r2, 4)} to {fmt(nineFit.r2, 4)}, because she added a great deal of variation in declared mass for the line to explain and it explained it. $s$ barely moved at all: {fmt(provingFit.s, 4)} to {fmt(nineFit.s, 4)} kt. The fit is no more accurate about any hull than it was, and the summary that says "how much" has improved while the summary that says "how far" has not. That is the whole argument for quoting both, and it is the yard's file making it, not a textbook.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"r² = {fmt(provingFit.r2, 2)}, so the correlation is {fmt(provingFit.r2, 2)}."* No: $r = \sqrt{r^2}$ with the sign of the slope, here {fmt(provingFit.r, 4)}. Two different numbers on two different scales, and quoting one for the other in a report to a Board will be the last thing anybody reads of it.

*"s is the spread of the response."* $s_y$ is the spread of $y$ about its own mean, before any line is fitted. $s$ is the spread of what is **left over** after the line. On the proving file those are {fmt(provingFit.sy, 3)} kt and {fmt(provingFit.s, 3)} kt, and the gap between them is exactly what the line bought you.

*"s is a limit."* A typical miss is not a maximum one. Roughly a third of honest individuals sit further from the line than $s$; a band of $\pm 2s$ holds most of them, and honest rows fall outside it too.

*The rule of thumb.* Flagging leverage above $4/n$, or Cook's distance above $4/n$, is a convention some software prints and a useful place to start looking. It is not a decision procedure and it is not a rule anyone is obliged to accept. The finding is always the with-and-without comparison, in the units of the problem: *the slope moves by this much when this row is removed.* Quote that, not a flag.

*Deleting the inconvenient.* A row leaves the analysis for a reason outside the plot, such as a recording error, a fault, or an individual from a different population, and the reader is told which. "It was distorting the fit" describes every influential observation ever recorded, including all the true ones.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
