<Briefing title="Least squares" topics={["2.6", "2.8"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Any straight line you lay across a scatterplot misses most of the points. The miss for one individual is its **residual**: how far the observed response sits above or below the line, measured vertically, $e = y - \hat{y}$. A line that is too high misses low on everything; a line that is too steep misses low at one end and high at the other.

So there are many candidate lines, and you need a rule for choosing one. Add the residuals and they cancel — the misses above pay for the misses below. Add their absolute values and the total has a corner at the bottom instead of a floor, and no formula comes out of it. **Square them and add**: every miss counts, big misses count disproportionately, and the total is a smooth bowl with exactly one lowest point. The line that makes $\sum e^2$ as small as any line can is the **least-squares regression line**, and it is what the instrument below snaps to.

Two things follow from that rule and are worth holding on to. The least-squares line always passes through the point of averages $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ — the balance point of the cloud. And its slope is the correlation, rescaled from standard deviations into real units: $b = r\,s_y/s_x$. Correlation says how tightly; slope says how much, in tonnes per tonne.

Reading the line out loud takes discipline. The slope is the **predicted** change in the response for a one-unit increase in the explanatory variable — on average, across the individuals in the file, and true of no single one of them. The intercept is the **predicted** response when the explanatory variable is zero: always that, whether or not zero is a place the data have ever been. And the whole line is evidence only across the range of $x$ the file covers. Off the end of it, the line still answers, but nothing observed is holding it down.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2 \;\longrightarrow\; \text{minimum}" terms={{ '\\hat{y}_i': "the line's prediction for the i-th individual", 'y_i': 'the i-th observed response', '\\text{SSE}': 'sum of squared residuals — the total area of the squares' }} label="The least-squares criterion" />

<Formula tex="b = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x} \qquad\qquad a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x}" terms={{ '\\bar{x}': 'mean of the explanatory variable', '\\bar{y}': 'mean of the response', 's_x': 'standard deviation of the explanatory variable', 's_y': 'standard deviation of the response' }} label="Slope and intercept of the least-squares line" />

The fitted line is $\hat{y} = a + bx$, where $b$ is the **slope**, $a$ the **y-intercept** and $r$ the correlation. The hat on $\hat{y}$ is not decoration: it marks a predicted value, never an observed one. A **residual** is $e = y - \hat{y}$, and the least-squares line is the unique line for which $\sum e = 0$ and $\sum e^2$ is as small as any line can make it.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Adlinda Yards keeps an acceptance file on every hull that leaves its slips. Eight Tessera-C hulls, each run out to the yard's standard 2.4-AU proving track and back, with the fuel each one loaded for the run recorded against the mass it declared (both in kilotonnes):

{proving.map((p) => `${p.hull} ${fmt(p.declared, 1)} / ${fmt(p.fuel, 2)}`).join(' · ')}

The summaries, all eight rows: $\bar{x}$ = {fmt(provingSummary.xMean, 3)} kt, $\bar{y}$ = {fmt(provingSummary.yMean, 3)} kt, $s_x$ = {fmt(provingSummary.sx, 4)}, $s_y$ = {fmt(provingSummary.sy, 4)}, and $r$ = {fmt(provingSummary.r, 4)}.

The slope comes first, because the intercept depends on it:

<Formula tex={`b = r\\,\\frac{s_y}{s_x} = ${fmt(provingSummary.r, 4)} \\times \\frac{${fmt(provingSummary.sy, 4)}}{${fmt(provingSummary.sx, 4)}} = ${fmt(fromSummary.slope, 6)}`} label="Slope of the proving-run fit (kt of fuel per kt of declared mass)" />

<Formula tex={`a = \\bar{y} - b\\bar{x} = ${fmt(provingSummary.yMean, 3)} - (${fmt(fromSummary.slope, 6)})(${fmt(provingSummary.xMean, 3)}) = ${fmt(fromSummary.intercept, 4)}`} label="Intercept — the line is pinned through the point of averages" />

So the fitted line is ŷ = {fmt(fromSummary.intercept, 3)} + {fmt(fromSummary.slope, 3)}x, and it reads: **for each additional kilotonne of declared mass, the predicted fuel loaded for the proving run increases by about {fmt(fromSummary.slope, 3)} kt.** Not "a hull one kilotonne heavier burns {fmt(fromSummary.slope, 3)} kt more" — the line predicts; the hulls do as they like.

The intercept, {fmt(fromSummary.intercept, 3)} kt, is the predicted fuel for a hull declaring nothing. Zero is well outside the observed range ({fmt(min(provingX), 1)} to {fmt(max(provingX), 1)} kt), so the honest reading is that the number sits close to zero, as the physics says it should, and that the yard holds no data on empty hulls.

Least squares earns its name here. Pin the line through the point of averages — every candidate has to pass through it — and swing it. At a slope of 0.195 the total is {fmt(sseForLine(provingX, provingY, 0.195, provingFit.yMean - 0.195 * provingFit.xMean), 4)} kt². At 0.170 it is {fmt(sseForLine(provingX, provingY, 0.17, provingFit.yMean - 0.17 * provingFit.xMean), 4)} kt². At the fitted {fmt(provingFit.slope, 4)} it is $\sum e^2$ = {fmt(provingFit.sse, 4)} kt², and nothing you do to the line brings it lower. Being worse at nothing else is the entire claim this line makes.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
Three ways to lose the marks, and the report.

*"Fuel loaded increases by {fmt(fromSummary.slope, 3)} kt for every extra kilotonne."* No **predicted**, no **on average**. That sentence says every hull does it, and no hull in the file does.

*"A hull declaring nothing burns {fmt(fromSummary.intercept, 3)} kt."* The intercept is always the predicted response at $x = 0$; it is only a physical statement when zero lies inside the observed range. Here it does not, and the yard's file cannot testify about a ship that does not exist.

*Swapping the axes.* The least-squares line of $y$ on $x$ and the line of $x$ on $y$ are different lines — the first minimises vertical misses, the second horizontal ones — and only one of them answers the question you asked. Regressing declared mass on inferred mass would give a slope with units of declared tonnes per inferred tonne, which is not the question the Bureau's file is being asked.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
