<Briefing title="Two quantitative variables, and one number for the cloud" topics={["2.4", "2.5"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Two quantitative variables on the same individuals give a **scatterplot**: the **explanatory** variable on the horizontal axis, the **response** on the vertical, one point per individual. Describing it is four things, always in the variables' own words.

**Direction.** As the explanatory variable increases, does the response tend to rise (positive) or fall (negative)? **Form.** Does the cloud follow a straight line, or a curve? **Strength.** How tightly do the points hug that form? **Unusual features.** Points off the pattern, gaps, clusters, anything the eye stops on.

The **correlation coefficient** $r$ puts a number on two of those four, and only two: direction and the strength of the *linear* part. It is built by standardising both variables, turning every value into how many standard deviations it sits from its own mean, and averaging the products. When a point is above average on both, the product is positive; above on one and below on the other, negative. Sum them and you have a number between $-1$ and $1$.

Three consequences follow from that construction and they are the whole of what $r$ is for.

$r$ **has no units.** Both variables were divided by their own standard deviations on the way in, and the units cancelled. Tonnes, kilotonnes or terawatts on one axis: the same $r$.

$r$ **is symmetric.** The product $z_x z_y$ does not care which factor is written first, so exchanging the axes leaves $r$ alone. The regression slope has no such property, and it is the thing that changes.

$r$ **is a mean, so it is not resistant.** One point far from the pattern can raise it or collapse it.

And one thing it is not. $r$ describes the individuals plotted. It says nothing about what produced the pattern, and in observational records, where hulls were logged rather than assigned, no arithmetic anywhere turns an association into a cause.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="r = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s_x}\right)\left(\frac{y_i - \bar{y}}{s_y}\right)" terms={{ 'r': 'correlation coefficient, always between −1 and 1', 'z_x': 'the standardised explanatory value: how many SDs from its own mean', 's_x': 'standard deviation of the explanatory variable', 's_y': 'standard deviation of the response' }} label="Correlation" />

Strength, by the usual convention: $|r| \geq 0.8$ strong, $0.4$ to $0.8$ moderate, below $0.4$ weak. The bands are a courtesy, not a law; the plot decides.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Eight hours of one Quiet-profile run out of Sandoval's cellar log: sink temperature at the top of the hour against the quiet hours the cellar still had in it.

<Plot
  spec={{ kind: 'scatter', points: cellarPoints, xLabel: 'sink temperature (K)', yLabel: 'remaining quiet hours (h)' }}
  label="CELLAR · EIGHT QUIET-PROFILE HOURS"
  description="Scatterplot of remaining quiet hours against sink temperature for eight hourly readings from one Quiet-profile run. The points fall from upper left to lower right in a roughly straight band."
  showTable
/>

The means are {fmt(mean(cellarTemp), 1)} K and {fmt(mean(cellarHours), 2)} h; the standard deviations {fmt(sd(cellarTemp), 1)} K and {fmt(sd(cellarHours), 2)} h. Standardise each pair, multiply the two z-scores, add the eight products and divide by $n - 1$:

$r$ = **{fmt(cellarR, 3)}**.

Strong, negative, linear: as the cellar warms, the quiet hours left in it fall, and the eight points hold that line tightly. Written for a reader: *the remaining quiet hours of a Quiet-profile run have a strong negative linear association with sink temperature.* Not one word in that sentence about what the temperature does to the clock, because this is a log of one run rather than eight runs somebody arranged.

Now the two properties, on the same eight points. Read the vertical axis in minutes instead of hours, multiplying every value by 60, and $r$ = **{fmt(cellarRMinutes, 3)}**. Put temperature on the vertical axis and hours on the horizontal, the same eight hours with the plot turned over, and $r$ = **{fmt(cellarRSwapped, 3)}**. Three plots, one number. That is what "unitless and symmetric" means when you actually do it.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
Four errors, and a career of reports is built out of them.

*"$r$ is near zero, so there is no relationship."* $r$ near zero means no **linear** relationship. A perfect arch has $r \approx 0$ and a relationship you could set a watch by. Look at the plot.

*"$r$ changed when we converted the units."* It cannot. If it did, something else changed too: a filter, a dropped row, a variable that is not a rescaling of the old one. Plume power is not inferred mass in different units; it folds in each hull's own acceleration, so it is a different variable and it may well have a different $r$.

*"$r$ is large, so the relationship is linear."* Curved data can return $r$ above $0.9$. Only the plot, and from the next module the residuals, can rule a curve out.

*"$r$ is large, so one causes the other."* These hulls were logged, not assigned. Every causal sentence written off an observational correlation is a sentence somebody else will strike, and they will be right to.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
