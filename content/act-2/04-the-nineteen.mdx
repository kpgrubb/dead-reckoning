The bridge at the back of the forenoon watch is four people and a great many instruments doing nothing in particular. Oyelaran has the Eyes back on the sweep schedule and resents every one of the four hours in twelve she has lent to archive work. Solberg has nothing to send. Ferrier has the plot.

Ebele puts the residual display on the intel terminal, and then, because he wants it seen, on the main. Two panels, stacked. On top, the scatter with the line through it: four hundred and twelve hulls laid against the diagonal so tightly that the line is hard to pick out from the cloud. Underneath, the same file with the line subtracted — residual against declared mass, a horizontal zero, a shaded band for the ordinary spread.

Under the line, the cloud should be featureless. That is the whole point of subtracting it. Take the model out and what is left is what the model could not account for, and if the model is right, what is left is noise: no bend, no funnel, no run of one sign. What is left is a flat grey band, hulls above and below it in roughly equal number, the same width at the lightest departure in the file as at the heaviest.

Except that is not all that is left.

Above the band, clear of it, with nothing in between, sits a knot of hulls. Not one hull. Not a scatter reaching up. A group, holding a height, spread the whole width of the declared-mass axis from the light end to the heavy. Nobody says anything for a moment, because everyone on the bridge has now seen the same thing and is waiting to find out whether somebody will say it wrong first.

Ebele reaches past the unit toggle and sets COLOUR BY to *later-lost*.

The band does not change. The knot changes colour. All of it, at once.

He stops talking. It is the first time you have known him to.

</Scene>

<Dialogue speaker="xo" aside="Not looking away from the lower panel.">
Before anyone writes anything. Tell me the high ones aren't just big ships.
</Dialogue>

It is a fair question, and the display answers it. If heavy hulls were simply measured worse than light ones, the band would open out toward the right: a funnel, narrow at the light end, wide at the heavy, with the worst misses belonging to the biggest ships because the biggest ships have the most to be wrong about. It does not open out. The band is the same width at fourteen thousand tonnes as at twenty-seven, and the knot above it sits at the same height over the light hulls as over the heavy ones. Whatever is happening to those hulls is not a measurement error that grows with mass.

<Dialogue speaker="analyst" aside="Fast, and not steady.">
So — then the data are wrong, sir. The telemetry or the certificates, one of the two, because both of them can't —
</Dialogue>

You stop him there, because it is the sentence the entire report turns on.

The model is not wrong. A bend in this plot, or a funnel, would mean the line was the wrong description and the fault was ours: we would go and find a better curve and say so. This plot has neither. The line describes four hundred and twelve hulls as well as anything could, and the band beneath it is flat and even, and the model has nothing left to answer for.

The data are not wrong either. The relay net measured what it measured, to half a percent, and the Bureau printed what it printed.

What is wrong is the declarations. A signed certificate and an independent measurement disagree, for a particular set of hulls and for nobody else in four hundred and twelve. *The data are wrong* is a complaint. *These hulls declared wrong* is a finding — and the distance between those two sentences is the whole report.

<Briefing title="Residuals" topics={["2.7"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A residual is what the line missed by, for one individual: **actual minus predicted**, $e = y - \hat{y}$, measured vertically. The order is a convention and the convention is not negotiable, because the sign carries the meaning. A positive residual says the individual sits *above* the line — the model gave it too little, it underestimates that point. A negative residual says the opposite. Reverse the subtraction and every conclusion downstream inverts.

The **residual plot** puts those misses on their own axes: residual up the side, the explanatory variable along the bottom, a horizontal line at zero. It is the scatterplot with the model taken out, magnified — and it is the only honest way to look at what a line could not explain, because on the original scatterplot a strong relationship squashes every interesting deviation flat against the trend.

What you are looking for, in order:

- **A bend.** One sign at both ends of the x-range and the other through the middle. That is curvature: the true relationship is not a straight line, and the residuals are tracing the part of the curve the line could not follow. The **model** is wrong.
- **A funnel.** The band widening (or narrowing) steadily across the x-range. The form may be fine, but the line predicts precisely at one end and loosely at the other, and equal spread — a condition that every interval and every test built on this line will quietly assume — fails. Again, the **model**.
- **Individuals standing clear.** No bend, no funnel, a band of constant width — and then a point, or a group of points, well outside it. The model has passed. What is unusual is not the description; it is those individuals, and the next question is about *them*.

That last distinction is the one that separates a competent report from a useless one. A pattern indicts the model. A clean band with a cluster above it indicts the members of the cluster. Neither indicts the data for being inconvenient.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i, \qquad \hat{y}_i = a + bx_i" terms={{ 'e_i': 'residual of the i-th individual — actual minus predicted', 'y_i': 'the observed response', '\\hat{y}_i': 'the response the line predicts at that x' }} label="Residual" />

For a least-squares line, $\sum e_i = 0$ always: the misses above exactly pay for the misses below, which is why their sum is no test of anything. The useful summary of their size is the standard deviation of the residuals, $s = \sqrt{\sum e_i^2 / (n-2)}$ — the typical vertical distance from the line, in the units of the response. A band of $\pm 2s$ about zero holds most of an honest residual plot.

Units matter twice here. A residual is in the units of $y$; a residual expressed as a percentage of $x$ is a different quantity with a different meaning, and a display that offers both will let you compare hulls of very different sizes on the same footing. Say which one you are quoting.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The Adlinda proving run again — eight Tessera-C hulls, declared mass against fuel loaded for the yard's 2.4-AU track, both in kilotonnes. The yard's terminal prints its fit to five places: ŷ = {fmt(provingFit.intercept, 5)} + {fmt(provingFit.slope, 5)}x, with $s$ = {fmt(provingFit.s, 4)} kt.

Take *{proving[worst].hull}*: declared {fmt(proving[worst].declared, 1)} kt, loaded {fmt(proving[worst].fuel, 2)} kt.

<Formula tex={`\\hat{y} = ${fmt(provingFit.intercept, 5)} + ${fmt(provingFit.slope, 5)}(${fmt(proving[worst].declared, 1)}) = ${fmt(provingFit.fitted[worst], 3)}`} label="What the line predicts for her" />

<Formula tex={`e = y - \\hat{y} = ${fmt(proving[worst].fuel, 2)} - ${fmt(provingFit.fitted[worst], 3)} = ${fmt(provingFit.residuals[worst], 3)}`} label="Her residual, in kilotonnes of fuel" />

In words: *{proving[worst].hull}* loaded {fmt(Math.abs(provingFit.residuals[worst]), 3)} kt {provingFit.residuals[worst] > 0 ? 'more' : 'less'} fuel than the line predicts for a hull of her declared mass, so the model {provingFit.residuals[worst] > 0 ? 'underestimates' : 'overestimates'} her. That is {fmt(Math.abs(provingFit.residuals[worst]) / provingFit.s, 2)} times the typical miss — {Math.abs(provingFit.residuals[worst]) / provingFit.s > 1.8 ? 'the largest miss in the file, though still well inside the ordinary band' : 'unremarkable, which is what an honest file looks like'}.

The eight residuals, in yard order (kt): {provingFit.residuals.map((e) => fmt(e, 3)).join(', ')}.

They sum to {fmt(provingFit.residuals.reduce((s, e) => s + e, 0), 3)}, as least-squares residuals always do. Their signs run {provingFit.residuals.map((e) => (e > 0 ? '+' : '−')).join(' ')} — no run of one sign at the ends, no widening from the light hulls to the heavy ones, nothing standing clear. Eight rows is too few to certify a model, but there is nothing here arguing against a line, and that is all a residual plot is ever asked to say.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Predicted minus actual.* The right magnitude, the wrong sign, and a report that says a hull ran light when it ran heavy. Actual minus predicted. Always.

*"There's a pattern in the residual plot, so the data are bad."* No. A pattern is the model speaking, not the data confessing. Curvature means fit a curve; fanning means the precision is not constant and the conditions for later inference do not hold. Neither is a reason to discard a row, and a report that discards rows because they disagree with a line is not a report.

*Reading the band as a rule.* The $\pm 2s$ band is the ordinary spread, not a fence. Honest individuals fall outside it; roughly one in twenty should. What earns attention is a *group* outside it, on one side, with a gap between them and everyone else.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

<Scene stamp="MET 036/15:20" location="Bridge · main display" condensed="Every hull above five percent is a hull that never arrived, and every one of them departed between six and thirteen percent heavier than its certificate. There are two shelves inside the group and Ebele cannot explain them. The remaining lost hulls sit inside the band, with everybody else.">

So it stands, and it will still stand in the morning.

The Manifest file holds {highLost.length} hulls that departed between {fmt(min(highPct), 0)} and {fmt(max(highPct), 0)} percent heavier than the mass their Bureau certificate declares. Every one of them is a hull that never arrived. Nothing else in the four hundred and twelve comes within a percent of them; below five percent the file is a flat, even band of honest scatter, and there is a clear gap in between with nobody in it.

It is the first hard fact of this deployment. Not a shape, not a pattern, not an observation regarding the distribution of something. A number, with a denominator, from two independent files that were never meant to be read against each other — the Bureau's signatures on one side, the Lane Authority's telemetry on the other, neither office with any reason to expect the comparison and neither able to have arranged it.

You take a minute with it, on the deck, where they can all see you take it. Six years. Thirty-one hulls and their crews, most of them never recovered, every file closed with a word that means nobody found out. And what reopens them is a column of arithmetic that has been sitting in two archives the whole time, waiting for somebody to subtract one from the other.

Ebele finds the other thing within the minute, because he is looking for it.

The group is not one group. It is two. There are {lowShelf} of them on a shelf at the lower end of that range and {highShelf} on a second shelf above it, and there is nothing between the shelves either. He runs the obvious checks — owner class, drive model, Lane length, departure year — and none of them separates the shelves, and he says so instead of inventing a reason, which is the most professional thing he does all watch.

You let it stand unexplained in the file. It will keep.

The last thing is the one nobody wants to look at. The Register has {lostTotal} losses in it. Of those, {highLost.length} are up here. The other {lostTotal - highLost.length} are down in the band with everybody else — ordinary hulls, ordinary certificates, ordinary scatter, and gone. Whatever happened to this file did not happen to all of them.

</Scene>

<Dialogue speaker="xo" aside="She has not written anything down. She wants it out loud first.">
Say it back to me, Captain. One sentence. What we know, and nothing we don't.
</Dialogue>

You give it to her the way it will have to go to the Board: that {highLost.length} hulls subsequently recorded as losses departed with an inferred mass between {fmt(min(highPct), 0)} and {fmt(max(highPct), 0)} percent above their certified declared mass, that no other departure in the file does so, and that the residual plot shows no curvature and no change in spread — so the discrepancy lies in the declarations and not in the model or the measurement.

Ferrier makes you say the second half twice: what is *not* proven. Who signed a false figure knowing it. Why. What the extra mass was. Where those hulls went, or whether they went anywhere. None of that is in the file, and the file is all you have.
