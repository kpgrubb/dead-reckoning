<Briefing title="Two variables in one table" topics={["2.1", "2.2", "2.3"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
One categorical variable gives a frequency table. Two give a **two-way table**: the categories of one variable down the side, the categories of the other across the top, and in each cell the count of individuals that are both. The margins (the row totals and the column totals) are the two one-variable tables you would have had if you had never crossed them.

From the same cells come three different relative frequencies, and they differ only in what you divide by.

A **joint** relative frequency divides a cell by the grand total: the share of *everybody* that is both things at once. A **marginal** relative frequency divides a row or column total by the grand total: the share of everybody in one category of one variable, ignoring the other. A **conditional** relative frequency divides a cell by one row's or one column's total. It throws away every individual outside that group first, and then asks the question.

Only the conditional one lets you compare groups. Counts cannot: a row with nineteen individuals in it will out-count a row with five almost whatever is going on, and the difference you are looking at is the size of the groups. Percentages inside each row put every group on the same denominator, which is the only way a comparison means anything.

Displayed, that is a **segmented bar** per group: one bar per row, each bar cut into the conditional distribution of the column variable. A **mosaic** does the same and lets the bar widths carry the group sizes as well.

Then the reading. If the conditional distributions are the same from group to group, the two variables **appear independent** in this file. If they differ, the two **appear associated**. Both are descriptions of the individuals in the table. Neither is an inference about anything outside it, and neither is a cause.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="P(B \mid A) = \frac{n_{AB}}{n_A} \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(A \mid B) = \frac{n_{AB}}{n_B}" terms={{ 'n_{AB}': 'the count in the cell where A and B meet; the same number in both', 'n_A': "the row total for A: every individual in A's group", 'n_B': "the column total for B: every individual in B's group" }} label="Two conditionals from one cell" />

Same numerator, different denominators, different questions. They are equal only when $n_A = n_B$, which is an accident of the margins and never a reason.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The 2176 escort report on *Asgard*, Mars–Belt corridor: {asgard.total} losses over one season, cross-tabulated by which half of the corridor the hull was lost in and by the cause code the escort filed.

Take the one cell where *outer half* meets *unknown*: {asgard.table[1][1]} losses. Three relative frequencies come out of it, and they differ only in the denominator.

**Joint.** {asgard.table[1][1]} over the grand total {asgard.total}: **{fmtPct(asgard.joint[1][1], 1)}** of the season's losses were both outer-half and unknown.

**Conditional, one way.** Of the {asgard.rowTotals[1]} losses in the outer half, {asgard.table[1][1]} were coded unknown. $P(\text{unknown} \mid \text{outer half})$ is {asgard.table[1][1]} over {asgard.rowTotals[1]} = **{fmtPct(asgard.rowConditional[1][1], 1)}**.

**Conditional, the other way.** Of the {asgard.colTotals[1]} losses coded unknown, {asgard.table[1][1]} were in the outer half. $P(\text{outer half} \mid \text{unknown})$ is {asgard.table[1][1]} over {asgard.colTotals[1]} = **{fmtPct(asgard.colConditional[1][1], 1)}**.

One cell, two conditionals, {fmtPct(Math.abs(asgard.rowConditional[1][1] - asgard.colConditional[1][1]), 1)} apart. Written out in English the two sentences look almost identical; only the arithmetic shows that they answer different questions.

The comparison the report was actually written for is a third thing again: {fmtPct(asgard.rowConditional[1][1], 1)} of the outer-half losses were coded unknown against {fmtPct(asgard.rowConditional[0][1], 1)} of the inner-half ones. The conditional distributions differ, so cause code and corridor half *appear associated* in the escort's file. That is what the report said, and all it said.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
Two errors, and the second one is the expensive one.

*Comparing counts across groups of different size.* Nineteen in one row and one in another says almost nothing while the rows are nineteen and five long. Convert to conditional percentages inside each row before you compare anything, every time.

*Conditioning on the wrong variable.* $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ share a cell and nothing else. Write the sentence out in full before you compute: *of the hulls that are X, what share are Y.* The denominator is whatever follows *of the*. A true number that answers a question nobody asked is the hardest kind of error to catch, because it survives every arithmetic check.

And a third, quieter one: *appear associated* is a description of the individuals in the table. It is not evidence that owner class does anything to a hull, and the word *cause* has no business in the sentence.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
