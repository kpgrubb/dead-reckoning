<Briefing title="What a finding survives, and what a level commits you to" topics={["6.7"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A significance test has exactly two ways to be wrong, and both of them are outcomes of running the procedure correctly.

A **Type I error** rejects a true $H_0$: a false alarm, at a rate $\alpha$ that the analyst sets. A **Type II error** fails to reject a false one: a miss, at a rate $\beta$ that nobody sets. **Power** is $1 - \beta$, always measured against a specific alternative.

That much is a definition. What a review adds is that the two error rates are now claims about work already done, and three things follow.

**A level is a commitment, and the commitment is about timing.** The sentence "this test has a false-alarm rate of $\alpha$" is true of a rule that says *reject when $P < \alpha$* with $\alpha$ fixed in advance. Choose $\alpha$ after seeing $P$ and the rule stops being that rule. In the limiting case, an analyst who picks whatever level is just above the observed $P$ has built a procedure that rejects every time, whose false-alarm rate is 1 and whose reported $\alpha$ is decoration. This is why a reviewer's first question about a level is not what it was but when it was written down, and why the date beside it is worth more than the number.

**A level is bought.** Lowering $\alpha$ slides the rejection cutoff away from the null. Fewer false alarms, more misses, in that proportion, every time. The price is quotable in advance, against an alternative named in advance, and quoting it is the difference between a level chosen and a level defaulted to.

**A finding is about a population, and only that one.** A test licenses an inference about the population the data came from. Nothing in the arithmetic stops the sentence being written about a wider one, and nothing in the arithmetic supports it either. The scope is supplied by the design, not by the P-value, and a finding with no population named in the same sentence as the number is a finding that whoever reads it next can enlarge.

Two consequences a reviewer will press on, and both are about what the test does **not** say.

*A test that comes back short is not a finding of no difference.* It is a finding that this test, at this level, on this much data, did not have the reach. Power says how often it would have had the reach against a difference of a stated size. Where the power is low, "no evidence of an effect" and "evidence of no effect" are indistinguishable on the page, and only the first is available.

*A test never proves anything.* It reports evidence, at a stated error rate, about a parameter, for a population. The word "proved" moves a claim from something the file supports to something it does not, and it is the cheapest thing in a report to attack.

**Decisions are the other half.** Some choices are made under a probability rather than a P-value: an exposure computed from stated inputs, weighed against what the action buys, and then taken. Such a decision has to be judged by the ledger that existed when it was made. A probability describes the process that produces an outcome, so one outcome, good or bad, is nearly no evidence about whether the reasoning was right. Grading a decision by its result is called *resulting*, and it has a symmetric failure: the reckless call that comes off is never reviewed at all.
</Briefing.Intuition>

</Briefing.Definition>

<Briefing.Caution>
*"The case was proven."* A test supplies evidence about a parameter at a stated error rate. Proof is a word from a courtroom, and saying it in a report hands a reviewer the first paragraph of their reply. Say what the evidence is about, how strong it is, and for whom.

*"We failed to reject, so there is nothing there."* Failing to reject leaves $H_0$ standing as an unrefuted possibility and never establishes it. A real difference seen through an underpowered test produces the identical page. When a null result is being used as an argument, the question that settles it is what the power was against a difference worth finding.

*Scope, forgotten at the moment it matters.* The population is easy to write down while the analysis is being planned and easy to drop from the sentence that reports it. Findings are lost far more often to an inference stretched past its frame than to arithmetic.

*Resulting.* A decision made on a computed exposure is not refuted by the exposure landing. The audit is of the inputs, the combination and the weighing, all of which existed before the outcome did. Its mirror image is the unexamined decision that happened to come off.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
