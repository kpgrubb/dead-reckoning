A logarithm trades multiplication for addition. That one property is why a curved law becomes a straight line on the right axes.

<Formula
  tex="\log(uv) = \log u + \log v \qquad \log(u^{k}) = k\log u"
  terms={{ 'u, v': 'positive quantities', 'k': 'any exponent' }}
  label="What logs do to products and powers"
/>

**A power law straightens on log–log axes.** Take $y = a\,x^{b}$ and log both sides:

$$\log y = \log a + b \log x$$

Plot $\log y$ against $\log x$ and you have a line: intercept $\log a$, **slope $b$**. The exponent is no longer something to guess — it is a slope you can fit and read off. An exponential law $y = a\cdot 10^{\,bx}$ behaves the same way with only $y$ logged: $\log y = \log a + bx$, a straight line against raw $x$. Which transformation straightens the data tells you which family of law it obeys.

**Why the far end compresses.** The natural log grows, but its growth rate falls:

<Formula
  tex="\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}"
  terms={{ '1/x': 'the slope of ln x at x — smaller the further out you go' }}
  label="The derivative that does the compressing"
/>

At $x = 1$ the tangent has slope 1; at $x = 10$ it has slope $0.1$. Integrate that rate from $x$ to $2x$ and the gain is the same wherever you start:

$$\int_{x}^{2x} \frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t = \ln 2x - \ln x = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$$

Every doubling moves the same distance along a log axis. That is the whole trick: a log axis measures **ratios**, not differences, so a variable spanning two orders of magnitude fits on one screen and its multiplicative structure shows as straight-line geometry.

**One caution the algebra hides.** Least squares on $\log y$ minimises squared error in the *logs*, not in the original units — it treats a 10 % miss on a small hull as equal to a 10 % miss on a large one. That is usually what you want for a multiplicative law, and it is why the residual plot you judge the model by is drawn on the transformed scale.

**Why this matters here.** The Lane's transit geometry is a power law: a brachistochrone crossing takes $t = 2\sqrt{d/a}$, so $t \propto d^{1/2}$ and the exponent $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is a log–log slope waiting to be fitted. Straighten the curve and the residuals stop blaming the season — which is the only way a residual can start blaming a hull.
