Two claims get made about the geometric distribution and neither is obvious. The probabilities add to one even though there are infinitely many of them. And the average wait is $1/p$, which nobody derives on a bridge. Both fall out of one series, and the second one falls out of the first if you are willing to differentiate it.

The setting: independent trials, the same success probability $p$ each time, and $X$ is the number of trials **up to and including** the first success. To wait exactly $k$ trials is to fail $k-1$ times and then succeed:

**It sums to 1.** Write $q = 1 - p$. The total is $p + pq + pq^2 + \cdots$ — a geometric series with first term $p$ and common ratio $q$. Its partial sum telescopes: multiply $S_n = p(1 + q + \cdots + q^{n-1})$ by $q$, subtract, and everything in the middle cancels, leaving $S_n(1-q) = p(1 - q^n)$. So

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} p\,q^{k-1} = \frac{p(1 - q^{n})}{1 - q} = 1 - q^{n} \;\xrightarrow[\;n \to \infty\;]{}\; 1 \quad (|q| < 1)$$

Two things arrive at once. The total is 1, so this is a legitimate distribution. And the amount still missing after $n$ terms is exactly $q^{n}$ — which is $P(X > n)$, the probability the first success has *not* happened yet. That is the survival function, and you did not have to add anything up to get it:

$$P(X > n) = (1-p)^{n}$$

**And the mean is 1/p.** Start from the sum you already have, as a function of $q$:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} q^{k} = \frac{q}{1-q}$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $q$. Term by term on the left, quotient rule on the right:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k\,q^{k-1} = \frac{1}{(1-q)^{2}}$$

Multiply by $p$ and the left-hand side is $\sum k \cdot P(X = k)$, which is the definition of $E(X)$:

<Formula
  tex="E(X) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k\,(1-p)^{k-1}p = \frac{p}{(1-q)^{2}} = \frac{p}{p^{2}} = \frac{1}{p}"
  terms={{ '\\frac{1}{p}': 'the expected number of trials to the first success', 'q = 1-p': 'the failure probability; differentiating the series in q is the whole trick' }}
  label="The mean of a geometric variable"

One differentiation of a sum you already knew. The same move a second time gives the variance, $\sigma^2 = (1-p)/p^2$, and hence $\sigma = \sqrt{1-p}\,/\,p$ — which for small $p$ is very nearly $1/p$ again. **The spread is the size of the mean.** That is not a decoration; it is why a wait of fifty passages tells you almost nothing about the next fifty.

**The shape nobody expects.** Every term is the previous one times $q < 1$, so the pmf is strictly decreasing. The single most likely wait is always **one trial**, whatever $p$ is. The mean $1/p$ sits far out in a long right tail, and $P(X > 1/p) = (1-p)^{1/p} \to e^{-1} \approx 0.37$ as $p$ gets small — more than a third of waits run longer than the average, every time.

**Why this matters here.** Sandoval is buying a window with lithium, and the thing she is buying is a wait. The expected wait is $1/p$ and the rate $p$ is the one number nobody on this ship knows — which is why Ebele's drill is a sensitivity and not an answer. The tail $(1-p)^{k}$ is what actually sets the window's length: you choose $k$ so that the chance of being seen, or of waiting through the whole window for nothing, is a number you are willing to write in the log.
