Sandoval says it while she is doing something else. *Zero, and I'll show you why it's still the most likely number.* She does not show you. She goes back to the gauge. This is the showing.

Two kinds of distribution sit on the engineering slate this watch and they are not the same object.

A **probability mass function** hands out probability in lumps. Hours of cold running required: 44, 48, 52, and so on, each with a share of the total, and every share is itself a probability. They are all at most 1 and they add to exactly 1, because the ship needs *some* number of hours.

A **density** hands out nothing at a point. Sink temperature at hour 60 is a real number; there are uncountably many of them; if every one carried a positive probability the total would be infinite. So the curve's height is not a probability at all.

**Why a pdf may exceed 1.** Height is probability per unit. Squeeze a distribution into a narrow interval and its total probability is still 1, so the height has to rise to keep the area fixed: a normal with $\sigma = 0.2$ peaks above $1.99$, and nothing is wrong. Ask what the units are and the paradox evaporates — $f(x)$ is measured in *per degree*, and $2\ \text{per degree} \times 0.1\ \text{degree} = 0.2$, which is a probability and behaves like one. A pmf cannot do this, because a pmf's values are already probabilities with nothing to multiply them by.

**Why a point has probability zero.** Take an interval of width $\Delta x$ around one temperature and shrink it. The probability is about $f(x)\,\Delta x$, and $f$ is bounded near $x$, so the probability goes to zero with the width:

$$P(X = x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \int_{x}^{x + \Delta x} f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = 0$$

And yet 240 °C is still the most likely *neighbourhood*: of two slivers of equal width, the one under the taller part of the curve carries more probability. "Most likely" for a continuous variable is a statement about density, not about mass. That is Sandoval's sentence, in full.

One consequence you will use in every remaining Act: for a continuous variable, $P(X \ge x)$ and $P(X > x)$ are the same number, and so are "at most" and "less than". For the discrete table on the same slate they are *not* — the cell at 64 hours is four hours of cellar, and whether it is inside or outside your answer is a decision, not a rounding.

Shrink the bin width below and watch the two readings separate: the bars get shorter (probability per bar → 0) while their heights divided by width settle on a fixed curve (probability per unit → the density).

**Why this matters here.** The heat plan holds both kinds at once. Hours-cold-required is a table: you read $P(X > 64)$ off it by adding cells, and the 64 itself belongs to one side of the inequality or the other. Sink temperature is a curve: you read a probability off it by taking an area, and asking for the chance of exactly 240 degrees is asking for an area of width zero. Getting those two rules the wrong way round produces a report that is confidently wrong in both directions at once.
