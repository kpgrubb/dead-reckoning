Ebele offers this one at the bridge door, on a slate, unasked — the same slate, two Acts later. You do not need it to work the watch. It is here because the readout the panel calls *area above the cutoff* has a name in a different room, and the name explains why the inverse direction works at all.

**The CDF is an accumulation.** A density $f$ has no probability in it until you sweep an interval. Sweep from the far left up to a moving right-hand end $x$ and record the total as you go, and what you have built is a function of where you stopped:

<Formula
  tex="F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t"
  terms={{ 'f(t)': 'the density — a height, never a probability', 'F(x)': 'the running total of that density up to x: the proportion of the population at or below x', 'x': 'the upper limit — the thing that moves' }}
  label="The cumulative function is the density, accumulated from the left"
/>

It is the Riemann sum you already met, with one difference: the upper limit is now the variable. Cut the sweep into $n$ strips of width $\Delta t$, take the density at each strip's midpoint, and add:

$$F(x) \;\approx\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(t_i)\,\Delta t \;\xrightarrow[\;n \to \infty\;]{}\; \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t$$

Push $x$ to the right and every strip you have already added stays added; you are only ever appending. That is why $F$ never decreases, why it leaves zero at the left end and arrives at one at the right, and why an upper tail is simply what is left over: $P(X \ge x) = 1 - F(x)$.

**Differentiate the accumulation and the density comes back.** Nudge the upper limit by $\mathrm{d}x$ and you add one more sliver of area, of height $f(x)$ and width $\mathrm{d}x$. So the rate at which the total grows *is* the height of the curve there:

<Formula
  tex="F'(x) = f(x)"
  terms={{ "F'(x)": 'how fast the running total is climbing at x — the slope of the CDF', 'f(x)': 'the density at x — the height of the curve' }}
  label="The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, stated without ceremony"
/>

Which is the whole explanation of the shape of a CDF. It is steepest where the density is tallest, because that is where each new strip contributes most; it is flat in the tails, because out there the strips have almost no height and the total has stopped moving. Accumulate a density and you get $F$. Differentiate $F$ and you get the density back.

**The inverse runs the accumulation backwards.** An inverse-normal question hands you the area and asks for the place you must have stopped to have collected it: given $q$, find the $x$ with $F(x) = q$.

<Formula
  tex="x_q = F^{-1}(q) \quad\text{where}\quad F(x_q) = q"
  terms={{ 'q': 'the area that must lie below the cutoff', 'x_q': 'the only value that accumulates exactly that much', 'F^{-1}': 'the inverse of the accumulation — area in, value out' }}
  label="Backwards: the area is given, the cutoff is wanted"
/>

The only property this needs is that $F$ is strictly increasing: each step to the right adds a positive slice of area, so no two cutoffs collect the same total, and the $x$ that produces an area $q$ exists and is the only one. That is all the calculator is using when it turns 0.99 into a number of days. Nothing about normality, nothing about symmetry — just a total that only ever goes up.

Run both experiments below. Drag the cutoff and watch one quantity in two places: the shaded area on the density is the height of the dot on the running total. Then push **accumulation strips** from 4 to 200 and read *strip sum − F(x) · the error* — the rectangles never become the curve, they converge on it, and the error is a number that shrinks rather than a promise. Then look at the second readout row: *slope of F at x* beside *f(x) · normal.pdf*, the Fundamental Theorem as two numbers that agree.

**Why this matters here.** Every inverse-normal cutoff this watch — the delay only one honest transit in a hundred exceeds, the percentile Ferrier wants at the top of the brief — is this accumulation read backwards, and the machine can only read it backwards because the total never turns around. And knowing that it is an accumulation is what keeps *the area above 6.4 days* from being confused with *the height of the curve at 6.4 days*, which is not a probability and never was.
