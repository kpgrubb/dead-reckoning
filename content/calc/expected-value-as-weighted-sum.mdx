One word, *mean*, covers two operations, and the difference is the whole of this briefing.

The data mean of Act I added the values and divided by how many. Every observation counted once, because every observation was one observation.

An expected value averages a **distribution**. Its values are not equally common, so they cannot count equally. Each one is weighted by how much probability sits on it:

<Formula
  tex="\mu_X = E(X) = \sum_{x} x \cdot p(x)"
  terms={{ x: 'one value the random variable can take — a cost in sink-hours', 'p(x)': 'how much of the total probability sits on that value', '\\sum': 'over every value in the support; the weights sum to 1' }}
  label="Expected value: a weighted sum"

The weights summing to 1 is what makes the result an average rather than a sum. The data case is the special case: give each of $n$ observations a weight of $1/n$ and the weighted sum collapses to $\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$, which is where you started.

**Finer grain.** Now let the variable be continuous. There is no list of values to run over, so cut the axis into cells of width $\Delta x$. The probability of the cell at $x$ is about $f(x)\,\Delta x$, so the same weighted sum reads

$$\sum_{i} x_i \, f(x_i)\,\Delta x \;\xrightarrow[\;\Delta x \to 0\;]{}\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

which is the definition of the mean for a density. Nothing new is introduced at the limit. The integral **is** the weighted sum; the cells have simply stopped being wide enough to be wrong. The same substitution turns every other weighted sum in this topic into an integral: $\sum p(x) = 1$ becomes $\int f = 1$, and $\sum (x - \mu)^2 p(x)$ becomes $\int (x-\mu)^2 f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$, which is the variance.

**What the weighting is for.** Two readings sit on the same table and neither is the expected value.

- The **unweighted** mean of the possible values. Average the numbers in the left-hand column and you have asserted that a 78-hour window and a 54-hour window are equally common. The p(x) column exists to say how common each one is, and the unweighted figure ignores it: change every probability in the table and that number does not move.
- The **most likely** value. The single tallest bar is the mode. On a symmetric distribution it lands where the mean lands and teaches you nothing; on a skewed one the two are far apart. A geometric wait has its mode at trial 1 and its mean at $1/p$.

Build the sum below one term at a time and watch the running total land on $\mu$. Then halve the cell width and watch it land on the same place with twice as many terms.

**Why this matters here.** The three loiter points differ in their means by a handful of sink-hours and in their tails by a factor of forty, and both figures come off the same column of weights. The mean is a weighted sum you can do on a slate. The tail is the weights past a capacity, added. What the mean promises is an average over many windows; about the next window it promises nothing at all.
