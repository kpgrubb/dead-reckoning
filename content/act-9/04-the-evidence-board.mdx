<Briefing title="Choosing the procedure, and judging a body of evidence" topics={["9.6"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
By the end of Unit 9 there are roughly a dozen procedures available, and almost every exam question that asks you to pick one can be settled by three readings of the design. The subject matter is never the clue: fuel, delay and output each appear under proportions, means and slopes depending on what was recorded.

**Reading 1 — what was recorded on each unit?**

- A yes/no outcome → **proportions**.
- One quantitative measurement → **means**.
- A category label → **counts**, which means $\chi^2$.
- Two quantitative measurements → **regression**, which means a slope.

**Reading 2 — how many samples, and were their sizes fixed in advance?**

- One sample against a published value → one-sample procedure.
- Two samples drawn separately → two-sample procedure.
- Two measurements on the *same* unit → **paired**, and the data are one column of differences.
- One sample classified two ways → $\chi^2$ **independence**.
- Several samples compared on one categorical variable → $\chi^2$ **homogeneity**.

**Reading 3 — does the question want an estimate or a verdict?** "How much" is an interval. "Is it this value" is a test. An interval can settle a claim about a stated value as well, and it reports the precision while it does so.

Two answers are not a procedure at all, and both are earned rather than defaulted to.

- **Simulate the null.** When an expected count falls below five, the $\chi^2$ curve is not the sampling distribution of that statistic, and no significance level repairs it. Shuffle the labels a few thousand times and count.
- **Describe.** When the file holds every unit the question is about, there is no sampling variability for a P-value to describe. The answer is counts, percentages and a sentence.

**Communicating one finding.** Any finding that will be read by somebody else needs five parts, and the fifth is the one that gets dropped.

1. The question, with the population named.
2. The procedure, and why the design calls for it.
3. The conditions, with their arithmetic shown.
4. The conclusion, in context, in the units of the parameter.
5. **The limit**: what this finding does not establish.

A finding written without part 5 is read as a claim about everything, and the reader supplies the bound you declined to write. A limit the author conceded reads as rigour; the identical limit extracted under questioning reads as concealment.

**Judging a body of evidence.** Several findings do not combine into one number, and the two attempts at making them are both wrong.

- **Multiplying P-values** requires independence, and findings drawn from overlapping records are not independent. It also produces the wrong kind of quantity: a P-value is the probability of data under a hypothesis, never the probability that a hypothesis is true, and no arithmetic on a set of them turns one into the other.
- **Quoting the smallest** is worse, because the smallest result in a set of tests is the one most likely to have arrived by looking. A procedure that runs several tests and reports the best of them has an error rate nothing like the level printed beside it.

What a body of evidence is actually judged on is a list of questions, each answered in a sentence. Do the findings point the same way? Was each procedure the one its design called for? Did the conditions hold, and where did they pass narrowly? Do the findings rest on independent sources, or on one archive? And the one that carries the most weight: **is there a single innocent explanation that accounts for all of them at once?** A finding constructed so that no innocent account predicts it is worth more than three findings that an innocent account predicts comfortably.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
One finding in all five parts: the fuel regression on the nineteen diverted departures.

**1. The question.** For the {fmtInt(DIVERTED_N)} diverted Perrine departures, how many tonnes of fuel went into the tanks per tonne the filed profile required?

**2. The procedure.** Each departure carries two quantitative columns, the fuel required and the fuel pumped, and the question asks for a rate with its precision. That is a **t-interval for the slope** of a population regression model, on $n - 2$ = {fmtInt(NINETEEN_FUEL_INTERVAL.df)} degrees of freedom.

**3. The conditions.** Linear: the residual plot shows no curve. Independent: {fmtInt(DIVERTED_N)} separate certifications from {fmtInt(DIVERTED_N)} separate voyages. Normal: a normal probability plot of the residuals is close enough to straight at this sample size. Equal SD: **this one is narrow**. The residual spread grows with the size of the hull, because fuel is measured in tonnes and a larger hull's tonnes of anything vary more. Random: the departures are the whole of one owner's diverted traffic rather than a random sample, which is the limit in line 5.

**4. The conclusion.** The fitted slope is {fmt(NINETEEN_FUEL_FIT.slope, 3)} tonnes per tonne required, with standard error {fmt(NINETEEN_FUEL_FIT.seSlope, 3)}. We are {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} confident that the true slope for these departures is between {fmt(fuelLo, 3)} and {fmt(fuelHi, 3)} tonnes of fuel per tonne required. Every value in that interval is above 1.00, so the interval excludes the requirement: these hulls were fuelled above what their own filed profiles called for, by roughly {fmtPct(NINETEEN_FUEL_FIT.slope - 1, 0)}.

**5. The limit.** The {fmtInt(DIVERTED_N)} are the diverted departures, not a random sample of departures, so this describes those {fmtInt(DIVERTED_N)} and generalises to nothing. The Equal SD condition is met loosely rather than comfortably. And nothing here establishes who authorised the loading, or why.

*The same five parts for the slope that closed the case.* Procedure: a **t-test for the slope**, run twice. Against $\beta = 0$, which is what every innocent account of the diversion predicts, $t$ = {fmt(KETTLE_TEST_ZERO.statistic, 2)} on {fmtInt(KETTLE_TEST_ZERO.df)} degrees of freedom, one-sided. Against the rocket equation's marginal fuel fraction at the thrust-limited Lane burn, two-sided, the test fails to reject. Conclusion: among those {fmtInt(DIVERTED_N)} departures, excess fuel per true tonne rises with the Kettle delta-v on the departure date, at a rate consistent with the physics of braking into Kettle. Limit: it locates the arithmetic at the berth and it names nobody.

Two findings, two procedures, two limits. What makes the pair worth more than either alone is **reading 5** above: the first is comfortably explained by undeclared freight, and the second is explained by nothing innocent at all.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Choosing the procedure from the subject matter.* Fuel appears in this Act as a proportion, as a mean, as a count in a cargo table and as a slope. What decides is the design, every time.

*A $\chi^2$ on banded quantitative data.* Two measurements per unit can always be cut into categories, and the resulting table always returns a P-value. It answers a smaller question than the data support, it discards most of the information, and the statistic depends on where somebody put the cut points.

*Overclaiming cause.* Random **assignment** licenses a causal claim; random **sampling** licenses a generalisation. An archive supplies neither by itself, and a small P-value supplies neither at all.

*Stacking P-values.* No arithmetic on a set of P-values produces the probability that a case is right. Judge the findings one at a time, then ask whether one innocent account explains all of them together.

*Leaving the limit line blank.* The unbounded version of a finding is the version a hostile review answers, and it is always the stronger version to attack.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

<LogEntry
  title="The design chooses the procedure, and the limit line is part of the finding"
  concept="A procedure is chosen from three readings of the design and never from the subject matter: what was recorded on each unit (yes/no for proportions, a measurement for means, a label for counts, two measurements for a slope), how many samples there were and whether their sizes were fixed in advance, and whether the question wants an estimate or a verdict.
