<Briefing title="Testing a slope: hypotheses, the statistic, and what a small P-value buys" topics={["9.4", "9.5"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A slope test asks one question: **is the population slope $\beta$ equal to some stated value?** Everything else is bookkeeping.

**The hypotheses.** They are always about the parameter, never about the fitted line:

$$H_0: \beta = \beta_0 \qquad H_a: \beta > \beta_0, \quad \beta < \beta_0, \quad \text{or} \quad \beta \neq \beta_0$$

$b$ is already known once the regression has run, so there is nothing to hypothesise about it. $\beta$ is the slope of the true line $\mu_y = \alpha + \beta x$ for the population the sample came from, and the test asks how surprising the observed $b$ would be if the null were true.

**The null value.** Most of the time $\beta_0 = 0$, because a slope of zero means the response does not change with the explanatory variable: no linear relationship. That is a special case and not a rule. When the science, a contract or a specification fixes what the rate has to be, that value is the null, and zero never enters the problem. A relay net's delay per AU is fixed by the speed of light. A hull's fuel per tonne required is fixed by the requirement. The marginal fuel fraction per extra kilometre per second of braking is fixed by the rocket equation.

**The direction.** The alternative is chosen from the question, before the data are seen. Choosing it afterwards so that it points the same way as $b$ is not a one-sided test; it is a two-sided test with the P-value quietly halved.

**Conditions.** Identical to the interval's: Linear, Independent, Normal residuals, Equal SD, Random. All five are judged on the residual plot, the normal probability plot of the residuals, and the sentence describing how the data were collected. A P-value computed on a fit whose conditions fail is a number with nothing behind it.

**The statistic.** The same shape as every $t$ statistic in the course: how many standard errors the estimate sits from the null value.

$$t = \frac{b - \beta_0}{SE(b)}, \qquad df = n - 2$$

Both ingredients come off the output, from the **Coef** and **SE Coef** columns of the predictor's row. The degrees of freedom are $n - 2$ because two parameters were estimated to place the line.

**The P-value, and the one thing output gets wrong.** Software prints a $T$ and a $P$ beside every coefficient, and they are always the same test: **two-sided, against zero.** Three cases follow.

- The question is two-sided against zero. The printed $P$ is the answer.
- The question is one-sided against zero. The answer is **half** the printed $P$ — and only when $b$ falls on the side the alternative claims. When it falls the other way the one-tailed area is $1 - P/2$, which will be larger than a half, and the data are evidence against the alternative rather than for it.
- The question is about a **non-zero** null. The printed $T$ and $P$ cannot be used at all. The null is in the numerator of $t$, so a different null is a different statistic, and it has to be rebuilt from Coef and SE Coef by hand.

**What a small P-value buys, and what it does not.** It buys one sentence: the data would be surprising if $\beta$ were $\beta_0$, so there is convincing evidence that it is not. It does not buy a cause, because observational data describe association and a confounding variable produces the same slope. It does not buy a size, because a P-value is a verdict on one value of $\beta$ and an interval is what says how big the slope is. And it does not buy a wider population than the one the sample was drawn from.
</Briefing.Intuition>

*The statistic the question wants.*

*What that conclusion stops short of.* It does not say why the stand reads high, it does not extend to any other yard's stand, and it says nothing about whether {fmtPct(standFit.slope - 1, 1)} matters to anybody. The last of those is a question for an interval and for a fitter.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*The printed P answers a question you did not ask.* Every statistics package tests every coefficient against zero, two-sided, because it has no idea what the analyst wanted. Before using the $P$ column, say out loud what null it was computed against and whether that is yours.

*Halving has a condition attached.* A one-sided P is half the printed value **when $b$ lies on the side the alternative claims**. When it does not, the one-tailed area is the complement, and a P-value above a half is the signal that this has happened.

*A significant slope is not a cause.* Nothing in the arithmetic distinguishes a relationship produced by one variable driving another from one produced by a third variable driving both. Random assignment is what separates them, and an archive has none.

*Significance is not size.* A huge sample makes a trivial slope significant and a small one hides a large slope. The test reports whether $\beta$ is plausibly $\beta_0$; the interval reports how far from it $\beta$ might be.

*Failing to reject is not confirmation.* A test against a physical value that comes back short leaves that value standing alongside every other value the interval would have contained. It is consistency, which is weaker than agreement and still worth reporting.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
