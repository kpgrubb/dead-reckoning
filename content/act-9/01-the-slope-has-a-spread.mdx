<Briefing title="The population regression model, and how far a fitted slope moves" topics={["9.1"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Regression inference rests on a model of the process, not on a picture of the data.

**The population regression model.** For every value of the explanatory variable $x$ there is a whole population of possible response values. The model says three things about it:

1. Its mean sits on a straight line: $\mu_y = \alpha + \beta x$.
2. Its standard deviation is the same $\sigma$ at every $x$.
3. Its shape is approximately Normal.

So $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\sigma$ are **parameters**. They are properties of the process that produced the data, and nobody ever sees them. What a sample gives you is a fitted line $\hat{y} = a + bx$, and a residual standard deviation $s$. Those three are **statistics**: $a$ estimates $\alpha$, $b$ estimates $\beta$, $s$ estimates $\sigma$.

**The slope is a statistic, so it has a sampling distribution.** Draw a new sample from the same process, fit a new line, and $b$ comes out different. Do it many times and those slopes pile up into a distribution with three properties worth memorising:

- it is centred at $\beta$, the true slope, so $b$ is unbiased;
- its standard deviation is $\sigma / (\sigma_x \sqrt{n})$;
- it is approximately Normal, which is why a $t$ procedure works on it.

Read the SD formula for what it says about design. Three things narrow it. A bigger sample. A smaller $\sigma$, meaning a process with less scatter about its own line. And a larger $\sigma_x$: **spreading the x-values out further makes the slope easier to pin down**, because a line is easier to aim through a long lever than a short one. That last one has no analogue in one-variable inference and it is the one people forget.

**Standard error.** In practice $\sigma$ and $\sigma_x$ are unknown, so the SD of $b$ is estimated from the sample. The estimate is the **standard error of the slope**, and every piece of statistical software prints it in a column beside the slope:

$$SE(b) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(y - \hat{y})^2}{n-2}}, \qquad S_{xx} = \sum(x - \bar{x})^2$$

The $n - 2$ is not decoration. Two parameters were estimated from the data to draw the line, so two degrees of freedom were spent, and every $t$ procedure about a slope runs on $df = n - 2$.

**Conditions.** They are usually remembered as **LINE** plus Random, and every one of them is a statement about the *residuals* or about the *design*:

- **L**inear: the residual plot shows no curved pattern.
- **I**ndependent: the observations are independent of one another, and if sampling without replacement, $n \leq 10\%$ of the population.
- **N**ormal: the residuals are approximately Normal: a dotplot or histogram of them with no strong skew and no outlier, or a normal probability plot close to straight.
- **E**qual SD: the residual plot's vertical spread is about the same all the way across, with no fan.
- **Random**: the data come from a random sample or a randomized experiment.

Nothing on that list is a statement about the response variable on its own. A histogram of $y$ is allowed to be any shape at all: it mixes together populations at many different $x$ values, and the model never claimed it was Normal.
</Briefing.Intuition>

That is the whole content of a standard error: **how far this statistic usually lands from the parameter.**

*Degrees of freedom.* {fmtInt(loopN)} runs, two parameters fitted, $df$ = {fmtInt(loopFit.n - 2)}. Not {fmtInt(loopN - 1)}.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Conditions are checked on the residuals, and a graph of the response is the wrong graph.* The model makes no claim about the overall shape of $y$: the responses at large $x$ are supposed to be centred somewhere else from the responses at small $x$, so pooling them into one histogram and calling it skewed says nothing about whether the procedure is valid. Fit the line first, then plot what is left over.

*A slope is not fixed just because the data are.* The nineteen numbers in front of you will not change, and the slope through them is still a statistic. The randomness is in which nineteen the process produced, not in the arithmetic afterwards.

*$SE(b)$ is not $s$.* Software prints both, in different places, and they measure different things. $s$ is how far a single observation typically sits from the line, in the units of $y$. $SE(b)$ is how far a fitted slope typically sits from the true slope, in the units of $y$ per unit of $x$. The second is built out of the first, divided by $\sqrt{S_{xx}}$, and on a well-spread set of $x$ values it is very much the smaller number.

*Degrees of freedom are $n - 2$, always.* One for the intercept, one for the slope. A $t^\star$ or a P-value taken from $n - 1$ is wrong in the safe direction for large $n$ and meaningfully wrong for small $n$, which is where slope problems tend to live.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
