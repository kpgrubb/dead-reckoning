<Briefing title="The distribution of nothing happening" topics={["5.5", "5.6"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A loss rate is a mean in disguise. Score every transit 1 if the hull is lost and 0 if it arrives; the sample proportion $\hat p$ is the average of those $n$ zeros and ones, which means everything from act-5-03 applies to it without amendment — centre, spread, shape, all of it. The only thing that changes is that a Bernoulli parent's standard deviation is not a free parameter. It is fixed by $p$ itself: $\sigma = \sqrt{p(1-p)}$. Put that into $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ and the standard deviation of the sample proportion drops out with no new theory in it.

**Centre.** $\mu_{\hat p} = p$. Over all samples of size $n$, the sample proportion lands on the true rate. It is unbiased, and it stays unbiased at every $n$ — which, as act-5-01 established the hard way, is a different property from being *precise*.

**Spread.** $\sigma_{\hat p} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. Note which $p$ is in there: the **true** rate assumed by the model, not the one you just measured. When a rate is given to you — the Authority's published Lane figure, the Board's baseline — the model is built on the given rate, and substituting your own observed $\hat p$ is answering a different question with the same arithmetic.

**Shape** needs a licence, and for proportions it is not $n \ge 30$. A proportion lives between 0 and 1, and when $p$ is small the distribution is jammed up against the floor and skewed right no matter how large $n$ is. What matters is whether there is room on both sides — whether the *counts*, not the sample size, are big enough:

**Large Counts:** $np \ge 10$ **and** $n(1-p) \ge 10$.

On the Lane, with a rate near one per cent, that condition bites. It takes several hundred transits before the expected number of losses reaches ten at all, and on a fleet of {fmtInt(SMALL_N)} transits the expected count is {fmt(SMALL_COUNTS, 2)} — the sampling distribution is real, but it is lumpy and right-skewed and a normal curve laid over it is a lie told with a smooth line. Quote the counts. Never quote the verdict alone.

**Independence** is the second condition and it is about where the $n$ came from. The textbook form is the **10 % condition**: when sampling *without replacement* from a finite population, take no more than a tenth of it, or the draws start informing each other. Here the population is not a bin. A fleet's transits are draws from an ongoing operation that will keep flying the Lane next year and the year after; the Ledger's {fmtInt(LEDGER_N)} rows are a window on a process, not the whole of it. That is why nine hundred transits — {fmtPct(PERRINE_SHARE, 0)} of the rows in the snapshot — does not break independence, and it is the sentence a reviewer will want to see written out rather than assumed.

**Two fleets.** Under a *common* rate the difference $\hat p_1 - \hat p_2$ is centred on **zero** — both fleets are estimating the same number, so their difference estimates nothing. Its spread is the one line in this Act that people get wrong in writing, in print, in evidence:

Variances add. Always, for independent quantities, and for a difference exactly as much as for a sum, because subtracting a random quantity is adding a random quantity that has been flipped, and flipping it does not make it quieter. The standard deviation of the difference is therefore *larger than either one alone* — never the difference of the two standard deviations, which is smaller than both and is a claim that two noisy fleets somehow make a quiet comparison.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\mu_{\hat p} = p, \qquad \sigma_{\hat p} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}" terms={{ '\\hat p': 'the sample proportion — losses divided by transits, in one sample', 'p': 'the TRUE rate the model assumes, never the one just observed', 'n': 'transits in the sample' }} label="Centre and spread of a sample proportion" />

<Formula tex="np \ge 10 \quad\text{and}\quad n(1-p) \ge 10 \qquad\text{(Large Counts)}" terms={{ 'np': 'expected losses — print this number, not the verdict', 'n(1-p)': 'expected arrivals' }} label="The condition that licenses a normal model for p̂" />

<Formula tex="\mu_{\hat p_1 - \hat p_2} = p_1 - p_2, \qquad \sigma_{\hat p_1 - \hat p_2} = \sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}}" terms={{ 'p_1 - p_2': 'zero under a common rate — the difference estimates nothing', '+': 'the whole lesson: a plus sign, under the root, for a difference' }} label="Difference in two sample proportions — variances add" />
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Take two classes with nothing at stake between them. The Ledger's **Mercantile** hulls flew {fmtInt(MERCANTILE_N)} Lane transits over the six years and the **independents** flew {fmtInt(INDEPENDENT_N)}. Assume both fly at the Authority's published Lane rate, $p$ = {fmt(POOLED_RATE, 5)} — {fmtPct(POOLED_RATE, 2)} — which is the model in which nothing whatever is going on.

**One fleet, first.** For the Mercantile hulls:

<Formula tex={`\\sigma_{\\hat p} = \\sqrt{\\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \\sqrt{\\frac{ ${fmt(POOLED_RATE, 5)} \\times ${fmt(1 - POOLED_RATE, 5)} }{ ${fmtInt(MERCANTILE_N)} }} = ${fmt(SD_MERCANTILE, 5)}`} label="One fleet's loss rate, at the published Lane rate" />

Conditions before the number is used for anything: $np$ = {fmt(COUNTS_MERCANTILE, 2)} and $n(1-p)$ = {fmt(ARRIVALS_MERCANTILE, 1)}, both at least ten, so a normal model is licensed — narrowly on the loss side, comfortably on the other, which is exactly the state of every proportion argument on this corridor.

**Then the difference.** The independents are the smaller fleet, so they are the noisier one: their $\sigma_{\hat p}$ is {fmt(SD_INDEPENDENT, 5)}. Combine them:

<Formula tex={`\\sigma_{\\hat p_1 - \\hat p_2} = \\sqrt{ ${fmt(SD_MERCANTILE, 5)}^2 + ${fmt(SD_INDEPENDENT, 5)}^2 } = ${fmt(SD_DIFF_WORKED, 5)}`} label="Variances add — and the result is wider than either piece" />

Larger than either one, as it must be. The wrong operation gives {fmt(SD_MERCANTILE, 5)} − {fmt(SD_INDEPENDENT, 5)} = {fmt(SD_DIFF_WRONG, 5)} — a **negative** standard deviation, which is at least frank about what sort of error it is; write the two fleets down in the other order and it comes out positive and about {fmt(SD_DIFF_WRONG_RATIO, 0)} times too small, which is the version that gets published.

**Forwards.** Suppose somebody reports the Mercantile hulls running {fmtPct(WORKED_GAP, 1)} above the independents. Under the common-rate model:

<Formula tex={`z = \\frac{ ${fmt(WORKED_GAP, 4)} - 0 }{ ${fmt(SD_DIFF_WORKED, 5)} } = ${fmt(WORKED_Z, 3)} \\qquad\\Longrightarrow\\qquad P(\\hat p_1 - \\hat p_2 \\ge ${fmt(WORKED_GAP, 3)}) = ${fmtP(WORKED_P)}`} label="A hypothetical gap, standardized against the noise floor" />

About {fmtPct(WORKED_P, 1)} of pairs of identical fleets this size land that far apart or further, by luck alone, with no difference between them of any kind.

**Backwards** is the direction this watch actually needs, and it is the same machine turned round. Instead of *here is a gap, how rare is it*, ask *here is a rarity, how big a gap does it take*. Pick the tail area, find the $z^\star$ that cuts it off, multiply by the spread. An area of one in a hundred in one tail gives a $z^\star$ of {fmt(normal.isf(0.01), 4)}, and the gap is $z^\star \sigma$ — a number in loss proportion that you can write down *before* you have looked at anything, which is the only time it is worth anything.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Using $\hat p$ in the standard deviation when $p$ is given.* The model's spread is built from the model's rate. If the Authority publishes {fmtPct(POOLED_RATE, 2)} and you are asking what that rate would produce, it is $p$ = {fmt(POOLED_RATE, 5)} that goes under the root. Substituting the rate you observed builds the null model out of the observation, which is the exact error Ferrier stopped with two fingers on a slate — and it is worth knowing that Act VI will *deliberately* pool the two observed counts to estimate a common rate, for a specific reason, under a stated hypothesis. That is a different procedure with a different justification. It is not this one.

*"$n$ is big, so it's normal."* Not for proportions. $n = 2{,}000$ at a rate of 0.002 gives four expected losses and a distribution that looks like a picket fence leaning on the axis. It is $np$ and $n(1-p)$ that decide, and when the rate is near one per cent the loss side is the one that fails. Print both counts.

*Subtracting standard deviations.* $\sigma_1 - \sigma_2$ for a difference is not a rounding error, it is a different universe: it says that comparing two uncertain fleets is more precise than measuring either. Variances add — under the root, a plus sign, every time. If your answer for a difference is smaller than the SD of one of the pieces, you have made this mistake and the display will tell you so in red.

*Forgetting what the 10 % condition is for.* It is not a ritual. It licenses treating the draws as independent when you are scooping from a finite bin. Transits on a working corridor are not scoops from a bin, and saying so in one sentence is cheaper than having a reviewer say it for you.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

Ebele builds it as a machine rather than as a formula, because he has stopped trusting formulas he has not watched run. **ONE FLEET** draws a fleet of $n$ transits at a true rate $p$, counts the losses, divides, and puts one dot on the stack. Do it five hundred times and the pile that grows is the sampling distribution of a loss rate.

Press RUN a few times and read `√(p(1 − p)/n) · the theory` against `SD of the simulated p̂ · the machine`. The moment it does, the normal curve is withdrawn from the plot and the readout turns red — the sampling distribution is still there, the pile is still a pile, but the smooth model over the top of it has lost its licence and the instrument will not draw a line it cannot defend.
