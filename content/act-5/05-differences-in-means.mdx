<Briefing title="Why the small group sets the precision" topics={["5.8"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Two independent groups, two sample means, one difference. Each mean has the sampling distribution act-5-03 built for it: centre $\mu$, spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. The difference of the two is a new random quantity with a sampling distribution of its own, and there is nothing new in it except the bookkeeping.

**Centre.** $\mu_{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2} = \mu_1 - \mu_2$. Under a null model in which both groups fly the same corridor at the same average lateness, that is **zero**. The difference estimates nothing, and it is centred on nothing.

**Spread.** Variances add — the same sentence as act-5-04, for the same reason, and it is worth being exact about the reason rather than taking it as a rule. For independent $A$ and $B$,

$$\operatorname{Var}(A - B) = \operatorname{Var}(A) + (-1)^2\operatorname{Var}(B) = \operatorname{Var}(A) + \operatorname{Var}(B)$$

The minus sign is squared out of existence. It has to be: variance measures *spread*, and flipping a quantity end for end does not make it steadier. Two groups, each wobbling on its own, produce a difference that wobbles more than either — never less. Since $\operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = \sigma^2/n$,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar x_1 - \bar x_2) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}, \qquad \sigma_{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}$$

**And the Chief's point, which is the one worth carrying out of this compartment.** Those two terms are almost never comparable in size. Each is a variance divided by its own count, so a large group's term is *small* and a small group's term is *large*, and the total is dominated by whichever group is short of data. Put {fmtInt(NINETEEN_N)} against {fmtInt(SURVIVING_PERRINE_N)} with a common $\sigma$ and the nineteen supply {fmtPct(SHARE_SMALL, 1)} of the variance of the comparison. Adding hulls to the big side buys essentially nothing. The small group sets the precision, and no amount of data anywhere else repairs it — which is why "we have thousands of transits" is never on its own an answer to "how well can you see?"

**Conditions**, and they are per group:

- **Independent groups** — the two samples must not be the same hulls counted twice, and nothing in one may determine anything in the other. This is the condition that licenses the addition itself, and it is the one that fails silently.
- **Shape**, group by group: each parent approximately normal, *or* that group's $n$ at least 30. With {fmtInt(NINETEEN_N)} on one side the second licence is closed, so the argument stands entirely on the first — and it is available here, because the Ledger's honest delays have skewness {fmt(LANE_SKEW, 3)}, which act-5-02 checked on a probability plot before anyone quoted anything.
- **10 %** per group, in the process sense act-5-04 set out: each group's transits are draws from a corridor that keeps running.

**Pooling.** When both groups are assumed to share one population standard deviation — as they are under a common-corridor null — the formula simplifies to $\sigma\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$, which is the same number written more compactly. That is a *consequence of the assumption*, not a licence to make it. Assuming a common $\sigma$ when the two groups plainly have different spreads understates the uncertainty of whichever comparison the analyst preferred, and Act VII will meet an officer who does exactly that with a straight face and a rank.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
The two cutters, because they are the one comparison in this Act with nothing riding on it. *Asgard* and *Tindr* each timed about **{fmtInt(PATROL_N)} transits** to Mark 9 during their patrols. Assume both watched honest Lane traffic: $\sigma$ = {fmt(LANE_DELAY_SD, 3)} d on each side, and a common mean, so the difference is centred on zero.

Each patrol's mean has standard error

<Formula tex={`\\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}} = \\frac{ ${fmt(LANE_DELAY_SD, 3)} }{ \\sqrt{ ${fmtInt(PATROL_N)} } } = ${fmt(SE_PATROL, 4)} \\text{ d}`} label="One patrol's mean delay" />

and the difference between them is wider than either:

<Formula tex={`\\sigma_{\\bar x_1 - \\bar x_2} = \\sqrt{ \\frac{ ${fmt(LANE_DELAY_SD, 3)}^2 }{ ${fmtInt(PATROL_N)} } + \\frac{ ${fmt(LANE_DELAY_SD, 3)}^2 }{ ${fmtInt(PATROL_N)} } } = ${fmt(SD_DIFF_PATROLS, 4)} \\text{ d}`} label="The gap between two equal patrols" />

Exactly $\sqrt{2}$ times one patrol's standard error, because the two groups are the same size — the only case in which the arithmetic is that tidy, and the reason it is worth seeing once before meeting a case that is not.

**Forwards.** Suppose the two cutters' mean delays differ by **{fmt(WORKED_GAP, 1)} d**. Under the common-mean model:

<Formula tex={`z = \\frac{ ${fmt(WORKED_GAP, 2)} - 0 }{ ${fmt(SD_DIFF_PATROLS, 4)} } = ${fmt(WORKED_Z, 3)} \\qquad\\Longrightarrow\\qquad P(\\bar x_1 - \\bar x_2 \\ge ${fmt(WORKED_GAP, 1)}) = ${fmtP(WORKED_P)}`} label="A hypothetical gap between two honest patrols" />

So {fmtPct(WORKED_P, 1)} of pairs of honest patrols this size differ by {fmt(WORKED_GAP, 1)} days or more in that direction — about one pair in {fmtInt(Math.round(1 / WORKED_P))}. Unremarkable, and two ships that saw the same corridor would report it without either of them being wrong.

**Backwards** is the direction the brief needs. Fix the rarity, find the gap: a one-in-a-thousand event in one tail has a $z^\star$ of {fmt(Z_1_IN_1000, 4)}, and the gap is $z^\star \sigma_{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}$. For these two patrols that comes to {fmt(Z_1_IN_1000 * SD_DIFF_PATROLS, 3)} d — a bar drawn before any measurement, which is the only kind worth drawing.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Subtracting variances, or subtracting standard errors.* Both produce a number smaller than either piece. For the lopsided comparison in this module the honest spread of the difference between {fmtInt(NINETEEN_N)} and {fmtInt(SURVIVING_PERRINE_N)} is {fmt(SD_DIFF_MEANS_SMALL, 3)} d; subtracting the two standard errors instead of adding their squares gives {fmt(WRONG_SUBTRACTED, 3)} d, which is *less than measuring the nineteen on their own* — {fmt(SE_19_ALONE, 3)} d. Think about what that claims: that comparing a badly-measured group against a well-measured one is more precise than measuring the badly-measured group by itself. Information does not appear because you wrote a minus sign.

*Reading "n = 900" as "this comparison is precise."* The comparison is only as good as its worst-served group. Check which term dominates before quoting any figure: here $\sigma^2/n$ for the nineteen is {fmt(VAR_SMALL / VAR_BIG, 0)} times the other group's, so the comparison is a measurement of the nineteen with a well-known constant subtracted from it.

*Pooling without justification.* A common $\sigma$ is an assumption about the world, and it has to be argued from the data or from the mechanism. Making it because it tidies the formula — or because it happens to shrink the standard error under the comparison you would rather win — is how a two-sample analysis is made to say whatever its author wanted.

*Treating a null model as a test.* Everything here is computed under the assumption that nothing is wrong. It says what honest looks like and how wide honest is. It does not say whether what the ship observed lies inside it; no hypothesis has been stated, no decision rule written, and no observed difference has been standardized against any of this. That is Act VI's work and it is deliberately not this module's.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

*A comparison against a baseline is only as good as the baseline, and the baseline is the Board's to choose. A comparison of one owner's hulls against everyone else's — same corridor, same file, same six years, on both sides of the comparison — is not.*

It does not need anybody's permission to pick a corridor, because the corridor is identical on both sides and cancels out of the question entirely.
