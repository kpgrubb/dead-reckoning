<Briefing title="A mean is steadier than anything it is made of" topics={["5.3", "5.7"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
There are three distributions in this compartment and the whole module is the discipline of never confusing them.

**The population.** Every honest transit's Mark-9 delay: {fmtInt(honestDelays.length)} rows in the Ledger, centred on {fmt(LANE_DELAY_MEAN, 3)} days, spread {fmt(LANE_DELAY_SD, 3)} days about that. It is a fact about drives and filing habits. Nothing you do with a sample changes it by a hair.

**One sample.** Nineteen of those rows. That is data. You can plot it, average it, and be wrong about it.

**The sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$.** The distribution of the *average of nineteen*, over every sample of nineteen the Lane could have handed you. It is not data. Nobody has ever observed one; you get exactly one sample in a career, and the sampling distribution is the thing that sample was drawn out of. It is built by theory and checked by simulation, and it is the only object in the room that can tell you whether an average of nineteen is surprising.

Two facts about it, and they hold for **every** population, of every shape, at every sample size:

- **Its centre does not move.** $\mu_{\bar x} = \mu$. Averaging is not a lens; it does not shift anything. Over repeated samples $\bar{x}$ lands on the population mean, which is what makes it an unbiased estimator.
- **Its spread shrinks — as $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, not $\sigma/n$.** A high draw and a low draw in the same sample cancel one another, so averages scatter less than individuals do. *How much* less comes out of the variance: the variances of $n$ independent draws add to $n\sigma^2$, and the averaging divides by $n$ twice, once in each factor, leaving $\sigma^2/n$. Take the square root and the standard deviation of $\bar{x}$ is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Four times the transits, half the spread. A hundred times the transits, one tenth.

The third fact — the **shape** — is the only one that needs a licence, and there are exactly two:

1. **The parent is itself approximately normal.** Then $\bar{x}$ is normal at *any* $n$, including 2, including 19. No rule of thumb required.
2. **$n \ge 30$.** Then the **Central Limit Theorem** does the work whatever the parent looks like: flat, skewed, two-humped, or nothing but ones and zeros.

The Lane's argument runs at nineteen, so the second licence is closed and the first is the one carrying the weight. It is available: the Ledger's honest delays have skewness {fmt(HONEST_SKEW, 3)}, symmetric and single-peaked, which is exactly what act-5-02 checked on a probability plot before anybody quoted a normal probability. That check is not a formality. It is the reason a number computed at nineteen is defensible at all, and a hostile reviewer will ask for it first.

What the theorem **says**: as $n$ grows, the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ approaches a normal shape, centred on $\mu$, with standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, *whatever the population looks like*. What it **does not say**: anything whatsoever about the population, about a data set, or about one transit. The population keeps the shape it always had. The theorem describes one object and leaves everything else exactly as it found it.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\mu_{\bar x} = \mu, \qquad \sigma_{\bar x} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}" terms={{ '\\mu_{\\bar x}': 'centre of the sampling distribution of the sample mean', '\\sigma_{\\bar x}': 'its standard deviation — the standard error of the mean', '\\sigma': 'standard deviation of ONE draw from the population', 'n': 'the sample size' }} label="Centre and spread of x̄ — true for every population, at every n" />

<Formula tex="\operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}) = \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\right) = \frac{1}{n^{2}} \cdot n\sigma^{2} = \frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}" terms={{ '\\frac{1}{n^{2}}': 'the averaging constant, squared, because variance scales with the square', 'n\\sigma^{2}': 'variances of n independent draws, added' }} label="Where the square root comes from" />

<Formula tex="z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \qquad\text{(shape licensed by: normal parent at any } n\text{, or } n \ge 30\text{)}" terms={{ '\\bar{x}': 'the observed sample mean', '\\mu': 'the centre the null model puts it at', '\\sigma/\\sqrt{n}': 'the spread of a MEAN of n — never σ, never σ/n' }} label="Standardizing a sample mean" />
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
A case with no stakes in it, so the arithmetic can be watched on its own. The Lane Authority's 2181 scheduling audit timed **{fmtInt(AUDIT_N)} Mercantile transits** to Mark 9 against the nominal plot. Their mean delay came out **{fmt(AUDIT_MEAN, 2)} days**. Is that a slow month, or is it nothing?

The population is the Ledger's honest transits: $\mu = {fmt(LANE_DELAY_MEAN, 3)}$ d, $\sigma = {fmt(LANE_DELAY_SD, 3)}$ d. Both licences for the shape are open here — the parent is near-normal *and* {fmtInt(AUDIT_N)} is at least 30 — so name one and move on.

$$\mu_{\bar x} = \mu = {fmt(LANE_DELAY_MEAN, 3)} \text{ d}, \qquad \sigma_{\bar x} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{{fmt(LANE_DELAY_SD, 3)}}{\sqrt{ {fmtInt(AUDIT_N)} }} = {fmt(AUDIT_SE, 4)} \text{ d}$$

$$z = \frac{ {fmt(AUDIT_MEAN, 3)} - {fmt(LANE_DELAY_MEAN, 3)} }{ {fmt(AUDIT_SE, 4)} } = {fmt(AUDIT_Z, 3)} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P(\bar{x} \ge {fmt(AUDIT_MEAN, 2)}) = {fmtP(AUDIT_P)}$$

So: **{fmtPct(AUDIT_P, 1)}** of audits of {fmtInt(AUDIT_N)} honest transits come out at least that slow. Unremarkable. A scheduling clerk with a grievance would be told to wait for more data, and would be told right.

Now hold that against the individual-level question, which is the one act-5-02 was about. A *single* honest transit {fmt(AUDIT_MEAN, 2)} days behind the plot is not even slightly unusual — {fmtPct(AUDIT_ONE, 0)} of them manage it, two in five. The same lateness is a shrug for one hull and a {fmtPct(AUDIT_P, 1)} event for a group of {fmtInt(AUDIT_N)}, because the spread the *mean* has to travel is {fmt(LANE_DELAY_SD / AUDIT_SE, 1)} times smaller. That factor is $\sqrt{ {fmtInt(AUDIT_N)} }$, and it is the entire engine of this Act.

And the blunder, priced. Divide by $n$ instead of $\sqrt{n}$ and the spread reads {fmt(AUDIT_WRONG_SD, 4)} d instead of {fmt(AUDIT_SE, 4)} d — six times too tight. The same audit then standardizes to $z = {fmt(AUDIT_WRONG_Z, 2)}$, and a quiet month on the Mars run becomes an event the display cannot print a probability for. Every figure downstream of that error is wrong in the same direction: too extreme, too confident, too easy to demolish.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*The CLT normalises the population.* It does not. It never touches the population. Put a flat parent in the machine, raise $n$ from 2 to 40, and watch the top plot: it does not move by a pixel while the bottom pile narrows and rounds off. Marks stay uniform. Silences stay skewed. Only the pile of averages changes, and it was never made of transits.

*"$n \ge 30$" makes data normal.* It is a statement about $\bar{x}$, not a spell cast on a data set. Collect ten thousand times-to-silence and the histogram is the same long-tailed thing it always was, drawn sharper. The condition licenses a *shape* for the sampling distribution of the mean, and it has a better sibling: a normal parent, which licenses that shape at every $n$ there is.

*$\sigma/n$ is not the standard deviation of $\bar{x}$.* $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}) = \sigma^2/n$, so the standard deviation is the square root of that: $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. Dividing by $n$ shrinks the spread by another whole factor of $\sqrt{n}$, which makes a sample of $n$ look as precise as a sample of $n^2$, and drives every probability afterwards far out into a tail where it does not belong. Root $n$. It is in the log.

*A group's mean can be impossible while every member of it is ordinary.* This is the exact inverse of act-5-02's caution, and the reason both modules exist. There, judging a group by whether any single member is extreme threw the evidence away. Here, the trap runs the other way: having found something impossible about an average, it is a short step to saying it about a hull. It is not true of any hull. $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ is the standard deviation of an average and of nothing else, and a probability computed with it is a statement about averages and about nothing else.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

Ebele's first parent is the one that makes the point hardest to argue with. **Mark at last contact · the Board's null** is flat — a hull is as likely to go quiet at mark 3 as at mark 11, which is the Board's own account of the losses. Nothing about that population is normal; it has no peak at all.

Set the PARENT POPULATION control to it, leave **sample size n** at 2, and press RUN. Then walk n up — 5, 12, 30, 60 — pressing RUN at each stop, and read two pairs of numbers against each other. `σ/√n · the theory` beside `SD of the simulated means · the machine`: the theorem's arithmetic and two thousand actual draws, agreeing to three or four places, with `σ/n · the common error` sitting beside them in alert red so that the size of that mistake is never abstract. Then `parent skewness` beside `skewness of the stack`. Both plots share one x-axis, which is the part to watch: as n rises, the bottom pile contracts and the top plot does not stir.
