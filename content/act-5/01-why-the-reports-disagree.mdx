<Briefing title="A distribution of statistics, one per sample" topics={["5.1", "5.4"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Three objects, and the whole Act depends on keeping them apart.

A **parameter** is a number about a **population**: the loss rate over every transit a corridor's file holds. It is fixed. Nobody has to estimate it if they hold the whole file, and it does not wobble when you look at it again tomorrow.

A **statistic** is the same kind of number computed from a **sample**: the rate observed in the transits somebody happened to watch. It is not fixed. Draw a different four hundred transits and a different number comes back, from the same corridor, with nothing done wrong.

The **sampling distribution** of a statistic is the distribution of that statistic **over all samples of size $n$** from the population. It is not a distribution of hulls. It is a distribution of *estimates*, one per sample, which is why a single dot on a simulated stack has already consumed four hundred transits, and why the stack has a shape at all when one sample of losses is nothing but ones and zeroes.

Every sampling distribution has a **centre** and a **width**, and different things damage each of them.

- **Bias** is the distance from the centre of the sampling distribution to the parameter. It is a property of the *recipe*: which units can be drawn, and what arithmetic is done to them. It is the same offset on every repetition. One sample cannot display it. Stacking the statistic and looking at where the pile sits can.
- **Variability** is the width. It comes from the luck of the draw and from nothing else, and for a sample proportion it shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$. Four times the transits, half the spread.

Two honest patrols of the same corridor produce two draws from the same sampling distribution. They are *expected* to differ, and the sampling distribution says by how much. A reviewer who reads that difference as a disagreement about the facts has mistaken the width of the pile for a fact about the corridor.

A statistic can also be honest in every input and wrong by construction. Take two patrols and report the **lower** of the two rates. Each patrol on its own is an unbiased estimate. The rule that picks between them throws away every draw that landed high, so the reported number sits below the truth far more often than above it, and the pile it builds is centred somewhere the corridor has never been.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\mu_{\hat p} = p, \qquad \sigma_{\hat p} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}" terms={{ '\\hat p': 'the sample proportion: one patrol’s observed loss rate, a statistic', 'p': 'the population proportion: the corridor’s own loss rate, a parameter', 'n': 'transits in the patrol' }} label="Centre and width of the sampling distribution of a sample proportion" />

<Formula tex="\text{bias} = E[\,\text{statistic}\,] - \text{parameter}" terms={{ 'E[\\,\\text{statistic}\\,]': 'the centre of the sampling distribution: where the pile sits after infinitely many samples', '\\text{parameter}': 'the population value the statistic is aimed at' }} label="Bias is a statement about the centre, not about one sample" />

An estimator is **unbiased** when that difference is zero, when the pile sits on the truth. It says nothing about how wide the pile is, and a wide unbiased estimator and a narrow biased one are different failures.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Take a corridor whose file is complete, so that the parameter is available for comparison. The {BOARD_TABLE.marsBelt.name} record holds {fmtInt(BOARD_TABLE.marsBelt.losses)} losses in {fmtInt(BOARD_TABLE.marsBelt.transits)} transits, so its rate of **{fmt(marsRate, 5)}**, or {fmtPct(marsRate, 2)}, is a *parameter*. There is nothing to estimate and nothing to wobble.

Now put a patrol on that corridor for {fmtInt(marsPatrolN)} transits. It expects {fmt(marsExpected, 1)} losses and will not observe {fmt(marsExpected, 1)} of anything, because losses come in whole hulls. The sampling distribution of the observed rate is centred on {fmt(marsRate, 5)} with

<Formula tex={`\\sigma_{\\hat p} = \\sqrt{\\frac{${fmt(marsRate, 5)} \\times ${fmt(1 - marsRate, 5)}}{${marsPatrolN}}} = ${fmt(marsSd, 5)}`} terms={{ '\\sigma_{\\hat p}': 'how far one honest patrol’s rate typically lands from the corridor’s own' }} label="One patrol of 600 on the Mars–Belt corridor" />

That is {fmt(100 * marsSd, 2)} of a percentage point, on a corridor whose whole rate is {fmt(100 * marsRate, 2)} of one. So {fmtPct(marsLow, 1)} of honest patrols come home with three losses or fewer and report a corridor running at half its true rate, and {fmtPct(marsHigh, 1)} come home with eleven or more and report it running at nearly double. Both patrols counted correctly. Neither of them observed the corridor.

**Now the rule that picks between two patrols.** Run two of them and report the lower of the two rates. Each patrol is unbiased. Summing over every way the smaller of two counts can fall,

<Formula tex={`E[\\,\\text{lower of two}\\,] = ${fmt(marsMinOfTwo, 5)}, \\qquad \\text{bias} = ${fmt(marsMinOfTwo, 5)} - ${fmt(marsRate, 5)} = ${fmt(marsMinBias, 5)}`} terms={{ '\\text{bias}': 'the centre of the rule’s sampling distribution, minus the corridor’s own rate' }} label="The lower of two honest patrols, on a corridor whose rate is known" />

The rule reports {fmtPct(marsMinOfTwo, 2)} for a corridor running at {fmtPct(marsRate, 2)}: low by {fmt(100 * Math.abs(marsMinBias), 2)} of a percentage point, which is {fmtPct(Math.abs(marsMinBias) / marsRate, 0)} of the thing being measured. It is low on average, low in the same direction every time, and low before anybody has counted a single transit, because the offset lives in the sentence *take the lower of the two* and not in the data.

The offset does not answer to repetitions. Stack a hundred of these and the centre is roughly where it will end up; stack ten thousand and the centre is pinned to five decimals, in the same place. Repetitions buy confidence about where the pile sits. Quadruple the patrol instead, to {fmtInt(4 * marsPatrolN)} transits, and the *honest* estimator's spread halves from {fmt(marsSd, 5)} to {fmt(marsSdBig, 5)} while its centre stays exactly on {fmt(marsRate, 5)}, where it has been the whole time.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*The sampling distribution called the distribution of the sample.* The error that matters most in this topic. A **sample** is made of transits: four hundred hulls, each lost or arrived. A **sampling distribution** is made of statistics: one number per sample, each of which has already consumed all four hundred. They have different units, different shapes and different widths, and only one of them says how far an estimate can land from the truth.

*"Bias" used for random error.* Two honest patrols that differ are not evidence that either is biased. Bias is an offset in the **centre** of the sampling distribution, which no single sample displays and no pair of samples can show. A difference of about one standard deviation between two independent estimates is the machine running normally.

*Larger $n$ as a cure for bias.* Sample size is the answer to variability and to nothing else. A method that drops the wrong units, or a rule that reports the smaller of two honest numbers, is aimed off-target; running it on more transits gives the same wrong aim with a tighter group, and tightness reads as authority. If you cannot say which direction a rule pushes, you cannot correct for it. You can only refuse to use it.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

<SamplingBuilder />

Work it in three passes, in this order, and write down what moves each time.

**One.** Leave the statistic on *loss rate of one patrol*, leave the patrol at four hundred transits, and run it. Both cutters are marked on the axis. Read *draws at or below Asgard* and *draws at or below Tindr* and find where each of them sits in the pile: not whether they are in it, but how far in.

**Two.** Switch the statistic to *lower of two patrols' loss rates* and run it again. Watch the *bias · mean − truth* readout. It goes alert-toned only when the offset is more than three standard errors of the stack's own mean from zero, so the word *biased* is computed for you rather than eyeballed. Keep running. The centre does not drift back.

**Three.** Push the *transits per patrol* slider up and run each setting. The *SD of the stack* falls away as $1/\sqrt{n}$. The Board's statistic is still sitting on the wrong side of the Lane's truth at every setting the slider offers, and no number of repetitions moves it.
