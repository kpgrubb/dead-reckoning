<Briefing title="The model, both directions" topics={["5.2"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A normal model is a **density**. Its height is not a probability; the **area** it encloses over an interval is. Every question in this module asks about that area in one of two directions.

**Forwards: value in, area out.** You hold a cutoff and you want the share of the population beyond it. Standardise, then read the tail: $P(X \ge x) = P\!\left(Z \ge \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)$. The answer is a **long-run proportion** of a population, not a forecast about one unit whose value you already know. A known quantity has no probability left in it.

**Backwards: area in, value out.** You hold a share and you want the cutoff that produces it. This is the **inverse normal**, and it is how a threshold gets set rather than guessed: find the $z$ with the required area below it, then undo the standardising, $x = \mu + z\sigma$. A **percentile** is the same request in different words. The 99th percentile is the value with 99 percent of the model below it.

Fix the direction and the tail before touching a number. *"Only one in a hundred above the line"* means one percent **above**, which is ninety-nine percent **below**, which is the 99th percentile. The commonest wreck in this topic is running the inverse on the wrong tail and reporting a cutoff from the bottom of the distribution with perfect arithmetic.

**The model has to be earned.** Before quoting any of these areas, look at the data. A **dotplot** or **histogram** shows symmetry and strandings. A **normal probability plot**, each sorted value against the $z$ a normal model would put it at, shows a straight line when the model fits and a bend at the ends when it does not. Read it like a residual plot: straight is good, and the middle of any plot looks straight, so look at the ends.

**A cutoff is not a list of suspects.** A line drawn at the 99th percentile is crossed by one percent of *honest* units. That is the definition of the line. In any group of a couple of dozen honest units you should expect the occasional one above it, and finding one tells you nothing you did not know before you drew the line.

**The variable this module runs on.** Mark-9 delay is actual minus scheduled time to a fixed waypoint, measured against one nominal acceleration plot that every hull on the corridor is scheduled against. A hull that makes the nominal plot exactly reads zero; a hull that arrives two days behind the reference reads two. The spread in such a variable is mostly a *persistent per-unit offset* rather than run-to-run noise, because each hull files its own acceleration inside the corridor's permitted band and then flies that filing for its whole life. Offsets of that kind are near-symmetric about the reference, which is what makes a normal model plausible here and worth checking rather than assuming.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="P(X \ge x) = 1 - F(x) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)" terms={{ 'F(x)': 'the cumulative area up to x: the running total of the density', '\\Phi': 'the standard normal cumulative area, the proportion below a z', '\\mu, \\sigma': 'the centre and spread of the model, in the units of the variable' }} label="Forwards: a value becomes a proportion" />

<Formula tex="x_q = F^{-1}(q) = \mu + z_q\,\sigma, \qquad z_q = \Phi^{-1}(q)" terms={{ 'q': 'the area required BELOW the cutoff', 'x_q': 'the q-th percentile: the value with q of the model below it', 'z_q': 'the standard-normal value with area q below it' }} label="Backwards: a proportion becomes a value" />

For a continuous model a single point has no area, so $P(X \ge x)$ and $P(X > x)$ are the same number, and "at least that late" and "later than that" are the same request.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Act I's drive signatures, run in both directions. The honest model built from the fleet's own sixty contacts, the flagged one set aside, is centred on {fmt(fleetModel.mean, 1)} percent of class-table expectation with a standard deviation of {fmt(fleetModel.sd, 2)}.

**Forwards.** What share of honest hulls run at or above 102 percent of expectation?

<Formula tex={`z = \\frac{102 - ${fmt(fleetModel.mean, 1)}}{${fmt(fleetModel.sd, 2)}} = ${fmt(fleetZ102, 3)}, \\qquad P(X \\ge 102) = ${fmt(fleetTail, 4)}`} terms={{ 'z': 'how many spreads above the fleet’s centre 102 percent sits' }} label="Value in, area out" />

Read it in words before it goes anywhere: about {fmtInt(Math.round(100 * fleetTail))} out of every 100 honest hulls burn at least that hot. That is a statement about the fleet, and it is the reason nobody opened a file on a hull at 102.

**Backwards.** Now a flag line is wanted: the ratio only 5 percent of honest hulls exceed. Five percent above means 95 percent below, so the area that goes into the inverse is 0.95, not 0.05:

<Formula tex={`z^\\star = \\Phi^{-1}(0.95) = ${fmt(fleetZ5, 4)}, \\qquad x = ${fmt(fleetModel.mean, 1)} + ${fmt(fleetZ5, 4)} \\times ${fmt(fleetModel.sd, 2)} = ${fmt(fleetCut5, 3)}`} terms={{ 'z^\\star': 'the standard-normal cutoff with 95 percent of the area below it', 'x': 'the ratio, in percent of expectation, that 5 percent of honest hulls exceed' }} label="Area in, value out" />

So the line goes at **{fmt(fleetCut5, 2)} percent of expectation**, and one honest hull in twenty crosses it by doing nothing but flying the Lane. Ask for the 90th percentile instead and the same machinery returns {fmt(fleetP90, 2)} percent, at a $z$ of {fmt(fleetZ90, 3)}: a smaller area below, a smaller $z$, a lower line. Every one of these is the same integral, evaluated once forwards and once backwards.

**The check nobody skips.** All of that is worth exactly what the model is worth. The sixty contacts were plotted before any area was quoted from them, and they were symmetric about their centre with nothing stranded in either tail. Had they been skewed, the arithmetic would have run just as smoothly and the tail would have been a lie. Normal-model areas fail hardest in precisely the tail anybody wants to quote.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"The Central Limit Theorem makes the population normal."* It does not touch the population at all. It is a statement about the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$, and it leaves the individual units exactly as skewed as it found them. Whether a normal model fits *these values* is a question only a plot of those values can answer, and $n$ does not answer it at any size.

*A normal model on a skewed variable, quoted without saying so.* The arithmetic never complains. A right-skewed variable pushed through a normal model returns a tail area that can be wrong by an order of magnitude in the far tail, which is the only part of the curve anybody ever puts in a brief. Look at the probability plot first, say in the brief what you looked at, and if it bends, say that instead.

*Judging a group by whether its members are extreme.* A percentile line is crossed by that percent of honest units by construction. Finding one or two units above a 1 percent line in a group of twenty is arithmetic about the line rather than evidence about the group. A case about a group has to be made about the group.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

<Calculator.Watch>**`invNorm` takes the area to the LEFT.** Entering 0.05 when you want the top 5 percent returns {fmt(fleetWrongTail, 3)}, a cutoff *below* the centre, with the same number of correct keystrokes. For an upper tail of $p$, enter $1-p$. On OS 5.3 and later the wizard has a third field, `tail`, and it remembers what you set last time, so read it before you press Calculate.</Calculator.Watch>
<Calculator.Watch>**The infinite bound.** A normal curve has no largest value, so a one-tailed area needs `1E99` as the upper bound or `−1E99` as the lower one. Substituting 0, or 200, or the largest value in the data changes the answer quietly and in the direction of looking reasonable. The negative sign is `(−)`, the key beside `ENTER`, not the subtraction key above it.</Calculator.Watch>
<Calculator.Watch>**Neither command reads a list.** `normalcdf` evaluates a *model* you have chosen and knows nothing about whether the data support it. The probability plot is the step that licenses the model, and the calculator will happily return a tail area for a variable that has no business having one.</Calculator.Watch>
</Calculator>
