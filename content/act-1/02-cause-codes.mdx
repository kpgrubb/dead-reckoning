<Briefing title="A chart's denominator is its first claim" topics={["1.4"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A **bar chart** shows one categorical variable: one bar per category, bars separated, order chosen by you. Its bars may be **counts** (how many) or **relative frequencies** (what share). Nothing else about it is free.

Four decisions sit under every bar chart, and each one is a claim:

1. **The denominator.** Which individuals are on the axis at all. A bar that is a sliver of every record in a file may be the tallest bar among the losses; nothing in the world changes between the two pictures.
2. **The baseline.** A bar chart's promise is that length is proportional to quantity. That promise holds only if the value axis starts at zero. Start it near the shortest bar and the *ratios* of the bars become an artefact of where you put the floor; the ordering and the labelled differences survive, the ratios do not.
3. **The scale.** Counts answer "how many"; relative frequencies answer "what share". Two groups of different size can only be compared on the second.
4. **The area.** A bar encodes its value in *one* dimension. Scale its width to the value as well and the ink grows as the square, and the reader's eye reads ink.

A **pie chart** is legitimate when its slices are mutually exclusive categories of one variable that between them account for exactly one whole, and when the question is about shares of that whole. It is never the right display for comparing two groups: two pies are two wholes, and their slices are not on a common axis. To compare two groups, put **relative frequencies side by side**, bar against bar, with both groups' totals written where the reader can see them.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\text{bar height}_k = n_k \quad\text{(counts)} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{n_k}{n} \quad\text{(relative frequency)}" terms={{ 'n_k': 'count in category k', 'n': 'the denominator — the individuals the chart is about' }} label="What a bar is" />

With a value axis truncated at $b$, a bar of value $v$ is drawn at length $v - b$, so two bars are drawn in the ratio

<Formula tex="\frac{v_1 - b}{v_2 - b} \neq \frac{v_1}{v_2} \quad \text{unless } b = 0" terms={{ 'b': 'the baseline of the value axis', 'v_i': 'the value of bar i' }} label="Why the baseline must be zero" />
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The cutter *Asgard*'s 2176 return: {fmtInt(asgard.lost)} losses in {fmtInt(asgard.transits)} transits. Charted against transits, the loss bar stands at {fmtPct(asgard.lost / asgard.transits, 2)}, a hairline. That bar is honest and it answers a real question, *how often does a transit end in silence*. It cannot answer *how are the losses classified*, because for that question the denominator is {fmtInt(asgard.lost)}, and every bar on the chart grows by a factor of {fmt(asgard.transits / asgard.lost, 0)}.

Take the Mars–Belt corridor's file, 2170–76: {marsBeltTotal} losses, {marsBelt.map((r) => `${r.value} ${r.count}`).join(', ')}. As relative frequencies of the {marsBeltTotal}: {marsBelt.map((r) => `${r.value} ${fmt(r.relFreq, 3)}`).join(', ')}, summing to {fmt(marsBelt.reduce((s, r) => s + r.relFreq, 0), 3)}.

Now truncate. *Accident* at {fmt(marsBelt[0].relFreq, 3)} is {fmt(mbTrueRatio, 2)} times *unknown* at {fmt(marsBelt[2].relFreq, 3)}. The honest chart draws one bar twice the other. Start the value axis at {fmtPct(mbBaseline, 0)} instead of zero and the drawn lengths become {fmt(marsBelt[0].relFreq - mbBaseline, 3)} against {fmt(marsBelt[2].relFreq - mbBaseline, 3)}: a height ratio of **{fmt(mbDrawnRatio, 1)}**. Nothing in the file changed and the axis labels are still correct. The drawing now overstates the difference by a factor the reader has no way of recovering from the picture alone.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*A pie for data that are not parts of one whole.* Two offices' *unknown* shares are two numbers from two different denominators; they do not partition anything and they need not sum to one, so they are not slices. Neither are quantities measured on a scale, since hull ages have no slices, and neither is a time series, where a pie throws away the ordering the trend lives in.

*A truncated axis read as a proportional difference.* "Nearly three times the height" is a statement about the drawing, not about the data. Read the axis before the bars, every time, and read it on charts other people send you as well as on your own.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
