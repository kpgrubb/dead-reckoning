<Briefing title="Distance in spreads, and the area past it" topics={["1.10"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A raw value answers nothing on its own. A plume running a few percent above expectation means one thing in a drive family whose hulls scatter by half a percent and something else entirely in a family that scatters by five. **Standardising** removes the units and the scale: a **z-score** says how many standard deviations a value sits from the centre, and its sign says which side.

$z = 1$ is one spread above the mean. $z = -2$ is two below. Because the numerator and denominator carry the same units, $z$ has none. That is what lets you compare a plume ratio with a sink temperature with a navigational fix error.

**A linear change of units changes the numbers and not the picture.** Re-express every value as $y = aX + b$ (percent to tonnes, kelvin to celsius, kilometres to megametres) and every measure of *centre or position* (mean, median, quartiles, a specific value) is transformed the same way, while every measure of *spread* (SD, IQR, range) is scaled by $|a|$ and never shifted by $b$: adding a constant to everything moves the whole distribution without stretching it. The **shape** does not change at all. And $z$ is unchanged, because $a$ divides out of the top and the bottom.

**From a value to a proportion.** When a variable is modelled by a normal curve, the proportion of the model between two cutoffs is the **area** under the curve between them. The **empirical rule** is three of those areas, rounded: about 68 percent within ±1 SD, 95 percent within ±2, 99.7 percent within ±3. A table or a calculator gives the rest. Run the model backwards, from a proportion to the cutoff that produces it, and you have an **inverse normal**, which is how a threshold gets set instead of guessed.

**A percentile** is the same idea stated as a rank: the percent of the distribution below a value.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula
  tex="z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}"
  terms={{ 'x': 'the observed value', '\\mu': 'the centre of the model', '\\sigma': 'the standard deviation of the model', 'z': 'distance from the centre, in standard deviations: unitless, signed' }}
  label="The z-score"
/>

<Formula
  tex="y = aX + b \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu_y = a\mu_x + b, \quad \sigma_y = |a|\,\sigma_x, \quad z \text{ unchanged}"
  terms={{ 'a': 'the scale factor: stretches centre and spread alike', 'b': 'the shift: moves the centre, leaves every spread alone' }}
  label="Effect of a linear transformation"
/>

<Formula
  tex="P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \Phi\!\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right)"
  terms={{ 'f(x)': 'the density: a height, never a probability', '\\Phi': 'the standard normal cumulative area, the proportion below a z' }}
  label="A proportion is an area"
/>

A density assigns no probability to a single point: an interval of width zero has no area, so $P(X = x) = 0$ and "at least" and "more than" are the same request.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
**A z, and the choice of what to divide by.** The class table rates the Mk 3 family at 100 percent of expectation with a rated spread of {fmt(RATED_SPREAD_PCT, 1)} percent. Standardising a contact of {fmt(harpagia.ratio_pct, 1)} percent against that certificate gives

<Formula tex={oyelaranTex} terms={{ 'z': 'distance above the certificate’s centre, counted in rated spreads: a tolerance a manufacturer promises, not a spread anyone measured' }} label="z against the class table" />

about {fmt(oyelaranZ, 1)} spreads above centre, *if* the manufacturer's tolerance is the right denominator. A rated spread is a specification. A set of measurements has a spread of its own, and it is usually tighter. Two defensible z-scores can be computed for the same observation, and the difference between them is entirely a claim about which model of "ordinary" you are willing to sign. State the model with the z, always.

**The empirical rule, on a normal model.** Whatever a model's centre and spread happen to be, a normal curve puts {fmtPct(bands[0], 1)} of itself within ±1 SD of the centre, {fmtPct(bands[1], 1)} within ±2 SD and {fmtPct(bands[2], 1)} within ±3 SD. So the whole region beyond three spreads, both tails together, holds {fmtPct(1 - bands[2], 2)} of the model, and one tail holds half of that. Past three spreads the rule can only gesture; for a figure you need the area itself.

**The same model in tonnes.** Take the fleet's centre of {fmt(fleetModel.mean, 1)} percent of expectation and its spread of {fmt(fleetModel.sd, 2)}, and re-express them as tonnes above a declaration of {fmtInt(HARPAGIA_CERTIFICATION.declared_mass_t)} t. That is a linear change of units with a scale factor of {fmt(tonnesA, 0)} tonnes per percent and a shift of minus the declaration:

<Formula tex={tonnesTex} terms={{ '\\mu_y': 'the centre in tonnes above the declaration: scaled and shifted', '\\sigma_y': 'the spread in tonnes: scaled only, and the shift never touches it' }} label="The fleet model, re-expressed in tonnes" />

The centre lands essentially on the declaration itself, and the spread becomes a few hundred tonnes. The shape is the shape it was, and every z on that axis is the number it was before: both the distance and the spread were multiplied by {fmt(tonnesA, 0)}, and the factor divides out.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"z only works for normal data."* It does not. A z-score is arithmetic, a distance divided by a spread, and it is defined for any distribution with a mean and an SD. What needs the normal model is the step *after* it: turning that z into a proportion. Standardise anything; take areas only when a normal model has been argued for.

*The empirical rule applied to every distribution.* 68–95–99.7 is a reading of the normal curve and of nothing else. On a strongly skewed variable (time to silence, say) "within one SD of the mean" can hold far more or far less than 68 percent, and a mean plus two SDs can sit beyond the largest value in the data.

*Reading the height of a density as a probability.* The curve's height is probability per unit of $x$; on a narrow enough axis it can exceed 1. Only an area is a probability. And because a point has no width, no single value has a probability at all. Under a continuous model "at least {fmt(harpagia.ratio_pct, 1)}" and "more than {fmt(harpagia.ratio_pct, 1)}" are one question. Asked of a list of {fmtInt(contacts.length)} recorded contacts, they are two, and the answers differ by one contact.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

<Calculator.Watch>**The infinite bound.** A normal curve has no smallest or largest value, so a one-tailed area needs `−1E99` as the lower bound or `1E99` as the upper one. Substituting 0, or 1000, or the smallest value in your data changes the answer, quietly and in the direction of looking reasonable. `(−)` for the negative sign is the key beside `ENTER`, not the subtraction key above it.</Calculator.Watch>
<Calculator.Watch>**`invNorm` takes the area to the LEFT.** Feeding it 0.01 when you want the top one percent returns a cutoff *below* the centre. For an upper tail of $p$, enter $1-p$, or on OS 5.3 and later set the third field of the wizard to `tail: RIGHT` and read it before you press Calculate, because it remembers what you did last time.</Calculator.Watch>
<Calculator.Watch>**`normalcdf` wants σ, not σ².** Entering the variance here returns {fmt(ratedVarianceTrap, 4)} where the answer is {fmt(ratedTail, 4)}: a number about {fmtInt(Math.round(ratedVarianceTrap / ratedTail))} times too large, still between 0 and 1, with nothing on the screen to say anything is wrong. The fourth argument is a standard deviation and carries the units of the data. Check it every time.</Calculator.Watch>
<Calculator.Watch>Neither command reads a list. `normalcdf` evaluates a **model** you have chosen; it knows nothing about whether the data support it. Standardising is arithmetic and always allowed; taking an area commits you to the normal curve.</Calculator.Watch>
</Calculator>
