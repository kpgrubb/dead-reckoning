<Briefing title="Five numbers, two fences, and what is not in them" topics={["1.8"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
The **five-number summary** is the minimum, $Q_1$, the median, $Q_3$ and the maximum. Five positions in the sorted list, and between them they cut the batch into four quarters, each holding about a quarter of the observations.

Quartiles in this course follow the **AP / TI-84 convention**, stated once and used everywhere: sort the values, split the list at the median, and take $Q_1$ as the median of the lower half and $Q_3$ as the median of the upper half. The overall median is excluded from both halves when $n$ is odd. Other software uses other conventions and gets slightly different quartiles from the same data; say which you used.

A **boxplot** draws those five numbers: a box from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ with the median marked inside it, and a whisker out each side. A **modified boxplot**, the one this course draws, cuts the whiskers back at the **fences** and plots anything beyond them as a point of its own.

The **1.5 × IQR rule** is a convention, not a law of nature. It flags values far enough out that somebody should look again. A flagged value may be a transcription error, an instrument fault, a genuinely unusual individual, or the most important thing in the file. The fence cannot tell you which, and it never claims to. **A fence flags; it does not convict.**

Now the cost. A boxplot is *only* those five numbers plus the flags. It is very good at spread (the box and the whiskers), at skew (compare the two halves of the box, and the two whiskers), and at pointing at extremes. It is blind to everything between the five:

- **modes:** one pile or three, the box is the same;
- **gaps:** an empty middle is invisible, because the box is drawn *across* it;
- **sample size:** nothing in a boxplot's geometry tells you whether it came from eleven observations or eleven thousand, which is why a comparison of two groups must print both $n$'s.

Use a boxplot to compare several groups at once, or to flag extremes. Use a dotplot, a stemplot or a histogram when the question is about shape. When it matters, draw the box and put the dots back on top of it.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\text{five-number summary} = \left(\, x_{\min},\; Q_1,\; M,\; Q_3,\; x_{\max} \,\right), \qquad \text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1" terms={{ 'Q_1': 'median of the lower half of the sorted values', 'Q_3': 'median of the upper half', 'M': 'the middle of the sorted list' }} label="The five numbers" />

<Formula tex="\text{lower fence} = Q_1 - 1.5 \times \text{IQR}, \qquad \text{upper fence} = Q_3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR}" terms={{ '1.5': 'a convention: one and a half box-widths out from each end of the box' }} label="The 1.5 × IQR rule" />

An observation outside either fence is flagged as an **outlier** and drawn as its own point; the whiskers then run only as far as the most extreme observation that is *not* flagged.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Oyelaran's **first {fmtInt(ratioFirstEleven.length)} contacts**, plume ratio in percent of the class-table expectation, sorted:

{elevenSorted.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}

With {fmtInt(elevenSorted.length)} values the median is the {fmtInt((elevenSorted.length + 1) / 2)}th of them, {fmt(elevenFive.median, 1)}. Strike it out of both halves. The lower half is {elevenLower.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}, whose median is Q1 = {fmt(elevenFive.q1, 1)}; the upper half is {elevenUpper.map((v) => fmt(v, 1)).join(', ')}, whose median is Q3 = {fmt(elevenFive.q3, 1)}.

Five-number summary: {fmt(elevenFive.min, 1)}, {fmt(elevenFive.q1, 1)}, {fmt(elevenFive.median, 1)}, {fmt(elevenFive.q3, 1)}, {fmt(elevenFive.max, 1)} percent of expectation. IQR = {fmt(elevenFive.q3, 1)} − {fmt(elevenFive.q1, 1)} = **{fmt(elevenBox, 1)}**.

Fences: {fmt(elevenFive.q1, 1)} − 1.5 × {fmt(elevenBox, 1)} = **{fmt(elevenFences.lowFence, 2)}** and {fmt(elevenFive.q3, 1)} + 1.5 × {fmt(elevenBox, 1)} = **{fmt(elevenFences.highFence, 2)}**.

And now the thing worth noticing. The largest of those eleven is {fmt(elevenFive.max, 1)} percent, the hull that has been sitting out on the right of every ratio display since MET 13, and it is **not flagged**, because {fmt(elevenFive.max, 1)} is inside a fence at {fmt(elevenFences.highFence, 2)}. The rule found {fmtInt(elevenFences.values.length)} outliers among these eleven.

Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic. The fence is built from the batch's own IQR. Eleven contacts, one of them the hot one, make a wide middle half, and a wide middle half pushes the fence far out. Add more honest contacts and the middle half tightens, the fence comes in, and the same value can cross it without changing at all. **A fence is a statement about the batch, not about the observation.** Which is exactly why the same hull deserves a second look now that the log holds {fmtInt(REGISTER_META.contacts)} contacts instead of {fmtInt(ratioFirstEleven.length)}.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"The box holds 50 percent of the range."* It holds 50 percent of the **observations**. Those are different claims and they are usually very different pictures: a tight batch with one far value can have a box covering a tenth of the axis and still hold half the individuals. Every quarter of a boxplot carries about a quarter of the data; a wide quarter means those observations are spread thin, which is what a tail is.

*Box width read as sample size.* Nothing in a boxplot encodes $n$. Two boxes drawn the same width may come from eleven observations and eleven thousand, and a comparison that does not print both counts is not checkable.

*A clean boxplot read as a well-behaved variable.* No flagged points means no value crossed a fence. It does not mean one mound, it does not mean no gap, and it does not mean the median describes anything. If the question is about shape, the box is the wrong display and reading it more carefully will not help.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
