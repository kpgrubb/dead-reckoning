export const mk3Plume = contacts.filter((c) => c.drive_family === 'Mk 3').map((c) => c.plume_TW)
export const tesseraPlume = contacts.filter((c) => c.drive_family === 'Tessera-C').map((c) => c.plume_TW)
export const mk2Plume = contacts.filter((c) => c.drive_family === 'Mk 2').map((c) => c.plume_TW)

export const coarseBins = bins(plumeTW, { method: 'width', width: 0.1, start: 0.1 })
export const fineBins = bins(plumeTW, { method: 'width', width: 0.025, start: 0.125 })
export const valleyBin = fineBins.bins
  .filter((b) => b.lo >= median(tesseraPlume) && b.hi <= median(mk3Plume))
  .reduce((a, b) => (b.count < a.count ? b : a))

export const nominalMk3 = expectedPlumeTW('Mk 3', 20000, 3)
export const hotExcess = harpagia.ratio_pct - 100

Twenty-five thousandths of an astronomical unit astern of Callisto, and twelve seconds of light between this ship and anyone who could answer her. Seventeen kilometres a second and building. The sensor bay is the coldest compartment aboard because Sandoval keeps it that way: the Eyes are four eight-hundred-millimetre cooled telescopes at the mast head, and a warm optic sees nothing but itself.

Oyelaran has been looking at it for eleven seconds before she says anything, and when she does she is quieter than she was.

<Briefing title="Three ways to draw one column, and four things to say about it" topics={["1.5", "1.6"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
One quantitative variable, three standard displays, and they differ in what they keep.

A **dotplot** puts one dot over every value on a number line. Nothing is lost and nothing is smoothed; it works while the batch is small enough to count.

A **stemplot** splits each value into a **stem** (the leading digits) and a **leaf** (the next digit), and states the **leaf unit** so the plot can be read back into numbers. Every value survives, sorted, which makes medians and quartiles readable straight off the page.

A **histogram** divides the axis into bins of equal **width**, counts how many individuals fall in each, and draws a bar of that height. Bars touch, because the axis is continuous and there is nothing between two bins. The individual values inside a bar are gone — that is the trade, and what you buy with it is a shape you can see at sixty values, or at six thousand.

**Bin width is a decision, not a property of the data.** One batch of numbers makes a different picture at every width. Too wide and two piles merge into one; too narrow and the display becomes a comb of ones and twos, and you are reading sampling noise as structure. Report the width, and look at the shape at more than one of them before you describe it.

Then describe it, and describe it in this order — **shape, centre, spread, unusual features (SOCS)**, every one of them with the units of the variable and the name of the individuals:

1. **Shape.** Symmetric, or skewed, or bimodal. Skew is named for the direction of the **long tail**, not for where the peak sits: a batch piled at the low end with a tail running to the right is *skewed right*.
2. **Centre.** A median or a mean, with its value.
3. **Spread.** An IQR, a standard deviation or a range, with its value.
4. **Unusual features.** Outliers, gaps, clusters — named, with their values.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\text{bar height}_j = \#\{\, x_i : e_j \le x_i \lt e_{j+1} \,\}, \qquad e_{j+1} - e_j = w" terms={{ 'w': 'the bin width — the analyst’s decision, not the data’s', 'e_j': 'the jth bin edge, so the jth bar covers the values from e_j up to e_{j+1}' }} label="What one histogram bar counts" />

A relative-frequency histogram divides each bar by $n$; the shape is identical and only the vertical axis changes. Neither version is readable without its bin width stated.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
Take the sixty contacts' **measured plume power**, in terawatts, and bin it two ways.

At a bin width of 0.1 TW there are {fmtInt(coarseBins.bins.length)} bars, and their counts are {coarseBins.bins.map((b) => fmtInt(b.count)).join(' · ')}. That is as flat as a deck plate. On that display the Lane is one undifferentiated batch of freighters.

At a bin width of 0.025 TW there are {fmtInt(fineBins.bins.length)} bars, and the same sixty numbers make two piles with a trough between them: the bar at {fmt(valleyBin.lo, 3)} to {fmt(valleyBin.hi, 3)} TW holds {fmtInt(valleyBin.count)}, while the piles on either side of it run to {fmtInt(maxOf(fineBins.bins.map((b) => b.count)))}. Nothing about the data changed. The first display could not resolve two drive families and the second can.

So: **shape** — bimodal, two piles with a gap; **centre** — the median plume power is {fmt(median(plumeTW), 2)} TW and the mean {fmt(mean(plumeTW), 2)} TW, and here is the trouble, because {fmt(mean(plumeTW), 2)} TW falls in the trough, and no hull on the Lane burns it; **spread** — the sixty run from {fmt(minOf(plumeTW), 2)} to {fmt(maxOf(plumeTW), 2)} TW, an IQR of {fmt(iqr(plumeTW), 2)}; **unusual features** — none beyond the two piles themselves, which are the {fmtInt(mk3Plume.length)} Mk 3 hulls at a mean of {fmt(mean(mk3Plume), 2)} TW and the {fmtInt(tesseraPlume.length)} Tessera-C at {fmt(mean(tesseraPlume), 2)}.

Say the shape first and the centre becomes a sentence you would never have written otherwise.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Naming the skew from the peak.* A distribution piled on the left with a long tail to the right is **skewed right**. Learners — and staff officers — read the peak, see it sitting low on the axis, and write "skewed left". The name belongs to the tail. When you are unsure, compare the mean with the median: the mean is dragged toward the tail.

*Treating a histogram as a bar chart.* Its bars touch because the axis is continuous, so they may never be reordered by height, the gaps between them are values and not categories, and a display without its bin width in the caption cannot be checked by anyone. A bar chart's categories have no order and no width; a histogram's bins have both.

*Describing a display instead of a distribution.* "There is a tall bar at the left" is about the ink. "The measured plume powers of the sixty contacts are bimodal, with a pile of Tessera-C hulls near {fmt(mean(tesseraPlume), 2)} TW and a pile of Mk 3 hulls near {fmt(mean(mk3Plume), 2)} TW" is about the Lane.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
