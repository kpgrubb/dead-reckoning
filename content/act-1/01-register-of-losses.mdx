export const lossClasses = countBy(losses.map((l) => l.classification), ['unknown', 'accident', 'piracy'])
export const uruk = losses.filter((l) => l.office === 'Uruk')
export const ceres = losses.filter((l) => l.office === 'Ceres')

export const ceresUnknown = ceres.filter((l) => l.classification === 'unknown').length / ceres.length
export const ceresPiracy = ceres.filter((l) => l.classification === 'piracy').length

export const marsBeltCodes = [
  ...Array.from({ length: 22 }, () => 'accident'),
  ...Array.from({ length: 13 }, () => 'piracy'),
  ...Array.from({ length: 11 }, () => 'unknown'),
]
export const marsBelt = countBy(marsBeltCodes, ['accident', 'piracy', 'unknown'])
export const marsBeltTotal = marsBeltCodes.length

<Briefing title="Individuals, variables, and a table that stands for a process" topics={["1.1", "1.2", "1.3"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Before any display: an **individual** is the thing one row describes. A **variable** is something recorded about each individual, and it takes one of a few kinds.

A **categorical** variable places the individual in a group — `classification`, `office`, `owner_class`. A **quantitative** variable measures it, and is either **discrete** (a count: dropouts filed in a year) or **continuous** (a measurement on a scale: `mark_last`, `time_to_silence`, `hull_age`). An **identifier** — `record_id`, `hull` — names the individual and is not a variable at all: no summary of it means anything.

The trap is a category written with digits. Office 01 and office 02 are two names. Severity coded 1, 2, 3 is three names. You can compute their mean, and the mean will be a number, and the number will be about nothing.

For a categorical variable a **frequency table** gives the count in each category; dividing each count by the total gives **relative frequencies**, which sum to 1 across the categories of one variable. Which total is the whole question. A share *among all thirty-one losses* and a share *among the losses one office classified* are different numbers answering different questions, and only the second is about that office.

And the last thing, which is not arithmetic: the Register's individuals are *incidents as recorded and classified by two offices*. It is a recorded sample of a reporting process. Every row in it passed through somebody's judgement on the way in, and every row that is not in it was somebody's judgement too.
</Briefing.Intuition>
<Briefing.Definition>
<Formula tex="\text{relative frequency of category } k = \frac{n_k}{n}, \qquad \sum_k \frac{n_k}{n} = 1" terms={{ 'n_k': 'count of individuals in category k', 'n': 'the denominator — every individual the table describes, after every filter' }} label="Relative frequency" />

A **conditional** relative frequency restricts the denominator to one group: the share of category $k$ among the individuals in row $i$ is $n_{ik} / n_{i}$, where $n_i$ is that row's total.
</Briefing.Definition>
<Briefing.Worked>
The Lane Authority's sister file for the Mars–Belt corridor, 2170–76, records {marsBeltTotal} losses with the same three codes: {marsBelt.map((r) => `${r.value} ${r.count}`).join(', ')}.

Relative frequencies, each count over {marsBeltTotal}: {marsBelt.map((r) => `${r.value} ${fmt(r.relFreq, 3)}`).join(', ')}. They sum to {fmt(marsBelt.reduce((s, r) => s + r.relFreq, 0), 3)}, as the relative frequencies of one variable's categories always do — which is the cheapest check there is on a table. In the Mars–Belt file *accident* carries {fmtPct(marsBelt[0].relFreq, 0)} of the losses and *unknown* {fmtPct(marsBelt[2].relFreq, 0)}: a corridor whose offices mostly reached a finding.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
Two errors, and the Service's own reports commit both.

*A coded number treated as a measurement.* The export ships `office` as 01 and 02 and `severity` as 1, 2, 3. A mean of 1.4 is arithmetic performed on names. Ask whether the digits could be swapped without losing anything; if they could, the variable is categorical.

*Relative frequencies that do not sum to one.* When they miss, a row has been dropped or the denominator has changed under you — a filter left on, a category with no records omitted from the table, a "not classified" row quietly deleted. Add them up every time. The sum is a free audit of the denominator.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

Ebele writes the two tables up properly, with denominators in the headings, the way the Service teaches and almost nobody does. The Uruk office classified {uruk.length} of the {losses.length} and coded {uruk.filter((l) => l.classification === 'unknown').length} of its {uruk.length} *unknown* — {fmtPct(urukUnknown, 0)} of everything it touched. 'none of them' : String(ceresPiracy)} *piracy*.

"It could be the hulls," he says, to himself more than to you. "Uruk takes the Compact flags. Sulcus Freight is Compact-flagged. Maybe those are just the ones where nothing gets found."

That is the correct instinct and you tell him so. Two offices with different caseloads will produce different tables for reasons that have nothing to do with anything being wrong: different hulls, different evidence, different cutters, different standards for calling a finding a finding. What you have is two rows and a difference between them. What you do not have is a reason.

Ferrier has the histogram back up — his first one, the flat four-bar picture of all {fmtInt(register.length)} records — and beside it the same axis with only the thirty-one on it. She does not comment on the second one. She is looking at it the way she looks at a fix she does not trust.

"Before you put the classification table in anything with my name under it," she says, "I want the other thing. Thirty-one hulls lost to ordinary accidents over six years on a hundred-day corridor — accidents happen where the ship is, and the ship is everywhere along it. Show me what thirty-one random accidents look like on that axis. Then show me these."

Ebele says he can have it by the afternoon watch. Nobody answers him, because the answer is not a thing you can assert; it is a shape, and the shape is not on any display on this bridge yet. The mark axis sits there with thirty-one marks on it, and nobody on the bridge has yet said out loud what is wrong with where they are.
