<Briefing title="A hypothesis is a claim about a parameter, written before the data" topics={["6.4"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A significance test is an argument with a fixed shape, and every part of it is fixed *before* the statistic is computed. Get the shape right and the arithmetic is bookkeeping. Get it wrong and no amount of arithmetic will repair it.

**The hypotheses are about parameters.** $p$, $p_1 - p_2$, $\mu$ — unknown numbers describing a process or a population. Never $\hat p$, never $\bar x$. The sample statistic is already known exactly; there is nothing left to test about it. When Ebele wrote $H_0: \hat p_1 = \hat p_2$ he wrote a claim that the table in front of him was a different table.

**$H_0$ is the statement of no effect, and it names one value.** $p = p_0$, or $p_1 = p_2$. It has to be a single value — or, for two samples, a single *relation* — because the whole procedure works by building the sampling distribution that $H_0$ implies and seeing where the data land on it. "$p$ is more than $p_0$" implies no particular distribution, so it can never be the null.

Note what a two-sample null does **not** say. It does not name the common rate. It says only that both groups share whatever that rate is, and that single sentence is enough to centre the sampling distribution of $\hat p_1 - \hat p_2$ at zero.

**$H_a$ is read off the question, not off the data.** One-sided when only one direction would answer the question asked, or change the decision that follows; two-sided when a departure of either sign matters. The test to apply: *could I have written this alternative before the audit came back?* If the honest answer is no — if the reason is "the sample came out high" — then the alternative was chosen after the fact, and the procedure is quietly two-sided while the number reported is one-sided. The advertised false-alarm rate is then half the real one.

**Conditions are checked at the null, because the whole calculation is a description of the world $H_0$ claims.**

- **Random.** The data arise from a random sample, a randomized experiment, or a process it is defensible to treat as random. Say which, in context.
- **Independent (10 %).** Sampling without replacement needs a population at least ten times the sample — or, for a process, that the process could produce far more than these observations.
- **Large Counts.** For one sample: $np_0 \ge 10$ **and** $n(1 - p_0) \ge 10$ — computed at $p_0$, not at $\hat p$. For two samples: all four of $n_1\hat p_c$, $n_1(1 - \hat p_c)$, $n_2\hat p_c$, $n_2(1 - \hat p_c)$, computed at the **pooled** $\hat p_c$, because under $H_0$ both groups run at the same rate and every observation is evidence about it.

**The standardized statistic is a distance, not a probability.** It is *(what we saw) minus (what the null said), divided by how much this statistic ordinarily wobbles* — the standard error of the null's own sampling distribution. Its units are standard errors, so a gap of two tenths of a percentage point can be enormous on five thousand transits and invisible on fifty. The statistic is what lets you say which.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Formula tex="H_0: p_1 = p_2 \qquad H_a: p_1 > p_2 \ \text{ or } \ p_1 < p_2 \ \text{ or } \ p_1 \ne p_2" terms={{ 'p_1': 'the true proportion in the first group', 'p_2': 'the true proportion in the second group' }} label="Two proportions — the null names no number at all" />

<Briefing.Worked>
The Themis Reach, 2181, and a review nobody has ever objected to.

The Reach survey office had been told for two seasons that {themis.group1} were being held at the mark more often than everyone else. Before it pulled a single row, the office wrote the question down: *are charter hulls held at a higher rate?* A lower rate would have been an interesting curiosity and would have changed nothing the office does, so the alternative went down one-sided, and it went down in the minute book with a date on it.

**Hypotheses.** $H_0: p_1 = p_2$ — charter hulls and everyone else are held at the same rate. $H_a: p_1 > p_2$ — charter hulls are held at a higher rate. $p_1$ and $p_2$ are the true proportions of transits held at the mark among {themis.group1} and {themis.group2}, over {themis.population}.

**The counts.** {fmtInt(themis.x1)} of {fmtInt(themis.n1)} charter transits held, against {fmtInt(themis.x2)} of {fmtInt(themis.n2)} for everyone else — {fmtPct(themisRate1, 2)} against {fmtPct(themisRate2, 2)}.

**Pooling.** Under $H_0$ there is only one rate, so every transit in the file is evidence about it:

<Formula tex={themisPooledTex} terms={{ 'x_1': 'transits held among the charter hulls', 'x_2': 'transits held among everyone else', 'n_1': 'charter transits in the file', 'n_2': 'other transits in the file' }} label="The pooled rate under the Themis null" />

Note what it is not: the average of {fmt(themisRate1, 4)} and {fmt(themisRate2, 4)}, which would be {fmt((themisRate1 + themisRate2) / 2, 4)}. The samples are different sizes, and pooling the raw counts weights them for you.

**Large Counts, all four, at the pooled rate.**

- n₁ · p̂c = {fmt(themisCounts[0].value, 1)}
- n₁ · (1 − p̂c) = {fmt(themisCounts[1].value, 1)}
- n₂ · p̂c = {fmt(themisCounts[2].value, 1)}
- n₂ · (1 − p̂c) = {fmt(themisCounts[3].value, 1)}

The smallest is **{fmt(themisSmallest, 1)}**, and it clears 10, so the normal model for the sampling distribution of $\hat p_1 - \hat p_2$ is defensible. The office recorded that number in the review, and it recorded it *first*, before the comparison — which is the only reason the review has never been argued with.

Stop there. Hypotheses, pooled rate, conditions with the number attached. That is the whole of a test plan, and it is written before the statistic exists.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*Hypotheses about $\hat p$.* The commonest error in the whole unit, and the one Ebele made out loud. The two sample rates — {fmt(PERRINE_RATE, 4)} and {fmt(OTHER_RATE, 4)} — are facts already in the file; they are unequal, and no test is required to establish it. The test is about $p_1$ and $p_2$, which nobody has seen and nobody will.

*Choosing the alternative after the data.* The seductive version does not feel like cheating: you look at the table, see which way the difference runs, and write the alternative to match. But a procedure that would have tested the other tail had the sample fallen the other way is a two-sided procedure. Reported as one-sided, it doubles its own false-alarm rate in silence. Write the direction first, with a reason and a date, and the objection cannot be made.

*Conditions with $\hat p$ instead of $p_0$ or $\hat p_c$.* For a **test**, every quantity is computed under $H_0$ — the standard error and the Large Counts products alike — because the question the test asks is *what would the world look like if the null were true.* $\hat p$ belongs to the confidence interval, which asks a different question. The two procedures can disagree about whether a condition is met, and when they do, each is right about its own question.

*The condition that passes narrowly is still a passed condition — and it is still the first thing a hostile reader will find.* {fmtInt(cutterLosses)} losses in {fmtInt(cutterTransits)} transits across {CUTTERS.asgard.name} and {CUTTERS.tindr.name} pools to {fmt(cutterPooled, 4)}, which puts the smallest expected count at {fmt(cutterSmallest, 1)}. That is not a narrow pass; that is a failure, and no z-procedure may be run on those two patrol records at all. Between {fmt(cutterSmallest, 1)} and {fmt(themisSmallest, 1)} lies every degree of comfort there is, and you are obliged to say which one you are standing on.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
