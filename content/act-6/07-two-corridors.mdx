<Briefing title="How big is the gap, and which way does it point" topics={["6.8", "6.9"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A significance test answers one question: are these two rates far enough apart that chance alone is a poor account of it? It never answers the other one. **How far apart?** For that you need an interval on the difference itself.

The estimate is the difference of the two sample proportions, $\hat p_1 - \hat p_2$. Its sampling distribution is centred at the true difference $p_1 - p_2$, and its spread comes from the fact that **variances add for independent quantities even when the quantities are subtracted**. Each sample contributes its own uncertainty and neither cancels the other, so the standard error is the square root of a sum.

Each sample keeps its own $\hat p$ in that sum. This is the **unpooled** standard error, and it is unpooled for a reason an exam will ask about: an interval is not assuming the two rates are equal. It is estimating how different they are. There is nothing here to pool *under*.

**The order of subtraction is a decision, and it is yours.** Group 1 minus group 2 and group 2 minus group 1 are the same arithmetic reflected through zero, and they carry opposite meanings. The interval does not record which you chose. The sentence has to.

Then the reading, which is where the marks are.

- **The whole interval above 0.** Every value the data finds plausible for $p_1 - p_2$ is positive, so the first rate is higher, and the interval says by how much: from its lower endpoint to its upper one.
- **The whole interval below 0.** The same statement with the groups the other way round.
- **The interval contains 0.** The data cannot rule out a difference of zero. It also cannot rule out any other value inside the interval, and some of those may be large enough to matter a great deal. "Consistent with no difference" is what you have. "The rates are equal" is a different claim, stronger than the evidence, and the interval is in fact the proof that it cannot be made: it lists dozens of other values that are equally consistent with the data.

Width is driven by the same three things as any interval. A higher confidence level widens it. Larger samples narrow it, both of them, and the smaller sample does most of the damage. A rate near zero or one contributes less variance than a rate near a half, which is why very rare events can be estimated surprisingly tightly in absolute terms and still hopelessly loosely in relative ones.

Last, the unit. A difference of proportions is in **percentage points**, not per cent. A rate that moves from one in two hundred to one in a hundred has risen by half a percentage point, and it has also doubled. Both are true, they sound nothing alike, and a report that reaches for whichever is more dramatic has made an argument rather than a measurement. Give the points, give the base, and let the reader do the division.
</Briefing.Intuition>

**Conditions**, all three stated with their numbers:

1. **Random.** Two independent random samples, or two groups formed by a randomized experiment. Two groups split out of one sample is a different design and a different procedure.
2. **10%.** Each sample is no more than a tenth of its own population, checked separately: $n_1 \le 0.1 N_1$ and $n_2 \le 0.1 N_2$.
3. **Large Counts.** All four **observed** counts at least 10: $x_1$, $n_1 - x_1$, $x_2$, $n_2 - x_2$. The interval checks the counts the samples actually produced. A test checks four different products, computed under its own null hypothesis.
</Briefing.Definition>

<Briefing.Caution>
*The sign.* The commonest lost mark on this topic, and it costs the whole interpretation rather than a point. Write the order into the sentence with both group names in it, before either number appears. If the interval comes out with the sign you did not expect, check the order before you check anything else.

*"The interval contains 0, so the proportions are equal."* An interval is the set of values the data cannot rule out, and it rules none of them in. Zero is one candidate among all the others it holds, including some at the far end that would matter enormously. Say the data are consistent with no difference, and then say what else they are consistent with.

*Per cent and percentage points.* A gap of {pts(PERRINE_RATE - OTHER_RATE)} points against a base of {fmtPct(OTHER_RATE, 2)} is also a rate {fmt(timesAsOften, 1)} times as high. The two numbers describe one fact and persuade very differently. Report the points, the two rates they came from, and the count of units the points translate into.

*Interpreting the interval as a statement about the samples.* The sentence is about the true difference between two populations. {fmtPct(PERRINE_RATE, 2)} and {fmtPct(OTHER_RATE, 2)} are already known exactly; nobody needs an interval for those. Keep the word **true** in the sentence and the parameter stays in view.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
