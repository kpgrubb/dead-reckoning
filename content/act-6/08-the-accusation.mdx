<Briefing title="The two-proportion z-test, end to end" topics={["6.10", "6.11"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
The interval asked how big the gap is. The test asks a narrower question with a sharper answer: **if the two populations really share one rate, how often would two samples this size come apart by this much?**

Everything in the procedure follows from taking the null hypothesis seriously.

$H_0: p_1 = p_2$ says there is one rate, not two. So the best estimate of that single rate uses every observation in both samples, and the **pooled proportion** is the total count of successes over the total number of units. The standard error of $\hat p_1 - \hat p_2$ is then built at that one rate rather than at two.

This is the whole difference between the test and the interval, and it is worth stating plainly because the two procedures are run on the same four numbers minutes apart. **The test pools because its null says there is one rate to pool.** The interval does not pool because it assumes nothing of the kind: it is estimating how far apart the rates are. Use the interval's unpooled standard error inside the test and the statistic is computed under an assumption the null hypothesis does not make, which is a procedural error even when the answer comes out on the same side of α.

The conditions move with the standard error. Large Counts for the **test** is checked at the pooled proportion, which gives four *expected* counts rather than four observed ones, and they are generally not the counts the file shows. Where it bites is a rare event in two samples of very different sizes: pooling pulls the smaller sample's expected count towards the larger sample's rate, and a success count that cleared 10 comfortably in the file can arrive at the condition with very little room. The expected count is the one that decides it.

After that the arithmetic is the same shape as every test in the unit. Standardize, find the tail the alternative names, compare with α, write the sentence.

The sentence has one extra part here that an exam will award and a hearing will attack: **scope**. A test of two proportions is a statement about two populations over one window of data, and nothing in it identifies a cause. Two groups that were *observed* rather than *assigned* can differ for any reason that also sorted the units into groups. Say what the finding covers, say what it does not, and say both before somebody else says them for you.
</Briefing.Intuition>

**Conditions for the test**, each stated with its number:

1. **Random.** Two independent random samples, or two groups created by random assignment. For a process rather than a frame, say what makes the process behave like a random one.
2. **10%.** $n_1 \le 0.1N_1$ and $n_2 \le 0.1N_2$, checked separately.
3. **Large Counts, at the pooled proportion.** All four of $n_1\hat p_c$, $n_1(1 - \hat p_c)$, $n_2\hat p_c$, $n_2(1 - \hat p_c)$ at least 10. Report the smallest.

Then $P$ from the standard normal in the tail $H_a$ names, the comparison with α, and a conclusion that carries the decision, the evidence framing, the direction, the context and the scope.
</Briefing.Definition>

Two things worth noticing before this is left alone. And had the unpooled standard error been used here instead, the statistic would have moved, because the two estimates are different quantities and only one of them is the test's.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*The unpooled standard error inside the test.* The most common procedural error in the topic, and the easiest to make on an exam that asks for an interval and a test on the same data. Pool for the test, do not pool for the interval. If you can say why in one sentence, you will not mix them up: the test is computed inside the world where $H_0$ is true, and in that world there is one rate.

*Large Counts checked on the observed counts.* For an interval that is correct. For a test it is the wrong four numbers. Multiply each sample size by $\hat p_c$ and by $1 - \hat p_c$ and report the smallest of the four, because the smallest is what a reader checks.

*A conclusion phrased about the samples.* "The {fmtPct(PERRINE_RATE, 2)} group was lost more often than the {fmtPct(OTHER_RATE, 2)} group" is a description of the data, which nobody needed a test to obtain. The conclusion is about the true rates in the two populations, and the word *evidence* keeps it honest.

*Scope left off.* Two failures hide here. The first is a conclusion with no window attached, which will be quoted as though it covered every transit in the system. The second is the slide from a difference to a cause: these groups were observed, not assigned, so the test licenses "they differ" and never "this made them differ".
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
