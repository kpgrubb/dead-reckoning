<Briefing title="The margin is the claim" topics={["6.2", "6.3"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
The interval is built the way the last one was: the statistic, plus and minus a critical value times the standard error. What changes here is what gets done with it, and there are three separate jobs.

**Say what it means.** One sentence, four parts, every part load-bearing: the **level**, the word **confident**, the **true** (population) parameter named in context, and **both endpoints**. Drop the word *true* and the sentence becomes a claim about the sample, which is known exactly and needs no interval. Write *probable* in place of *confident* and it becomes a claim about the one interval in hand, which is the error the previous topic disposed of.

**Know what moves it.** The margin is

$$m = z^\star\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n}}$$

and only three things are in it. The **level** enters through $z^\star$ alone: raise it and the interval widens, and nothing else about the estimate changes. The **sample size** enters under a square root, so four times the data halves the margin and a hundred times the data divides it by ten. The **sample proportion** enters through $\hat p(1 - \hat p)$, which is largest at one-half and shrinks toward zero as the proportion approaches either end, so rare events have small standard errors in absolute terms and appalling ones relative to the rate itself. The size of the population appears nowhere in the formula. It appears once, as a condition, and then never again.

**Buy a margin.** Since $n$ is the only lever that improves precision without weakening the claim, it is worth being able to price. Set the margin required, solve for $n$, and round **up**, because a sample size counts units and a fraction of one buys nothing:

$$n = p^\star(1 - p^\star)\left(\frac{z^\star}{m}\right)^2$$

with $p^\star$ a pilot estimate, or $0.5$ when there is nothing to go on, that being the value which makes $p^\star(1 - p^\star)$ as large as it can be and the sample size therefore as large as it will ever need to be. The margin sits in the denominator and is squared, which is the economics of the whole thing: **halving the margin costs four times the data.**

**Judge a claim with it.** An interval is a set of plausible values, so it answers a question of the form *is the parameter at least $k$?* by geometry.

- If the **whole interval lies above** $k$, every plausible value exceeds $k$ and the claim is supported at that level.
- If $k$ lies **inside** the interval, $k$ has not been ruled out. Failing to exclude a value is a statement about what the data could not exclude, and the honest report says it in those words rather than treating $k$ as established.
- The point estimate has no vote. $\hat p$ beats all sorts of values the interval cannot carry an argument against, and the endpoint is what carries the argument.
</Briefing.Intuition>

so the corridor's rate lies in **({fmt(boardCi[0], 5)}, {fmt(boardCi[1], 5)})** with {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} confidence, or {fmt(100 * boardCi[0], 2)} to {fmt(100 * boardCi[1], 2)} losses per hundred transits.

The table prints {fmt(marsBeltRate, 5)} as though it were a fact about the corridor. It is a sample statistic carrying a margin of {fmt(board.marginOfError ?? 0, 5)}, which is {fmt(100 * (board.marginOfError ?? 0), 2)} of a percentage point either side. Any argument that turns on tenths of a point between two corridors is an argument this table cannot settle.

<Formula tex={sizeTex} label="Mars–Belt corridor · transits for a 0.3-point margin" />

Rounded up: **{fmtInt(boardNeeded)} transits**, more than half again what the corridor has flown in the whole file. Precision about a rare event is bought with years and with nothing else.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"A bigger sample makes us more confident."* The level is chosen by the author before the data exists, and $n$ appears nowhere near it. A larger sample buys a **narrower interval at the same level**, which is more useful than more confidence and a different thing.

*"The interval contains 95% of the sample proportions."* It is a range of plausible values for one fixed parameter, built around the single $\hat p$ that happened to be drawn. Future sample proportions cluster around the parameter rather than around that estimate, so an interval centred on an unlucky $\hat p$ would contain rather fewer of them than the label suggests.

*"The interval proves the rate is above $k$."* An interval lying entirely above $k$ says that at this level every plausible value exceeds $k$, which is a strong signable statement and not a proof. The reverse is weaker still: a value **inside** the interval has only failed to be excluded. Reporting "the data are consistent with $k$" as though it read "the rate is $k$" is the commonest way an honest interval becomes a dishonest paragraph.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
