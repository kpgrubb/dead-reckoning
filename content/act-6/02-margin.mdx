<Briefing title="The margin is the claim" topics={["6.2", "6.3"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
The interval is built the way the last module built it: the statistic, plus and minus a critical value times the standard error. What changes today is what you do with it, and there are three separate jobs.

**Say what it means.** One sentence, four parts, and every part load-bearing: the **level**, the word **confident**, the **true** (population) parameter named in context, and **both endpoints**. Drop the word *true* and the sentence becomes a claim about the sample, which is known exactly and needs no interval. Say *probable* instead of *confident* and it becomes a claim about the one interval you have, which the last module disposed of.

**Know what moves it.** The margin is

$$m = z^\star\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n}}$$

and only three things are in it. The **level** enters through $z^\star$ alone: raise it and the interval widens, and nothing else about the estimate changes. The **sample size** enters under a square root: four times the data halves the margin, a hundred times the data divides it by ten. The **sample proportion** enters through $\hat p(1 - \hat p)$, which is largest at one-half and shrinks toward zero as the proportion approaches either end — so rare events have small standard errors in absolute terms and appalling ones relative to the rate itself. Notice what is *not* in the formula: the size of the population. It appears once, as a condition, and then never again.

**Buy a margin.** Because $n$ is the only lever that improves precision without weakening the claim, it is worth being able to price it. Set the margin you need, solve for $n$, and round **up**, because a sample size is a count of units and a fraction of one buys nothing:

$$n = p^\star(1 - p^\star)\left(\frac{z^\star}{m}\right)^2$$

with $p^\star$ a pilot estimate, or $0.5$ when there is nothing to go on — the value that makes $p^\star(1 - p^\star)$ as large as it can be, and therefore the sample size as large as it will ever need to be. The margin is in the denominator and squared, which is the whole economics of the thing: **halving the margin costs four times the data.**

**Judge a claim with it.** An interval is a set of plausible values, so it answers a question of the form *is the parameter at least $k$?* by geometry.

- If the **whole interval lies above** $k$, every plausible value exceeds $k$ and the claim is supported at that level.
- If $k$ lies **inside** the interval, $k$ has not been ruled out. That is not the same as showing $k$ is right; it is a statement about what the data could not exclude, and the honest report says so in those words.
- The point estimate has no vote. $\hat p$ beats all sorts of values it cannot carry an argument against; the endpoint is what carries the argument.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
Not our corridor. The Board's — the Mars–Belt comparison the rebuttal table leans on: {fmtInt(marsBelt.losses)} losses in {fmtInt(marsBelt.transits)} transits, a rate of {fmt(marsBeltRate, 5)}.

**Conditions.** Same three, same process argument: the corridor is an ongoing operation that will produce far more than ten times these {fmtInt(marsBelt.transits)} transits, and the Authority states the record complete. Counts: {fmtInt(marsBelt.losses)} losses and {fmtInt(boardArrivals)} arrivals, both well past ten.

**The interval.** The standard error is {fmt(board.se, 5)} and the {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} critical value is {fmt(zStar(0.95), 4)}.

<Formula tex={boardTex} label="Mars–Belt corridor · statistic ± margin" />

so the corridor's rate lies in **({fmt(boardCi[0], 5)}, {fmt(boardCi[1], 5)})** with {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} confidence — {fmt(100 * boardCi[0], 2)} to {fmt(100 * boardCi[1], 2)} losses per hundred transits.

Read what that does to the Board's own sentence. The rebuttal table prints {fmt(marsBeltRate, 5)} as though it were a fact about the corridor. It is a sample statistic with a margin of {fmt(board.marginOfError ?? 0, 5)} on it, which is {fmt(100 * (board.marginOfError ?? 0), 2)} of a percentage point either side. Any argument that turns on tenths of a point between two corridors is an argument this table cannot settle, and it is the Board's table.

**What a tighter margin would cost them.** Suppose the Directorate wanted the Mars–Belt figure to {fmt(100 * boardTarget, 1)} of a point either side, at the same level:

<Formula tex={sizeTex} label="Mars–Belt corridor · transits for a 0.3-point margin" />

Rounded up: **{fmtInt(boardNeeded)} transits** — more than half again what the corridor has flown in the whole file. Precision about a rare event is not bought with better analysis. It is bought with years.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"A bigger sample makes us more confident."* It does not. The level is chosen by the author before the data exists, and $n$ appears nowhere near it. A larger sample buys a **narrower interval at the same level** — which is more useful than more confidence, and a different thing.

*"The interval contains 95% of the sample proportions."* No: it is a range of plausible values for one fixed parameter, built around the single $\hat p$ you happened to draw. Future sample proportions cluster around the parameter, not around your estimate, so an interval centred on an unlucky $\hat p$ would contain rather fewer of them than the label suggests.

*"The interval proves the rate is above $k$."* Nothing proves. An interval lying entirely above $k$ says that at this level every plausible value exceeds $k$, which is a strong, signable statement and not a proof. And the reverse is weaker still: a value **inside** the interval has not been shown to be right, only not excluded. Reporting "the data are consistent with $k$" as though it were "the rate is $k$" is the commonest way an honest interval is turned into a dishonest paragraph.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
