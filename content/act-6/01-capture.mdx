<Briefing title="An interval is a statistic with its error bar; the level belongs to the method" topics={["6.1", "6.2"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A point estimate is a number that refuses to admit it came from a sample. {fmt(LANE_RATE, 4)} is the Lane's rate *in this file*; run the Lane again for the same six years and the file would say something else, and the whole of Act V was about how much else.

An interval is the estimate with that variability attached, and it is built out of one fact you already have. Over repeated samples of size $n$, the sample proportion $\hat p$ is centred on the true $p$ with standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}$, and when the counts are large that distribution is close to Normal. So for the central $C$ of samples,

$$\left|\hat p - p\right| \;<\; z^\star\sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}}$$

and now read that inequality from the other end. If $\hat p$ lands within a margin of $p$, then $p$ lies within the same margin of $\hat p$ — the distance between two numbers does not care which one you stand on. That single reversal is the entire logic of a confidence interval. The margin is borrowed from the sampling distribution; the interval is the statistic with the margin hung on either side; and the fraction of samples for which the inequality held becomes the fraction of intervals that capture.

**statistic ± margin of error**, where the margin is a critical value times a standard error. Nothing else is going on.

Three consequences worth having in your hands before the panel:

- The level is a property of the **procedure**, measured over repetitions you did not perform. It is not a property of the interval on your screen, which is a finished object with no randomness left in it. The parameter does not move; the interval does, from sample to sample.
- Because $p$ is unknown, the standard error is computed from $\hat p$ instead — which is why an interval's conditions are checked with the observed counts of successes and failures rather than with a hypothesised value.
- Raising $C$ raises $z^\star$ and nothing else, so the interval gets **wider**. Wider is not better and it is not more precise; it is a wider claim, held more often. Precision is bought only with $n$, and only at the rate $1/\sqrt{n}$.

The conditions are three, and each guards a different part of the machinery.

1. **Random.** The data must come from a random sample, or — as here — from a process the analyst is prepared to state produced these units independently of what happened to them. A census of a process is still a sample *of the process*, and the condition is the sentence you are willing to sign about it.
2. **10%, or independence.** Sampling without replacement from a fixed pool makes later draws depend on earlier ones. Keep $n$ to a tenth of the pool and the dependence is negligible. When the units come from an ongoing operation rather than a fixed pool, the condition reads: the process could produce far more than ten times these units.
3. **Large Counts.** At least 10 successes and at least 10 failures, counted from the sample itself. This is what licenses the Normal model for $\hat p$, and it is the condition the panel will let you break on purpose.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
Your own file, and not this one. The *Asgard* report of 2176: {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.losses)} losses in {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} Lane transits, 2168 to 2176, written by a lieutenant commander who is now sitting in this compartment.

**Conditions.** The transits are the Lane's own traffic over eight years, and the report states in its second paragraph that the record is complete and that nothing about how a transit came to be flown was related to whether it was lost — that is the random condition, stated rather than assumed. The Lane goes on running, so the process behind it could produce far more than ten times these transits: {fmtInt(10 * ROOK_2176.transits)} of them, and it will. Counts: {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.losses)} losses and {fmtInt(rookArrivals)} arrivals, both at least 10 — the first of them narrowly, which the report should have said and did not.

**The interval.** The sample proportion is {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.losses)} over {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)}, or {fmt(rookRate, 5)}. Its standard error, $\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n}$, comes to {fmt(rook.se, 5)}, and at {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} the critical value is {fmt(zStar(0.95), 4)}.

<Formula tex={rookTex} label="Asgard 2176 · statistic ± margin" />

which gives an interval of **({fmt(rookCi[0], 5)}, {fmt(rookCi[1], 5)})** — about {fmt(100 * rookCi[0], 2)} to {fmt(100 * rookCi[1], 2)} losses per hundred transits.

Notice what the margin costs. {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} transits, eight years of a corridor's whole life, and the answer is still a range more than {fmt(rookCi[1] / rookCi[0], 1)} times as wide at the top as at the bottom. Rare events are expensive to measure. That is not a failure of the method; it is the method telling you the truth about what eight years bought.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"There is a 95% probability that the true rate is in this interval."* The sentence the Board's counsel is waiting for. The true rate is a fixed number and the interval is already computed; one of them is inside the other, and the probability of that is 1 or 0. The {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} describes the method — the fraction of intervals it produces, over repetitions, that capture the parameter. Say **confident**, and say what you are confident *about*.

*"95% of the transits fall in the interval."* The interval is a range of plausible values for one proportion, not a range of values for individual units. A transit is lost or it is not; it has no rate of its own to fall inside anything. The same error wearing a better suit is *"95% of future sample proportions would land in this interval"* — also false, because this interval is centred on the $\hat p$ you happened to draw, and future $\hat p$'s cluster around the parameter rather than around your draw.

*"A wider interval is more precise."* Backwards. Width is imprecision. A {fmtPct(0.99, 0)} interval is wider than a {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} one because it is a weaker claim about where the parameter is, and it captures more often for exactly that reason. The only thing that buys confidence and precision at the same time is more data.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
