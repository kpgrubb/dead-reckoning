<Briefing title="An interval is a statistic with its error bar; the level belongs to the method" topics={["6.1", "6.2"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
A point estimate is a single number with nothing attached to say how far it might be from the truth. Take another sample the same way and it moves.

A confidence interval attaches that variability, and it is built out of one fact about sampling distributions. Over repeated samples of size $n$, the sample proportion $\hat p$ is centred on the true $p$ with standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}$, and when the counts are large the distribution of $\hat p$ is close to Normal. So for the central $C$ of samples,

$$\left|\hat p - p\right| \;<\; z^\star\sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}}$$

Now read that inequality from the other end. If $\hat p$ lands within a margin of $p$, then $p$ lies within the same margin of $\hat p$, because the distance between two numbers does not care which one you stand on. That single reversal is the whole logic of a confidence interval. The margin is borrowed from the sampling distribution, the interval is the statistic with the margin hung on either side, and the fraction of samples for which the inequality held becomes the fraction of intervals that capture.

**statistic ± margin of error**, where the margin is a critical value times a standard error.

Three consequences follow.

- The level is a property of the **procedure**, measured over repetitions that were never performed. The interval on the screen is a finished object with no randomness left in it: the parameter sits inside it or outside it. What varies from sample to sample is the interval, not the parameter.
- Because $p$ is unknown, the standard error is computed from $\hat p$ instead. That is why an interval's conditions are checked with the observed counts of successes and failures rather than with a hypothesised value.
- Raising $C$ raises $z^\star$ and changes nothing else, so the interval gets **wider**. A wider interval is a weaker claim about where the parameter is, held more often. Precision is bought only with $n$, and only at the rate $1/\sqrt{n}$.

Three conditions guard three different parts of the machinery.

1. **Random.** The data come from a random sample, from a randomized experiment, or from a process the analyst is prepared to state produced these units independently of what happened to them. A census of a process is still a sample *of the process*, and the condition is then a sentence somebody has to be willing to sign.
2. **10%, or independence.** Sampling without replacement from a fixed pool makes later draws depend on earlier ones. Keep $n$ to a tenth of the pool and the dependence is negligible. For an ongoing operation rather than a fixed pool, the condition reads: the process could produce far more than ten times these units.
3. **Large Counts.** At least 10 successes and at least 10 failures, counted from the sample itself. This is what licenses the Normal model for $\hat p$.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
The Asgard report of 2176 covers eight years of Lane traffic: {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.losses)} losses in {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} transits, 2168 to 2176.

**Conditions.** The report states in its second paragraph that the record is complete and that nothing about how a transit came to be flown was related to whether it was lost, which is the random condition stated rather than assumed. The corridor goes on running, so the process behind it could produce far more than {fmtInt(10 * ROOK_2176.transits)} transits. Counts: {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.losses)} losses and {fmtInt(rookArrivals)} arrivals, both at least 10, the first of them narrowly.

**The interval.** The sample proportion is {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.losses)} over {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)}, or {fmt(rookRate, 5)}. Its standard error, $\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n}$, comes to {fmt(rook.se, 5)}, and at {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} the critical value is {fmt(zStar(0.95), 4)}.

<Formula tex={rookTex} label="Asgard 2176 · statistic ± margin" />

which gives **({fmt(rookCi[0], 5)}, {fmt(rookCi[1], 5)})**, or about {fmt(100 * rookCi[0], 2)} to {fmt(100 * rookCi[1], 2)} losses per hundred transits.

Notice the price of the margin. {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} transits, eight years of a corridor's whole life, and the answer is still a range more than {fmt(rookCi[1] / rookCi[0], 1)} times as wide at the top as at the bottom. Rare events are expensive to measure, and the width of the interval is the honest report of that.

**And the level's price.** Ask for {fmtPct(0.99, 0)} on the same file and $z^\star$ rises to {fmt(zStar(0.99), 4)}, which widens the interval to ({fmt(rook99Ci[0], 5)}, {fmt(rook99Ci[1], 5)}). Nothing about the corridor changed. The claim got weaker and therefore easier to keep.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"There is a 95% probability that the true rate is in this interval."* The true rate is a fixed number and the interval is already computed; one of them is inside the other, and the probability of that is 1 or 0. The {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} describes the method: the fraction of intervals it produces, over repetitions, that capture the parameter. Say **confident**, and say what you are confident about.

*"95% of the transits fall in the interval."* The interval is a range of plausible values for one proportion, not a range of values for individual units. A transit is lost or it is not; it has no rate of its own to fall inside anything. The same error in better clothes is *"95% of future sample proportions would land in this interval"*, which is also false: this interval is centred on the $\hat p$ that was drawn, and future $\hat p$'s cluster around the parameter instead.

*"A wider interval is more precise."* Width is imprecision. A {fmtPct(0.99, 0)} interval is wider than a {fmtPct(0.95, 0)} one because it is a weaker claim about where the parameter is, and it captures more often for that reason. Only more data buys confidence and precision together.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
