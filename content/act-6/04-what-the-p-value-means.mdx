<Briefing title="A p-value is a probability about data, computed inside a world where H₀ is true" topics={["6.5"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
Build the world $H_0$ describes. Run it. Count how often it does something at least as surprising as what you actually saw. That count, as a proportion, is the p-value, and every word of the definition is load-bearing.

**Assuming $H_0$ is true.** The sampling distribution you measure the statistic against is the one $H_0$ predicts — its centre, its spread, its shape. $H_0$ is an *input*. Nothing that comes out the other end can be a verdict on it, any more than the answer to "how far is Ceres if the ephemeris is right?" is a verdict on the ephemeris.

**At least as extreme.** Not "this result". The probability of any exact sample proportion is essentially zero, so a p-value that meant "the probability of exactly what we saw" would be zero for every data set ever collected and would tell you nothing. What the p-value collects is a whole **tail**: every outcome the alternative counts as at least as surprising as the one observed. Which outcomes those are is decided by $H_a$ — before the data — and by nothing else.

**A probability, which is to say a long-run rate.** Read it as a frequency over repetitions that never happened: *if the Lane really ran at one rate, a season like this one would come back at least this lopsided in about p of all seasons.* Say it that way and the misreadings become impossible to write down.

**Two roads to the same number.** You can compute the tail area under the normal model the conditions licensed, or you can build the null by brute force — simulate ten thousand seasons in which $H_0$ is exactly true, standardize each one, and count the runs at least as extreme. The second is not an approximation of the first. Both are approximations of the same thing, which is the true sampling distribution of the statistic, and the normal curve is a *description* of the distribution the simulation is drawing from.

They differ in where they fail. The normal model is smooth all the way into the far tail and is worst exactly there, where the counts are thin and the curve's symmetry is a fiction. The simulation is honest everywhere and has a floor: **ten thousand runs cannot resolve a probability finer than one in ten thousand.** If the true p is smaller than that, the simulation will report zero, or one, or two, and the difference between those is noise.

**What it is not.** Four sentences, and each of them will be read back to you:

1. *the probability that $H_0$ is true* — the p-value conditions **on** $H_0$;
2. *the probability the result is due to chance* — under $H_0$, chance is the only mechanism, with certainty;
3. *1 − p, the probability that $H_a$ is true* — both numbers are computed entirely inside $H_0$;
4. *a one-tailed area, reported for a two-sided alternative* — which halves the number and doubles the real false-alarm rate.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
**The 2176 report. Yours.**

Thirteen losses in {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} transits, eight years of Lane traffic ending in 2176, signed at the bottom by a lieutenant who had never had a finding argued with. The Directorate's standing figure for the Lane was {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)} — five losses per thousand transits — and the question the Directorate asked was whether the Lane was running worse than its posted figure.

**Hypotheses.** $H_0$ — the Lane's true loss rate is exactly {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)}. $H_a$ — it is greater than {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)}. One-sided, because a Lane running *better* than its posted figure required no action from anybody.

**Conditions at the null.** $n p_0$ = {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} × {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)} = {fmt(rookCount, 1)}, and $n(1 - p_0)$ = {fmt(rookFailures, 1)}. Both clear 10, the first of them by {fmt(rookCount - 10, 1)}.

**The statistic.** {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.losses)} in {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} is a sample rate of {fmt(rookRate, 5)} — {fmtPct(rookRate, 3)} against a posted {fmtPct(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 1)}.

<Formula tex={rookTex} terms={{ '\\hat p': 'the rate the eight years actually logged', 'p_0': 'the Directorate’s standing figure', '\\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}': 'the standard error under H₀ — computed at p₀, because the test lives inside H₀' }} label="Eight years of Lane traffic, standardized" />

**The p-value.** One tail, upper, because that is the side the alternative named.

<Formula tex={rookTailTex} terms={{ 'P(Z \\ge z)': 'the share of eight-year records a Lane running at exactly p₀ would send back at least this far above it' }} label="The area beyond the statistic" />

And here is the sentence that went in the report, and it was the right one: *if the Lane's true loss rate were exactly {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)}, then {fmtPct(rook.pValue, 1)} of all eight-year records of {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} transits would show a loss rate at least as far above {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)} as this one does.* Nearly half of them. The record was as ordinary as a record can be.

Note what the sentence does **not** say, and what the lieutenant who wrote it did not say either: that the Lane's rate *was* {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)}. A p-value of {fmtP(rook.pValue)} is the absence of a finding. It is entirely consistent with a true rate of {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)}, and just as consistent with {fmt(rookRate, 4)}, and — this is the part that costs something — with a rate twice the posted figure on one class of hull and the posted figure on all the others, averaged out across {fmtInt(ROOK_2176.transits)} transits that nobody had thought to split by owner.

The arithmetic in that report is correct. It is still correct now. What it could not do, because nobody asked it to, was look at a column that was not in the file.

**The same case, two ways.** The archive console below runs that report both ways: the normal tail, and ten thousand simulated eight-year records built at exactly {fmt(ROOK_NULL_RATE, 3)}. Toggle between them. The two agree to within what ten thousand runs can resolve — as they must, because the curve is a description of the thing the simulation is sampling from — and the sides toggle shows what the reviewer's objection would have cost: read two-sided, the same statistic returns {fmtP(rookTwoSided)}.
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"There is a {fmtP(rook.pValue)} probability that $H_0$ is true."* The commonest error in the unit and the one a hostile reader reaches for first. $H_0$ was assumed in order to compute the number; the number cannot then be a probability for it. $H_0$ is either true of the Lane or it is not, and no sample turns a fact into a probability.

*"There is a {fmtP(rook.pValue)} probability the result is due to chance."* Under $H_0$ the result is due to chance with probability one — chance is the only mechanism the model contains. What the p-value reports is how often chance alone would produce a departure at least this large. The difference sounds like pedantry until somebody reads the sentence back to you and asks which of the two you meant.

*"So there is a {fmt(1 - rook.pValue, 4)} probability that $H_a$ is true."* Both {fmtP(rook.pValue)} and {fmt(1 - rook.pValue, 4)} are computed inside the world where $H_0$ holds. The complement of a conditional probability is conditional on the same thing. Neither is a probability for a hypothesis, and nothing in this procedure ever produces one.

*"The probability of getting exactly this result is {fmtP(rook.pValue)}."* A p-value is an area, never a point. The probability of any exact sample proportion is very close to zero; the tail is the whole point, and "at least as extreme" is the phrase that says so.

*A one-tailed area quoted under a two-sided alternative.* Not a misreading of what p means — a misreading of which p was asked for. The alternative was fixed before the data, and it decides how many tails get counted. Choose the side after seeing the sample and you are running a two-sided procedure while printing a one-sided number; the false-alarm rate on the page is half the real one.

*A simulated p-value of zero.* It does not mean the probability is zero. It means the simulation ran out of resolution: ten thousand runs cannot distinguish 1 in 50,000 from 1 in 500,000, and both come back as "none of ours". Report the floor — *fewer than 1 in 10,000* — and, where the conditions allow, take the number from the model.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>

<LogEntry
  title="The p-value: a probability about data, computed under H₀"
  concept="A p-value is the probability — computed by assuming H₀ is true — of obtaining a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, where 'at least as extreme' is decided by the alternative and fixed before the data. It is therefore a statement about what data the null world produces, never a probability that H₀ is true, never the probability that a result is 'due to chance' (under H₀ chance is the only mechanism, with certainty), and never 1 − p as the probability of Hₐ. The same number can be obtained two ways: as a tail area under the model the conditions licensed, or by building the null explicitly, simulating many runs of it and counting the runs at least as extreme. The two agree to within the simulation's resolution, which is its floor: N runs cannot report a probability smaller than 1/N, so a simulated p of zero means 'finer than this simulation can see', not 'impossible'."
