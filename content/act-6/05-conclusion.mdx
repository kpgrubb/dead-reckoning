Ebele brings the draft at the end of the second dog watch, on paper, which he has never done before. Eleven lines. The arithmetic occupies two of them and is correct. The conclusion occupies one.

> *The test proves that hulls insured to {PERRINE_HOLDINGS} are being targeted on the Hundred-Day Lane.*

You read it twice. Then you find the stylus, take out the word *proves*, take out the word *targeted*, and hand it back with two gaps in it.

He does not argue, which is new, but he does not understand either, and that is worse.

<Dialogue speaker="analyst" aside="He is holding the page at the angle you hold a page when you want the other person to read it again and agree with you.">
Sir — {fmtInt(inTenThousand)} in ten thousand. If that is not enough to say something, then nothing in the whole of statistics is ever enough to say anything, and I do not see what we are for.

Two words, two different errors, and he has made both at once. Take them one at a time.

*Proves.* A significance test is a machine with a designed failure rate: the level you set in advance is a promise about how often it will shout when nothing is happening — one time in twenty, one in a hundred, whatever you signed for. Nobody builds a machine like that to get certainty out of it. A procedure that is wrong on purpose at a stated rate cannot prove. It can provide convincing evidence — a word about the strength of an argument, not the truth of a fact, which is why a hearing cannot take it away from you.

*Targeted.* Worse, and quieter. Go back through the arithmetic and find the step where a hull was aimed at. There is none. There are two counts, two denominators, a pooled rate, a standard error and a tail area, and a difference in proportions has no room in it for an actor or a motive. It is equally consistent with a harder route, an insurer that writes policies on older hulls, a berth master with a habit, or nineteen genuinely bad nights. Every one of those produces the same number.

<Dialogue speaker="xo" aside="She has been at the chart table with the file open and has not looked up once. She does not need to look up for this.">
And the one you believe is not admissible either. We all believe the same thing on this deck tonight. What you can write down is what the procedure paid for, and the procedure paid for a rate.
</Dialogue>

He rewrites the line twice, neither version is a sentence yet, and then he stops and asks the question that shows he has understood the problem rather than the correction.

<Dialogue speaker="analyst" aside="He has the two-day plan open in the other pane — the interval Ferrier has already told him to build.">
So — then what does the sentence say instead of *proves*? Because if I write *convincing evidence*, a reviewer asks convincing at what level, and we have not fixed a level. And — sir, in two days you have me building a confidence interval for the difference. If the test says one thing and the interval says another, which of them do I put in the brief?
</Dialogue>

Both halves are the right question, and they have the same answer.

On the level: tonight you use the conventional {fmt(ALPHA, 2)} and you say so in the sentence. Tomorrow it gets chosen properly, with the two ways of being wrong written out and priced, because on this file they are not priced the same. That is a separate day's work and it is the next one.

On the interval: it must agree, and he needs to be able to say why in one sentence. The interval and the test are the same arithmetic asked in two directions. The test fixes a null value and asks how far out the data landed; the interval fixes the data and asks which null values would have survived. Ask both at matched levels and they cannot contradict each other.

<Dialogue speaker="xo" aside="Now she looks up, and what she says is the part Ebele has not thought about, which is the part that gets written into the brief.">
Matched. Not *the same*. Write both caveats down now, while you are calm, because you will be asked them in front of people. Our test is one-sided — its partner is the *wider* interval, not the one you would print by habit. And the test pools the two samples, because the null says they share a rate, and the interval does not. The two standard errors are different quantities. They will agree; they are not the same algebra, and if you claim they are, that is the sentence they take apart instead of the conclusion.
</Dialogue>

</Scene>

<Briefing title="The decision is arithmetic. The conclusion is a sentence about ships." topics={["6.6"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
**The decision has one moving part.** You fixed α before the data: the rate at which you are willing to shout when nothing is happening. The data produced a p-value. Compare them.

Reject $H_0$ when $P < \alpha$. Otherwise fail to reject. That is the whole rule, and nothing else in the file changes it — not the size of the sample, not how much you would like the answer, and not how close the two numbers turn out to be.

**The two words that are never written.** *Accept $H_0$* and *proves*.

Failing to reject is the absence of a finding, not a finding of absence. A test that cannot see a difference this size would fail to reject whether or not the difference is there, so "we fail to reject" is a statement about what this procedure could detect, not about what is true. Write *there is not convincing evidence that…* and you have said exactly what happened.

And a test never proves, in either direction, because a test is built with a known failure rate. At α = 0.05 one true null in twenty is rejected by construction. That is not a flaw; it is the specification. A procedure with a specification like that produces **convincing evidence**, which is a claim about an argument and survives being quoted, rather than proof, which is a claim about the world and does not.

**Five parts, and a hostile reader checks for all five.**

1. the decision — reject or fail to reject $H_0$;
2. the comparison that licensed it — $P$ against $\alpha$, both numbers on the page;
3. the framing — *convincing evidence*, or *not convincing evidence*;
4. the direction — what $H_a$ actually claimed;
5. the context — which population, which variable.

Drop the fifth and you have written arithmetic with a full stop after it. Drop the second and you have written an opinion. The conclusion is the only part of the analysis that travels: the file stays in the office and the sentence goes into somebody else's report, without the working and without you there to add the clause you meant.

**Why the p-value is reported and not just the verdict.** The decision changes at exactly $\alpha = P$. Above that value the test rejects, at it or below it the test does not, and a reader who works to a different standard than yours is entitled to redo the decision without redoing the work. "Significant at the 0.05 level" says only which side of one line a number fell on; the number itself says how far.

**Interval and test: the same question, asked twice.** A $(1 - \alpha)$ confidence interval is the set of null values a **two-sided** test at $\alpha$ would fail to reject. So if the interval excludes the null value, the two-sided test at $\alpha$ rejects it, and if it contains it, the test does not. Same estimate, same standard error, same $z^\star$; the interval simply runs the comparison for every null value at once, and tells you the size of the effect while it is at it.

Two caveats, and they are the hardest idea in the module:

- **One-sided tests do not pair with $(1 - \alpha)$ intervals.** A one-sided test at $\alpha$ puts all of its risk in one tail; its two-sided partner is the $(1 - 2\alpha)$ interval. A one-sided test at 0.05 matches a 90 % interval, not a 95 % one.
- **For two proportions the standard errors are different quantities.** The test pools, because $H_0$ says both groups share a rate and every observation is evidence about it. The interval does not pool, because it is not assuming they are equal — it is estimating how far apart they are. The correspondence is therefore a near-identity rather than an algebraic one, and a result sitting on the line can come out on either side of it. When the two disagree, each is right about its own question.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Caution>
*"We fail to reject, so $H_0$ is true."* The single commonest error in the topic. Failing to reject means the data are consistent with $H_0$ — and with a great many other values as well, which is exactly what the confidence interval shows you. Absence of evidence is evidence of absence only when the procedure had a real chance of finding the effect, and whether it did is the question 6-06 asks.

*"We reject, so $H_a$ is proven."* At α = 0.05 the test rejects one true null in twenty, by design. The finding is convincing evidence and nothing stronger. "Proves" is the first word a hearing strikes, and striking it removes nothing the procedure actually bought.

*"We accept $H_0$."* Never written, in either direction. There is no procedure here that accepts a hypothesis; there is one that finds the data too unlikely under $H_0$ to keep working with it, and one that does not.

*A conclusion with no context.* "P = 0.0043 < α = 0.05, so we reject H₀ and the result is statistically significant" is arithmetically perfect and useless. Significant about what, for whom, in which direction? Name the variable and name the population, in the same sentence as the decision.

*A conclusion with no p and no α.* The mirror error. "There is convincing evidence that the rate is higher" states the finding and hides the only thing that licensed it. Both numbers go in, so that a reader working to a different standard can use your file instead of taking your verdict.

*Matching a one-sided test at α to a $(1 - \alpha)$ interval.* The most common duality mistake, and it is a factor of two in the tail, not a rounding difference. One-sided at α pairs with $(1 - 2\alpha)$.

*Treating the interval and the test as the same arithmetic for two proportions.* The test's standard error is pooled and the interval's is not. They agree on every case that is not on the line, and "they always agree exactly" is a claim you cannot defend when somebody produces the case where they do not.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
