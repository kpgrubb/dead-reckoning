<Briefing title="The decision is arithmetic. The conclusion is a sentence about ships." topics={["6.6"]}>
<Briefing.Intuition>
**The decision has one moving part.** $\alpha$ was fixed before the data: the rate at which the analyst is willing to shout when nothing is happening. The data produced a p-value. Compare them.

Reject $H_0$ when $P < \alpha$. Otherwise fail to reject. That is the whole rule, and nothing else in the file changes it: not the size of the sample, not how much anybody would like the answer, not how close the two numbers turn out to be.

**The two words that are never written.** *Accept $H_0$* and *proves*.

Failing to reject is the absence of a finding rather than a finding of absence. A test that could not see a difference this size would fail to reject whether or not the difference is there, so "we fail to reject" describes what this procedure could detect. Write *there is not convincing evidence that…* and the sentence says exactly what happened.

A test never proves, in either direction, because a test is built with a known failure rate. At α = 0.05 one true null in twenty is rejected by construction. That is the specification rather than a flaw, and a procedure with a specification like that produces **convincing evidence**, which is a claim about an argument and survives being quoted.

**Five parts, and a hostile reader checks for all five.**

1. the decision, reject or fail to reject $H_0$;
2. the comparison that licensed it, $P$ against $\alpha$, both numbers on the page;
3. the framing, *convincing evidence* or *not convincing evidence*;
4. the direction, what $H_a$ actually claimed;
5. the context, which population and which variable.

Drop the fifth and the conclusion is arithmetic with a full stop after it. Drop the second and it is an opinion. The conclusion is the only part of an analysis that travels: the file stays in the office and the sentence goes into somebody else's report, without the working and without its author there to add the clause they meant.

**Why the p-value is reported and not just the verdict.** The decision changes at exactly $\alpha = P$. Above that value the test rejects, at it or below it the test does not, and a reader who works to a different standard is entitled to redo the decision without redoing the work. "Significant at the 0.05 level" says only which side of one line a number fell on. The number itself says how far.

**Interval and test: the same question, asked twice.** A $(1 - \alpha)$ confidence interval is the set of null values a **two-sided** test at $\alpha$ would fail to reject. So if the interval excludes the null value, the two-sided test at $\alpha$ rejects it, and if it contains the null value, the test does not. Same estimate, same standard error, same $z^\star$. The interval runs the comparison for every null value at once, and reports the size of the effect while it is at it.

Two caveats, and they are the hardest idea here:

- **One-sided tests do not pair with $(1 - \alpha)$ intervals.** A one-sided test at $\alpha$ puts all of its risk in one tail, so its two-sided partner is the $(1 - 2\alpha)$ interval. A one-sided test at 0.05 matches a 90% interval.
- **For two proportions the standard errors are different quantities.** The test pools, because $H_0$ says both groups share a rate and every observation is evidence about it. The interval does not pool, because it is estimating how far apart the rates are rather than assuming them equal. The correspondence is therefore a near-identity, and a result sitting on the line can come out on either side of it. Where the two disagree, each is right about its own question.
</Briefing.Intuition>

<Briefing.Worked>
**The Themis Reach review of 2181, carried from its plan to its conclusion.**

The survey office had its hypotheses on the page and dated before it pulled a row: $H_0: p_1 = p_2$, and $H_a: p_1 > p_2$, that {themis.group1} are held at the mark at a higher rate than {themis.group2}. It fixed α at {fmt(ALPHA, 2)}. The conditions passed at the pooled rate. Then it ran the file.

**The counts.** {fmtInt(themis.x1)} of {fmtInt(themis.n1)} charter transits held, which is {fmtPct(themisRate1, 2)}, against {fmtInt(themis.x2)} of {fmtInt(themis.n2)} for everyone else, which is {fmtPct(themisRate2, 2)}. The charter hulls are held more often in this file.

**The conclusion.** *Because the p-value ({fmtP(themisTest.pValue)}) is greater than α = {fmt(ALPHA, 2)}, we fail to reject $H_0$. There is not convincing evidence that the true proportion of {themis.group1} held at the mark on {themis.population} is greater than the proportion for {themis.group2}.*

Read what that sentence does. It reports that {fmtInt(themis.n1)} charter transits are not enough to distinguish a gap of {fmtPct(themisRate1 - themisRate2, 2)} from what {fmtInt(themis.n1)} transits do on their own. It does not establish that the charter hulls are held at the same rate, which the office never showed and could not. A review ten times the size might separate them easily.

**The same question as an interval.** The office also published the 95% interval for the difference, unpooled:

<Formula tex={themisCITex} terms={{ 'z^\\star': 'the 95 % critical value', '\\text{the square root}': 'the UNPOOLED standard error: the interval is not assuming the two rates are equal' }} label="Themis Reach, 95 % interval for p₁ − p₂" />

The interval **contains zero**, so zero is still a plausible value for the difference, and the two-sided test at {fmt(ALPHA, 2)} would keep $H_0$, which is what it did. Note how the duality holds: the two-sided p-value is {fmtP(themisTwoSided)}, comfortably above {fmt(ALPHA, 2)}, and the 95% interval comfortably straddles zero. The matched pair for the office's *one-sided* test at {fmt(ALPHA, 2)} is the 90% interval, ({fmt(themisCI90.ci[0], 5)}, {fmt(themisCI90.ci[1], 5)}), which is narrower and contains zero too.

**And the flip point.** The decision turns over at α = {fmtP(themisFlip)}. It rejects at every level above that and at none at or below it. A reviewer working at α = 0.20 would read this file and reject, which is why the report prints {fmtP(themisTest.pValue)} rather than the words "not significant".
</Briefing.Worked>
<Briefing.Caution>
*"We fail to reject, so $H_0$ is true."* The commonest error in the topic. Failing to reject means the data are consistent with $H_0$, and with a great many other values as well, which is exactly what the confidence interval shows. Absence of evidence is evidence of absence only when the procedure had a real chance of finding the effect, and whether it did is the next topic's question.

*"We reject, so $H_a$ is proven."* At α = 0.05 the test rejects one true null in twenty, by design. The finding is convincing evidence and nothing stronger. "Proves" is the first word a hearing strikes, and striking it removes nothing the procedure bought.

*"We accept $H_0$."* Never written, in either direction. There is no procedure here that accepts a hypothesis. There is one that finds the data too unlikely under $H_0$ to keep working with it, and one that does not.

*A conclusion with no context.* "P = 0.0043 < α = 0.05, so we reject H₀ and the result is statistically significant" is arithmetically perfect and useless. Significant about what, for whom, in which direction? Name the variable and name the population in the same sentence as the decision.

*A conclusion with no p and no α.* The mirror error. "There is convincing evidence that the rate is higher" states the finding and hides the thing that licensed it. Both numbers go in, so that a reader working to a different standard can use the file instead of taking the verdict.

*Matching a one-sided test at α to a $(1 - \alpha)$ interval.* The commonest duality mistake, and it is a factor of two in the tail rather than a rounding difference. One-sided at α pairs with $(1 - 2\alpha)$.

*Treating the interval and the test as the same arithmetic for two proportions.* The test's standard error is pooled and the interval's is not. They agree on every case that is not on the line, and "they always agree exactly" is a claim that cannot be defended when somebody produces the case where they do not.
</Briefing.Caution>
</Briefing>
